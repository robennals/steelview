## 3b. Candidate datasets

### D50. Home Office immigration system statistics: returns (Ret tables)
- **Source:** Home Office, *Immigration system statistics, year ending June 2026: How many people are returned from the UK?*, published 27 Aug 2026. https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/immigration-system-statistics-year-ending-june-2026/how-many-people-are-returned-from-the-uk. Data tables: https://www.gov.uk/government/statistical-data-sets/immigration-system-statistics-data-tables#returns. Older series: Migration Observatory, *Deportations, removals and voluntary departures* (2016), http://migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/Briefing-Deportations.pdf.
- **Measures:** enforced returns, voluntary returns (assisted, controlled, and other verified), port returns, FNO returns, asylum-related returns, small-boat arrivals returned, by nationality. **Excludes:** people who leave unrecorded. It counts returns, not unique people. It does not record *why* a removal failed.
- **Coverage:** the current series runs from YE Dec 2010 (asylum-related from 2010). Revised back to 2007 in 2020 (read, D51). Old series 2004–2015 (read, PDF, MO 2016 Figure 1). Pre-2004 figures are not comparable, since voluntary departures were recorded separately only from 2004.
- **Shape:** quarterly series.
- **Slices:** type of return, nationality, FNO or not, asylum-related or not, small-boat arrivals.
- **What it shows (read; as of YE June 2026):**
  - 40,605 returns (+8%), of which 9,669 enforced (+7%) and 30,936 voluntary. 5,962 FNO returns (+12%). 12,568 asylum-related returns, the highest in the series. 18,646 port returns (−11%).
  - FNO returns peaked at 6,437 in 2016 and averaged about 5,500 a year in 2010–19.
  - 3,310 small-boat arrivals returned in the year. **9,694 returned out of all small-boat arrivals 2018–Jun 2026, 40% of them Albanian.** The next largest groups were Turkish (269) and Eritrean (230).
  - Albanians are 21% of enforced returns. Indians (10,226) are the largest group of voluntary returns.
  - Run (read, PDF, MO 2016): 40,896 removals and departures in 2015. Enforced removals declined from 2004 while voluntary departures rose. Asylum-case removals have declined every year since 2006.
  - **Highest year: 2008, about 67,980 removed or departed including port cases. Quarterly peak 18,080 in Q3 2008** (***unverified***: a secondary source and a Home Office Q1 2011 release seen in snippets only). Enforced removals peaked at 21,425 in 2004 (***unverified***, secondary).
  - Whether the 40,605 includes port returns needs checking. 9,669 + 30,936 = 40,605 exactly, so port returns appear to be counted outside it or inside voluntary returns.
- **Helps:** `tighten-within-the-echr` (returns up each year since 2021; asylum-related returns at a record, while still in the ECHR). **Hurts:** `bar-and-remove` (rising removals inside the ECHR undercut "impossible while in"; 150,000 a year is about 15 times current enforced returns); `tighten-within-the-echr` (total returns are still below 2008, and small-boat returns are under 5% of arrivals); `refuge` (the record asylum-related returns weaken any claim that returns are not happening, though that is not its argument).

### D51. Migration Observatory: *Deportation and returns of unauthorised migrants from the UK*
- **Source:** Migration Observatory, University of Oxford, briefing published 31 Mar 2026. https://migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk/resources/briefings/returns-of-unauthorised-migrants-from-the-uk/ (the old `deportation-and-voluntary-departure-from-the-uk` URL redirects there).
- **Measures:** a secondary synthesis of Home Office returns data, NAO and ICIBI reports, and FOI responses. The outcome tracking of refused asylum claims is the Observatory's own analysis of Home Office cohort data.
- **Coverage:** returns 2007/2010–2025. Refused-claim outcome cohorts 2010–2022.
- **Shape:** series plus cohort outcomes.
- **Slices:** type of return, FNO, asylum, small boats, claim cohort.
- **What it shows (read; as of 2025):**
  - About 38,000 returns in 2025, the highest since 2016. About 10,000 enforced.
  - **Average cost £48,800 per enforced return and £4,300 per voluntary return, 2024/25.** The original Home Office source for these costs is not given on the page; trace it.
  - **About half of refused asylum applications 2010–2022 had led to a return by end-2025: 72% of 2010 claims, 33% of 2018 claims, 48% of 2022 claims.**
  - About 7,500 small-boat arrivals returned 2018–2025, under 4% of 194,000.
  - Reasons removals fail:
    - 52% of enforced return attempts were cancelled in 2019 (11% in 2013); the NAO attributed this mostly to last-minute asylum claims and legal challenges.
    - At least one legal issue was raised in 73% of detentions in 2019.
    - Inability to obtain documents was the commonest reason not to detain (2014/15 inspection).
    - Origin-country non-cooperation is "hard to measure".
  - **No share of removals blocked by the ECHR is given.** The Home Office does not hold data on people granted status after a removal notice.
- **Helps:** `bar-and-remove` (half of refused claimants are never returned; last-minute legal challenges are a major cause); `refuge` (the barriers are mostly process, documents and countries of origin, not the ECHR as such). **Hurts:** `bar-and-remove` (no evidence that the ECHR is the main barrier); `tighten-within-the-echr` (only a third of 2018's refused claimants returned).

### D52. Home Office: *Issues raised by people facing return in immigration detention*, and later updates
- **Source:**
  - Home Office, published Mar 2021, updated 19 Jul 2021. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/issues-raised-by-people-facing-return-in-immigration-detention/issues-raised-by-people-facing-return-in-immigration-detention
  - *Additional statistics referred to in the asylum policy statement*, 21 Nov 2025. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/asylum-and-returns-policy-statement/additional-statistics-referred-to-in-the-asylum-policy-statement
  - News release "8 out of 10 last-minute attempts … rejected", 16 Mar 2021. https://www.gov.uk/government/news/8-out-of-10-last-minute-attempts-by-immigration-offenders-to-avoid-removal-are-rejected-study-finds
- **Measures:** the share of detention periods (people detained after immigration offences) in which each of nine "issues" was raised, and whether the detention ended in return or release. **Excludes:** FNOs and people detained on arrival. It does not attribute release to an issue: it shows only that someone raised an issue and was later released.
- **Coverage:** detention exits 2017–2019, outcomes to Sep 2020. Rights-based release rate for Jan–Sep 2022. **This is the closest thing to a "why removals fail" breakdown; it is internal management information, not National Statistics.**
- **Shape:** short series (3 years) plus a 2022 snapshot.
- **Slices:** issue type, year.
- **What it shows (read; as of 2019, with outcomes to Sep 2020):**

  | Issue raised (share of detentions) | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 | 2019: share ending in return |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Asylum claim | 32% | 37% | 47% | ~3% |
  | Human rights or other rights-based application | 10% | 8% | 5% | ~7% |
  | Further submissions | 13% | 14% | 13% | ~11% |
  | Appeal | 6% | 5% | 3% | ~5% |
  | Judicial review | 20% | 15% | 9% | ~8% |
  | Modern slavery referral | 3% | 5% | 16% | ~1% |
  | Rule 35 medical report | 12% | 15% | 14% | n/a |
  | Medico-legal report | 0% | 9% | 19% | n/a |
  | Disrupted return | 13% | 12% | 5% | ~25% |

  - 73% of 2019 detentions raised at least one issue, and 94% of those ended in release.
  - **86% of people who raised rights-based applications in detention, Jan–Sep 2022, were released** (read, 2025 page; the full analysis is "to be published").
  - Of last-minute claims (human rights, JR, modern slavery) from 2017 detainees, about 80% were eventually refused and about 10% found to have some merit (read, 2021 news).
- **Mechanism reading** (mine, for stage 4 to check):
  - The issue most often raised is an asylum claim. That engages the Refugee Convention and domestic asylum law, and Article 3 only partly through humanitarian protection.
  - A rights-based application (the ECHR route proper) was raised in only 5–10% of detentions.
  - Modern slavery referrals rest on ECAT and the Modern Slavery Act, as well as Article 4 ECHR.
- **Helps:**
  - `bar-and-remove`: most detainees raise something; almost all who do are released; about 80% of last-minute claims fail. This supports "claims are used to delay".
  - `tighten-within-the-echr`: this is the evidence base for the Bill's Article 8 and modern-slavery changes.
- **Hurts:**
  - `bar-and-remove`: the ECHR-specific route is a small minority of issues. Leaving the ECHR would not touch asylum claims (Refugee Convention), JRs (domestic law) or most modern-slavery referrals (ECAT).
  - `refuge`: 80% refusal of late claims.

### D53. National Audit Office: *Immigration enforcement* (HC 110, 2020)
- **Source:** NAO, 9 Jun 2020. https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Immigration-enforcement.pdf. Earlier: NAO, *Returning failed asylum applicants* (2005), https://www.nao.org.uk/report/returning-failed-asylum-applicants/.
- **Measures:** Immigration Enforcement performance, returns, detention, cost, and estimates of the illegal population.
- **Coverage:** mainly 2015–2019, with 2013/2014 comparators. No later NAO enforcement report was found (searched NAO for a 2023–26 successor; none found).
- **Shape:** audit snapshot with short series.
- **Slices:** enforced vs voluntary, FNO.
- **What it shows (read, PDF; as of 2019):**
  - **25,700 removal directions were set in the 12 months to Dec 2019 and 13,500 (52%) cancelled.** In 2014, 80% of enforced returns succeeded at the first attempt.
  - **62% of detainees (14,900) were released without removal in 2019**, against 54% in 2015.
  - Enforcement blamed "late or spurious claims for asylum", medical claims and modern slavery claims. The NAO saw "no systematic analysis" of why claims were rising.
  - Para 2.21 lists barriers: humanitarian or conflict grounds; human rights for people long in the UK; no identity documents; no return agreement. **No breakdown by reason.**
  - Voluntary returns fell from about 1,200 a month in 2015 to about 460 in 2019.
  - Illegal population estimates are in Figure 5; see D67.
  - The 2005 report put an enforced removal at about £11,000 and an assisted voluntary return at about £1,100 (***unverified***, snippet).
- **Helps:** `bar-and-remove` (half of removals cancelled, mostly by late claims). **Hurts:** `bar-and-remove` (barriers named are mixed, and the NAO does not single out the ECHR); `tighten-within-the-echr` (the department lacked the analysis to target fixes).

### D54. ICIBI: *An inspection of the Home Office's management of non-detained foreign national offenders* (2017)
- **Source:** Independent Chief Inspector of Borders and Immigration, published Nov 2017. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/656584/An_inspection_of_non-detained_FNOs.pdf. A 2023 ICIBI FNO inspection found removal performance "not efficient" and data poor (***unverified***, EIN snippet; not fetched).
- **Measures:** FNO removal planning and outcomes, barriers, absconding.
- **Coverage:** 2014/15–2016/17, plus two charter-flight case studies.
- **Shape:** short series plus case record.
- **Slices:** none beyond the charter flights.
- **What it shows (read, PDF):**
  - **Planned FNO removals 2014/15–2016/17: 24,289; 16,517 succeeded (68%); 7,772 failed (32%).**
  - "Almost 80%" of 1,653 reviewed cases had one or more barriers. Only 194 were "removable".
  - A "barrier" includes an outstanding asylum claim or application, or no passport or travel document.
  - Two Jamaica charters planned 144 and 163 removals and removed 42 and 32.
  - Commonest reasons for failure: fresh asylum claim after removal directions (55); judicial review (35); further representations (26); FNO not found (20); no enforcement visit for lack of resources (13).
  - Travel documents: "certain nationals were a particular problem" because embassies cooperated little.
- **Helps:** `bar-and-remove` (last-minute claims defeat most charter seats); `tighten-within-the-echr` (process fixes such as RED.0004 notices). **Hurts:** `bar-and-remove` (fresh *asylum* claims and JRs, not Article 8, top the list; a fifth of failures were Home Office capacity).

### D56. Bonavero Institute: *The ECHR and Immigration Control in the UK: Informing the Public Debate*
- **Source:** Bonavero Report 3/2025, University of Oxford, 4 Sep 2025. https://www.law.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-09/European%20Convention%20on%20Human%20Rights%20and%20Immigration%20Control%20in%20the%20UK_4%20Sept%202025.pdf. Academic, pro-Convention in framing.
- **Measures:** FNO human-rights appeals allowed (from D55) set against FNO stock and deportations, plus a media-accuracy review (379 reports, Jan–Jun 2025).
- **Coverage:** Apr 2016–Jun 2021.
- **Shape:** derived ratios (snapshot).
- **Slices:** Article 8 vs all human-rights grounds (by extrapolating the 70% sample).
- **What it shows (read, PDF):**
  - 922 FNOs won human-rights appeals at the First-tier Tribunal, Apr 2016–Jun 2021, against 26,091 FNOs deported: **3.5%**. Article 8 alone about 645: **about 2.5%**.
  - 45 such appeals in the 15 months to Jun 2021 equalled 0.73% of the 6,188 sentenced FNOs in custody. That is a mismatched denominator, so prefer the 3.5%.
  - Some press stories reported First-tier wins that the Upper Tribunal had already overturned.
- **Helps:** `tighten-within-the-echr` and `refuge` (Article 8 wins are a small fraction of deportations). **Hurts:** `bar-and-remove` (Article 8 is not stopping most FNO deportations). **Caveat** for all sides: the ratio is of appeals won to deportations done, not of blocked to attempted deportations. FNOs who never appeal, or who are not pursued, are outside it.

### D57. Home Office immigration statistics: detention
- **Source:** Home Office, *How many people are detained under immigration powers in the UK?*, YE June 2026, published 27 Aug 2026. https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/immigration-system-statistics-year-ending-june-2026/how-many-people-are-detained-under-immigration-powers-in-the-uk
- **Measures:** entries, stock, leavers by outcome, length of detention.
- **Coverage:** series from 2010 or earlier (range not yet checked).
- **Shape:** quarterly series.
- **Slices:** outcome, length, place (immigration removal centre or prison).
- **What it shows (read; as of YE / end June 2026):**
  - 22,037 entries (32,447 in 2015). **2,152 held at end June 2026, the most since June 2018.**
  - 21,517 leavers: 51% bailed, 46% returned (25% in YE June 2023).
  - 66% of returned leavers were held under a month.
  - Capacity was expanded, with Campsfield reopening; no total capacity is stated.
- **Implication for 150,000 a year** (my arithmetic, rough): if each enforced removal used about 2–4 weeks of detention, 150,000 removals would need roughly 6,000–12,000 beds continuously filled. That is 3–6 times today's population.
- **Helps:** `tighten-within-the-echr` (the returned share of leavers has nearly doubled since 2023 inside the ECHR). **Hurts:** `bar-and-remove` (the capacity gap to 150,000 a year).

### D58. Government policy record: *Restoring Order and Control* (CP 1418) and the Immigration and Asylum Bill impact assessment
- **Source:**
  - Home Office, *Restoring Order and Control: a statement on the government's asylum and returns policy*, Nov 2025. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691ae05b493305b49ce6e845/Restoring_Order_and_Control_-_A_statement_on_the_Government_s_asylum_and_returns_policy_final.pdf

- **Measures:** government claims and stated policy. Several figures rest on "internal Home Office analysis" that is not published.
- **Coverage:** claims and returns 2021–2026.
- **Shape:** record (policy), with snapshot figures.
- **Slices:** none.

### D64. Refugee Convention Article 31 in UK law: *Adimi*, s31 IAA 1999, *Asfaw*, s37 NABA 2022, UNHCR Guidelines No. 14
- **Source:**
  - *R v Uxbridge Magistrates' Court, ex p Adimi* [2001] QB 667 (Div Ct, 29 Jul 1999). Read via McConnachie, "Landmark Cases: Adimi" (UEA open access, 2026). https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/101943/1/McConnachie_-_Landmark_Cases_Adimi_for_UEA_Open_Access.docx
  - Nationality and Borders Act 2022 s37 (in force 28 Jun 2022). https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2022/36/section/37
  - *Adow v R* [2026] EWCA Crim 868 (7 Jul 2026). https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2026/868
  - UNHCR, *Guidelines on International Protection No. 14* (HCR/GIP/24/14, 23 Sep 2024). Not fetched; summary via EIN.
- **Measures:** legal rule and its interpretation.
- **Shape:** record (dated legal timeline).
- **What it shows:**
  - *Adimi* (read, secondary): Simon Brown LJ rejected the Crown's view that a refugee "must claim it where first he may". He held that "some element of choice is indeed open to refugees" and that "a merely short-term stopover en route … cannot forfeit the protection". Article 31 also covered offences committed when leaving the UK for elsewhere.
  - s31 IAA 1999 narrowed this at once. s31(2) bars the defence if the refugee stopped in another country, unless they could not reasonably have expected protection there. *Asfaw* [2008] UKHL 31 (3:2) upheld *Adimi*'s purposive reading but accepted that s31 "narrowed its scope".
  - **s37 NABA 2022 (read):** a refugee "is not to be taken to have come to the United Kingdom directly … if … they stopped in another country", unless they "could not reasonably be expected to have sought protection" there. "Without delay" means "as soon as reasonably practicable".
  - *Adow* (2026) quashed a 2007 conviction. A short Swedish stopover did not defeat s31.
  - The JCHR estimated that up to 5,000 people may have been wrongly imprisoned when the defence was available (read, secondary; date to check).
  - Lord Brown (Simon Brown LJ) later supported the Illegal Migration Act 2023 (read, secondary).
  - UNHCR GIP 14 adopts *Adimi*'s definitions (read, secondary).
  - Other states: the EU applies "first country of asylum" and safe-third-country rules through Dublin and the asylum procedures rules (read, secondary). No specific foreign court rulings on "coming directly" were read.
  - **Scope note:** Article 31 governs *penalties* for illegal entry. It does not by itself allow a state to refuse to hear a claim. Returning someone to a safe third country is governed by Article 33 and readmission law, which depends on the third country agreeing.
- **Helps:** `bar-and-remove` (UK statute now says what it argues, and has since 2022). **Hurts:** `bar-and-remove` (the international reading, in *Adimi* and UNHCR, rejects a duty to claim in the first safe country; Article 31 does not authorise barring claims); `refuge` (UK law has moved away from its reading).

## 3d. Problems for the human

**Outline claims the evidence does not support as written:**

- **The crux "How many failed removals does the ECHR actually block?" cannot be answered with a number.**
  - No source measures it. The Home Office does not hold data on grants after removal notices (D51) and has not published human-rights appeal data since June 2021 (D55, D56).
  - The best proxies point the same way: asylum claims (Refugee Convention and domestic law), JRs and modern-slavery referrals far outnumber human-rights claims among issues raised in detention (D52). Fresh asylum claims and JRs top the ICIBI charter-flight failures (D54). FNO human-rights appeals won are about 3.5% of FNO deportations (D56).
  - The ECHR's real weight may be larger than these show. Article 3 risk on return sits inside "asylum claims", and people never pursued for removal because return is impossible are not counted.
  - **Keep it as a crux, framed as an `assumption`, and state the proxies.**
- **`bar-and-remove`: "Lord Wolfson's review found the ECHR to be a major barrier to removals."** Narrow it.
  - Wolfson advised that the ECHR places "significant legal constraints" on immigration policy, but quantified nothing (D59).
  - He also advised that leaving the Refugee Convention is not necessary. That cuts against Reform's position in the same viewpoint.
- **`bar-and-remove`: "When Parliament tightened Article 8 in 2014, outcomes in the courts barely changed."** Partly supported.
  - Allowed human-rights-only FNO appeals fell about 70% between 2012/13 and 2015/16, then recovered by 2017/18 (D55).
  - Better wording: "the effect faded within three years". Mention the 2017 *Kiarie* ruling as a possible cause.
- **`bar-and-remove`: "Australia stopped boat arrivals by guaranteeing that no one arriving by boat would settle there."** The source evidence (D65) attributes the fall to **turnbacks at sea**. Arrivals continued for weeks after the July 2013 no-settlement rule. Narrow it to "turnbacks, plus a no-settlement guarantee", and note that Australia stayed in the Refugee Convention.
- **`bar-and-remove`: "Hundreds of thousands of people live in Britain with no right to be here."** Supported as a range, but the latest estimate is for 2017. The often-quoted 1.2 million upper bound was retracted by Pew in March 2025 (D67).
- **`tighten-within-the-echr`: "all 46 Council of Europe states signed the May 2026 declaration."** True, but say it is **non-binding** (D62).
- **`bar-and-remove` concession "removal at the proposed scale is far beyond anything achieved":** confirmed.
  - 150,000 is about 15 times current enforced returns and over double the 2008 high of about 68,000 including port cases (D50, ***unverified***).
  - The pledged £1.6bn equals about £10,700 a removal, against £48,800 per enforced return (D51, D70).

**Viewpoints with thin evidence:**
- **`bar-and-remove` on mechanism.** Its best evidence (D52, D53, D54) shows that late *claims* frustrate removals. Most of those claims are not ECHR claims, so the evidence supports "bar claims" more than "leave the ECHR".
- **`tighten-within-the-echr` on the Bill's effect.** The impact assessment sets no target (D58). No evidence yet that the IIAA will be faster.
- **`refuge`** gets little from this area beyond the Australia costs (D65, D66), the Article 31 record (D64) and polling (D72). It needs at least one candidate that cuts against it on enforcement. D52 (80% of late claims refused) is the strongest.
