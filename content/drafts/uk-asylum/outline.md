## Topic

- **Slug:** `uk-asylum` (new)
- **Title:** Asylum and Channel crossings in the UK
- **What is being argued about:** How Britain should treat people who
  claim asylum, how to stop small-boat crossings, how to remove people with
  no right to stay, and whether to stay in the European Convention on Human
  Rights (ECHR).
  - About 204,500 people crossed the Channel by small boat between 2018 and
    June 2026 (D1). Crossings in 2026 to 20 October were about 43% below
    the same point in 2025, a fall matched across Europe (D1, D5).
  - The UK–France "one in, one out" pilot returned 1,087 people to France
    and took 1,117 from France to 30 June 2026, about 4% of arrivals in the
    period; it ended on 30 September 2026, and the UK Resettlement Scheme is
    reopening (D9, D10).
  - Refugee status granted on claims made from 2 March 2026 lasts 30
    months and is reviewed at each renewal (D15). The Immigration and
    Asylum Bill (Bill 105, second reading 13 July 2026) would narrow
    Article 8 (family life) claims by statute, replace the tribunal with an
    Independent Immigration Appeals Authority, and set a twenty-year wait
    for refugees' settlement (D31, D63).
  - All 46 Council of Europe states agreed the non-binding Chişinău
    Declaration on 15 May 2026 on interpreting the Convention (D62).
  - The Conservatives and Reform UK propose banning asylum claims by
    people who enter illegally, mass removal and leaving the ECHR; Reform
    would also disapply the Refugee Convention (D70). A non-binding
    Conservative motion to leave was defeated 309–113 on 14 October 2026.
- **Scope boundaries:** unchanged from draft 3.
  - **In:** asylum claims and decisions; Channel crossings and other
    irregular entry; asylum accommodation and its cost; removals of failed
    asylum seekers, foreign offenders and visa overstayers; the ECHR,
    Refugee Convention and Human Rights Act as they bear on these;
    resettlement and safe routes; refugees' settlement.
  - **Out, to [`uk-immigration`](../uk-immigration/outline.md):** work,
    study and family routes and their settlement rules, except the
    "visa-to-asylum" route, which both topics touch.
  - **Out entirely:** the ECHR's role outside immigration; overseas aid.

## Viewpoints

### `bar-and-remove` — No one who enters illegally should be allowed to stay: bar their asylum claims, remove them, and leave the ECHR to make it possible

The position the Conservatives and Reform UK share (D70); Reform would also
disapply the Refugee Convention.

- **Arguments**
  - Over 200,000 people have crossed from France since 2018 (D1). Since
    2022, UK law has required refugees to claim in the first safe country
    they reach to benefit from Article 31's protection (s37 Nationality and
    Borders Act; D64). Parliament has already decided that crossing from
    France is not "coming directly"; the asylum system should act on it.
  - Removal barely happens. About half of claims refused in 2010–22 had
    led to a recorded departure by the end of 2025 (D51); in the year to
    June 2025, 58,000 claims were refused and under 11,000 people were
    removed (D58). In 2019, 52% of removal directions were cancelled (D53).
  - The system is gamed at the last moment. Among people in detention
    facing removal, late asylum claims, modern-slavery referrals and
    judicial reviews were the most common obstacles (D52), and charter
    flights failed for the same reasons (D54); 80% of late claims were
    refused (D52). Barring claims from illegal entrants closes that door.
  - Changing the law within the Convention has been tried and has not
    lasted. After the 2012 rules and the 2014 Act, foreign offenders'
    appeals allowed on human-rights grounds fell by about 70%, then
    recovered to near their old level by 2017/18 (D55). Lord Wolfson
    advised that the ECHR places significant legal constraints on removal
    and that withdrawal is legally possible (D59).
  - The appeal system is now the bottleneck: 87,450 asylum appeals were
    waiting in March 2026, against 6,319 in 2022, and appeals take 65 weeks
    on average (D22).
  - Certainty deters. Australia's arrivals collapsed once boats were
    turned back and no boat arrival could settle (D65).
  - Asylum support cost £4.0bn in 2024–25 (D26), and hundreds of thousands
    of people live in Britain with no lawful status (D67).
- **Concedes**
  - 59% of small-boat arrivals' claims decided at first instance were
    granted (2018–25, excluding 24,000 withdrawals; D2), and two in five
    appeals succeed (D22).
  - No source measures how many removals the ECHR itself blocks.
    Human-rights claims were 5–10% of issues raised in detention, and
    foreign offenders' successful human-rights appeals were about 3.5% of
    their deportations in 2016–21 (D52, D56). Most obstacles are asylum
    claims and judicial review, which leaving the ECHR alone would not
    remove.
  - Australia's fall followed turnbacks at sea, and Australia stayed in the
    Refugee Convention (D65); Wolfson advised that leaving the Refugee
    Convention is unnecessary (D59). Reform's further step has no evidence
    behind it.
  - 150,000 removals a year is about 15 times current enforced returns
    (9,669) and more than double the highest year on record; at the
    current cost of £48,800 per enforced return it would cost about £7.3bn
    a year, against £1.6bn pledged (D50, D51, D70).
  - The EU may end law-enforcement cooperation under the Trade and
    Cooperation Agreement if the UK denounces the ECHR (D61); whether
    withdrawal breaches the Good Friday Agreement is disputed (D60).
  - The latest estimate of the unlawful population is for 2017, and the
    most-quoted upper bound was retracted (D67).
  - Pluralities have opposed leaving the ECHR since 2016 (D72).
- **Principles:** `rules-must-mean-what-they-say`;
  `democratic-consent-over-membership`; `first-duty-to-its-own-people`.

### `tighten-within-the-echr` — Stop the crossings and speed up removals by changing British law, without leaving the ECHR

The government's position.

- **Arguments**
  - The obstacles to removal are mostly late claims, repeat submissions and
    slow appeals, not the Convention itself (D52, D54, D56). The Bill
    targets those directly: one route of appeal, a new appeals body, and
    statutory limits on Article 8 claims; its impact assessment models
    about 11,700 more Article 8 refusals a year (D58, D63).
  - Clearing the first-decision backlog works: it is down 77% from its
    peak, hotel use is down 71%, and asylum support fell from £4.7bn to
    £4.0bn a year (D3, D23, D26). Hotels were 35% of people but 76% of
    contract cost, so closing them is where the money is (D25).
  - Albanian arrivals have stayed more than 90% below their 2022 level,
    after the returns deal, fast processing and a grant rate that fell from
    62% to 3% (D2, D12, D21). Returns agreements with fast decisions keep a
    route closed.
  - Europe is moving with Britain: all 46 Council of Europe states signed
    the Chişinău Declaration on reading the Convention (D62).
  - Leaving the ECHR would let the EU end law-enforcement and
    criminal-records cooperation (D61), would not remove the Refugee
    Convention or the Convention against Torture, and would fix few of the
    measured obstacles (D52).
- **Concedes**
  - The 2026 fall in crossings cannot be credited to policy: French
    preventions fell in step with arrivals, weather explains little, and
    arrivals fell by about as much across Europe (D1, D5, D7).
  - The France pilot moved about 4% of arrivals and has ended (D9).
  - The Albanian fall began before the deal (D12).
  - The backlog has moved to the appeals system (D22). The impact
    assessment says its 11,700 figure "is not a target or forecast", and
    expects about 55% of those refused to stay anyway (D58).
  - Domestic Article 8 reform faded within three years once before (D55).
  - Temporary status has not raised refugees' employment where it has been
    studied (Denmark, Germany), and Denmark's fall in claims in 2016 matched Sweden's — though Sweden
    also switched to temporary permits that year, so the comparison does
    not isolate the policy (D13, D17, D19).
- **Principles:** `rules-must-mean-what-they-say`;
  `obligation-to-people-in-danger`.

### `refuge` — Britain owes refuge to people fleeing persecution: safe routes, fair hearings and the right to work

The Refugee Council, Liberal Democrats and Greens.

- **Arguments**
  - Most people who cross are found to be refugees: 59% of decided claims
    by small-boat arrivals were granted at first instance (D2), and two in
    five appeals succeed (D22). For claims made in 2007–2022,
    appeals and reconsideration raised the final grant rate by about 20
    points above the first decision (D21).
  - Safe routes empty the boats. Ukrainians and Hong Kongers came in the
    hundreds of thousands by visa and almost none by boat; for Eritreans,
    Sudanese and Iranians, the only route of any size is family reunion,
    now paused (D2, D11).
  - Deterrence has cost a great deal for little: the UK spent £715m on the Rwanda scheme and the Illegal Migration
    Act and relocated four volunteers and no asylum seekers (D30); Australia's offshore
    system has cost over A$13bn, with 18 deaths among those transferred
    (D65, D66).
  - Letting people work sooner gets them into jobs: early work bans leave
    lasting damage to refugees' employment (D28), and the evidence that
    work rights draw claims finds at most a modest association (D27).
  - Temporary status does not help refugees into work, and can hurt
    (Denmark, Germany; D17, D19, D20).
  - Removal failures are mostly late claims and judicial reviews, not the
    ECHR (D52); leaving it would cost the EU cooperation that tackles
    smuggling (D61).
- **Concedes**
  - UK claims per head are now about the European average (14.5 per
    10,000 against 14.9 in 2025), up from well below it before 2020 (D13).
  - Most of the 2022–2025 fall in the grant rate happened within
    nationalities (Afghan, Syrian, Turkish and Vietnamese claimants were
    each granted less often), not from a change in who claims (D21).
  - Arrivals in 2023 and 2024 were granted at under 50% at first instance,
    and 24,000 small-boat claims were withdrawn (D2).
  - 80% of late claims raised in detention were refused (D52), which
    supports the charge that some claims are made to delay removal.
  - Work rights show a modest association with where people claim (D27).
  - Australian boat arrivals fell from 20,587 people in 2013 to 160 in
    2014 after turnbacks began, while Australia stayed in the Refugee
    Convention (D65).
- **Principles:** `obligation-to-people-in-danger`;
  `rules-must-mean-what-they-say`.

## Crux candidates

Kind first; then the viewpoint ids divided.

- **`prediction` — What stops Channel crossings: certainty of removal, or
  safe routes?** All three. Australia's turnbacks (D65) and Ukraine's
  visas (D11) are each side's best case; the 2026 fall is unexplained
  (D1, D5, D7).
- **`assumption` — How many removals does the ECHR actually block?**
  `bar-and-remove`, `tighten-within-the-echr`. No source measures it; the
  proxies (D52, D55, D56) point to a small direct share, but Article 3 risk
  sits inside asylum claims, and people never put up for removal are not
  counted. A crux because no published data can settle it.
- **`prediction` — Will the Bill's appeal changes speed removals?**
  `tighten-within-the-echr`, `bar-and-remove`. The impact assessment models 11,700 more refusals a year but calls it
  neither a target nor a forecast (D58); the 2014 reforms faded (D55).
- **`priority` — Are refugee and human-rights obligations constraints to
  work within, or choices Parliament can revisit?** All three.
- **`tradeoff` — Is a refugee wrongly refused a worse error than a
  non-refugee wrongly allowed to stay?** `refuge`, `bar-and-remove`. The
  rates beside it: 59% initial grants, two in five appeals allowed, 80% of
  late claims refused (D2, D22, D52).
- **`prediction` — Would lower numbers restore trust, or only a system
  that visibly works?** Shared with `uk-immigration`.
