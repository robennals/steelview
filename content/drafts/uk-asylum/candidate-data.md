Stage 3 output ([WORKFLOW](../../WORKFLOW.md#3-find-candidate-data)),
researched 2026-10-24 by two parallel agents against
[outline.md](outline.md) draft 3, and merged here. The agents' working
files are in [research/](research/). The report plan (3c) was added in
stage 4 (below).

- **Candidate numbers:** D1–D33 flows, decisions and policy; D50–D76
  removals, enforcement and the law. D75 duplicated D22 and is merged
  into it. **Question numbers:** F (flows and policy), X (enforcement and
  law).
- **How figures are marked:** **(read)** seen on a fetched page at the URL
  given; **(read, PDF)** from a downloaded PDF's text; **(read,
  secondary)** on a fetched secondary page; ***unverified*** search
  snippets, press or memory. "My arithmetic" marks sums computed from a
  read dataset, which stage 5 recomputes.
- **"Read" is not a quote.** Stage 5 quotes every figure from the source.
- **Certificate checks.** The flows research downloaded the Eurostat files
  and two NAO PDFs with TLS certificate checking turned off. Their
  figures are provisional until stage 5 re-downloads them normally.

- **Source:** Home Office, `23_October_2026_Small_boats_-_time_series.ods`, updated 24 Oct 2026. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6adb41a0b62d80a107bc5c3f/23_October_2026_Small_boats_-_time_series.ods ; collection page https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/migrants-detected-crossing-the-english-channel-in-small-boats
- **Measures:**
  - SB_01: daily arrivals and boats.
  - SB_02: weekly arrivals, boats, and the people and events "prevented", as estimated by the French authorities.
  - Excludes larger vessels, clandestine entry on ferries and undetected arrivals. Figures are provisional operational data and differ slightly from the quarterly statistics (D3).
- **Coverage:** daily from 1 Jan 2018. In this file, preventions appear from about May 2024.
- **Shape:** series.
- **Slices:** day or week, boats, people per boat, preventions.
- **What it shows (read; my arithmetic):**

  | Year | Arrivals | Boats | Days with arrivals |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 2018 | 299 | 43 | 29 |
  | 2019 | 1,843 | 164 | 81 |
  | 2020 | 8,462 | 641 | 130 |
  | 2021 | 28,526 | 1,034 | 154 |
  | 2022 | 45,755 | 1,110 | 162 |
  | 2023 | 29,437 | 602 | 140 |
  | 2024 | 36,816 | 695 | 151 |
  | 2025 | 41,472 | 672 | 139 |

  - **2026 to 23 Oct:** 20,935 arrivals, against 36,954 to the same date in 2025 (−43%).
  - **Boats, same period:** 305 against 603 (−49%).
  - **Days with arrivals, same period:** 97 against 128.
  - **People per boat:** 68.6 in 2026 against 61.7 in 2025 and 41.2 in 2022.
  - **French preventions, weeks to about 24 Oct:** 10,708 in 2026 against 20,109 in 2025 (−47%).

- **Helps:**
  - `tighten-within-the-echr`: the 2026 fall is real and large.

  - `bar-and-remove`: crossings fell 43% while the UK stayed in the ECHR.
  - `refuge`: no new safe route opened before September 2026, yet crossings fell.

### D6. Migration Observatory, "Q&A: Why have small boat arrivals declined in 2026?" (think tank; academic)
- **Source:** Mihnea Cuibus, Migration Observatory, 10 Sep 2026. https://migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk/resources/commentaries/qa-why-have-small-boat-arrivals-declined-in-2026/
- **Measures:** a synthesis of evidence on the causes.
- **Shape:** commentary.
- **What it shows (read):**
  - 16,300 arrivals from 1 Jan to 9 Sep 2026, 47% fewer than in 2025. The year ending 9 Sep 2026 had about 27,900, the lowest since Dec 2021.
  - Weather is "unlikely to have had a significant impact".
  - French interceptions at sea are legally possible, but "it remains unclear how often this has been implemented".
  - Other possible factors:
    - Returns to France were about 4% of arrivals.
    - Boats are getting larger; one carried a record 230 people in Aug 2026.
    - More departures from Belgium.
    - Family reunion was suspended (Sep 2025).
    - The grant rate fell (76% in 2022 to 42% in 2025).

    - Syrian arrivals fell 67% (2024→25).

- **Helps:** `tighten-within-the-echr` (policy plausibly contributed); `refuge` (wider EU trends).
- **Hurts:** any viewpoint claiming a single cause.

  - Article 3 is absolute, but the severity threshold should stay "high and constant". Health-care gaps in the receiving state should only exceptionally create a risk. Diplomatic assurances may remove a risk.
  - Article 8: national authorities have "primary responsibility" for the balance, and the Court needs "strong reasons" to substitute its own view.
  - Third-country processing and "return hubs" are acceptable if they respect the Convention.
  - Critics: Amnesty ("two-tier" rights); ENNHRI.
  - The UK did not sign the May 2025 letter (***unverified***).
- **Helps:** `tighten-within-the-echr` (Europe moving its way, inside the Convention). **Hurts:** `tighten-within-the-echr` (non-binding; the Court is not bound to follow it); `bar-and-remove` (undercuts "reform from inside is impossible", but does not test it yet); `refuge` (a dilution, by its critics' account).

### D64. Refugee Convention Article 31 in UK law: *Adimi*, s31 IAA 1999, *Asfaw*, s37 NABA 2022, UNHCR Guidelines No. 14
- **Source:**
  - *R v Uxbridge Magistrates' Court, ex p Adimi* [2001] QB 667 (Div Ct, 29 Jul 1999). Read via McConnachie, "Landmark Cases: Adimi" (UEA open access, 2026). https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/101943/1/McConnachie_-_Landmark_Cases_Adimi_for_UEA_Open_Access.docx
  - Nationality and Borders Act 2022 s37 (in force 28 Jun 2022). https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2022/36/section/37
  - *Adow v R* [2026] EWCA Crim 868 (7 Jul 2026). https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ewca/crim/2026/868
  - UNHCR, *Guidelines on International Protection No. 14* (HCR/GIP/24/14, 23 Sep 2024). Not fetched; summary via EIN.
- **Measures:** legal rule and its interpretation.
- **Shape:** record (dated legal timeline).
- **What it shows:**
  - *Adimi* (read, secondary): Simon Brown LJ rejected the Crown's view that a refugee "must claim it where first he may". He held that "some element of choice is indeed open to refugees" and that "a merely short-term stopover en route … cannot forfeit the protection". Article 31 also covered offences committed when leaving the UK for elsewhere.
  - s31 IAA 1999 narrowed this at once. s31(2) bars the defence if the refugee stopped in another country, unless they could not reasonably have expected protection there. *Asfaw* [2008] UKHL 31 (3:2) upheld *Adimi*'s purposive reading but accepted that s31 "narrowed its scope".
  - **s37 NABA 2022 (read):** a refugee "is not to be taken to have come to the United Kingdom directly … if … they stopped in another country", unless they "could not reasonably be expected to have sought protection" there. "Without delay" means "as soon as reasonably practicable".
  - *Adow* (2026) quashed a 2007 conviction. A short Swedish stopover did not defeat s31.
  - The JCHR estimated that up to 5,000 people may have been wrongly imprisoned when the defence was available (read, secondary; date to check).
  - Lord Brown (Simon Brown LJ) later supported the Illegal Migration Act 2023 (read, secondary).
  - UNHCR GIP 14 adopts *Adimi*'s definitions (read, secondary).
  - Other states: the EU applies "first country of asylum" and safe-third-country rules through Dublin and the asylum procedures rules (read, secondary). No specific foreign court rulings on "coming directly" were read.
  - **Scope note:** Article 31 governs *penalties* for illegal entry. It does not by itself allow a state to refuse to hear a claim. Returning someone to a safe third country is governed by Article 33 and readmission law, which depends on the third country agreeing.
- **Helps:** `bar-and-remove` (UK statute now says what it argues, and has since 2022). **Hurts:** `bar-and-remove` (the international reading, in *Adimi* and UNHCR, rejects a duty to claim in the first safe country; Article 31 does not authorise barring claims); `refuge` (UK law has moved away from its reading).

### Flows, decisions and policy

### Claims the evidence undercuts

1. **`refuge`'s "the UK receives fewer claims per head than many European countries."** This was true before 2020, at about 6 per 10,000 against an EU average of 14–27. In 2025 the UK is at 14.5, level with the EU27's 14.9, and ranks 16th of the EU+. Suggested rewording: "about the European average per head, and below Germany, Ireland, Greece and Spain".
2. **`tighten-within-the-echr`'s "crossings fell with French interceptions."**
   - French preventions fell 47% in 2026, about as much as arrivals. The prevention rate stayed near a third (D1).
   - Channel attempts as a whole fell 43%, in line with EU-wide falls: central Mediterranean −56% (D5).
   - Weather is not the reason (D7), but the evidence fits fewer launches (fewer boats, more people per boat), which fits supply disruption *or* lower demand.
   - No source separates them. Present the 2026 fall as **complicated**, not as a policy success.
3. **"The Albania deal caused the collapse."** The outline already flags this. Confirmed: the collapse began two months before the deal (D12, D2).
4. **"Denmark's temporary protection cut claims."** Denmark's intake is very low (D13). But its 2016 fall matched Sweden's, and its grant rate is near the EU's (D14). The integration evidence from Denmark, Germany and Sweden mostly finds that less-secure status does not help employment (D17, D19), with one short-run exception (D18). The government statement (D15) cites a "40-year low" that I could not check beyond 2008.
5. **"The backlog is cleared."** The initial-decision backlog is down 77%, but the asylum appeal caseload rose from 6,319 (2022) to 87,450 (March 2026), and appeals take 65 weeks (D22). The cost has moved rather than vanished.
6. **"Most small-boat arrivals are found to be refugees."** At initial decision this is true over 2018–25 (59%), and for the main 2025 nationalities. But it excludes 24,000 withdrawals. Arrivals in 2023 and 2024 were granted at under 50% initially.

### Removals, enforcement and the law

**Outline claims the evidence does not support as written:**

- **The crux "How many failed removals does the ECHR actually block?" cannot be answered with a number.**
  - No source measures it. The Home Office does not hold data on grants after removal notices (D51) and has not published human-rights appeal data since June 2021 (D55, D56).
  - The best proxies point the same way: asylum claims (Refugee Convention and domestic law), JRs and modern-slavery referrals far outnumber human-rights claims among issues raised in detention (D52). Fresh asylum claims and JRs top the ICIBI charter-flight failures (D54). FNO human-rights appeals won are about 3.5% of FNO deportations (D56).
  - The ECHR's real weight may be larger than these show. Article 3 risk on return sits inside "asylum claims", and people never pursued for removal because return is impossible are not counted.
  - **Keep it as a crux, framed as an `assumption`, and state the proxies.**
- **`bar-and-remove`: "Lord Wolfson's review found the ECHR to be a major barrier to removals."** Narrow it.
  - Wolfson advised that the ECHR places "significant legal constraints" on immigration policy, but quantified nothing (D59).
  - He also advised that leaving the Refugee Convention is not necessary. That cuts against Reform's position in the same viewpoint.
- **`bar-and-remove`: "When Parliament tightened Article 8 in 2014, outcomes in the courts barely changed."** Partly supported.
  - Allowed human-rights-only FNO appeals fell about 70% between 2012/13 and 2015/16, then recovered by 2017/18 (D55).
  - Better wording: "the effect faded within three years". Mention the 2017 *Kiarie* ruling as a possible cause.
- **`bar-and-remove`: "Australia stopped boat arrivals by guaranteeing that no one arriving by boat would settle there."** The source evidence (D65) attributes the fall to **turnbacks at sea**. Arrivals continued for weeks after the July 2013 no-settlement rule. Narrow it to "turnbacks, plus a no-settlement guarantee", and note that Australia stayed in the Refugee Convention.
- **`bar-and-remove`: "Hundreds of thousands of people live in Britain with no right to be here."** Supported as a range, but the latest estimate is for 2017. The often-quoted 1.2 million upper bound was retracted by Pew in March 2025 (D67).
- **`tighten-within-the-echr`: "all 46 Council of Europe states signed the May 2026 declaration."** True, but say it is **non-binding** (D62).
- **`bar-and-remove` concession "removal at the proposed scale is far beyond anything achieved":** confirmed.
  - 150,000 is about 15 times current enforced returns and over double the 2008 high of about 68,000 including port cases (D50, ***unverified***).
  - The pledged £1.6bn equals about £10,700 a removal, against £48,800 per enforced return (D51, D70).

**Viewpoints with thin evidence:**
- **`bar-and-remove` on mechanism.** Its best evidence (D52, D53, D54) shows that late *claims* frustrate removals. Most of those claims are not ECHR claims, so the evidence supports "bar claims" more than "leave the ECHR".
- **`tighten-within-the-echr` on the Bill's effect.** The impact assessment sets no target (D58). No evidence yet that the IIAA will be faster.
- **`refuge`** gets little from this area beyond the Australia costs (D65, D66), the Article 31 record (D64) and polling (D72). It needs at least one candidate that cuts against it on enforcement. D52 (80% of late claims refused) is the strongest.

**Missing data:**

- Reasons for failed removals after 2019, by category. The 2025 statement promises an updated "issues raised" analysis "as soon as possible" (D52).
- Article 3 success rates separately from Article 8: not published.
- European states' practice on Article 8 in deportation: not researched to the end (searched; nothing comparable found).
- A UK government legal view on the GFA and ECHR withdrawal: not found.
- More in Common and Ipsos items on leaving the ECHR or banning asylum claims: searched, not found. No 2026 YouGov ECHR tracker reading was found.
