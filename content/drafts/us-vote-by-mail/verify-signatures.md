## D66. Signature rejections by state, county and voter group — **corrected**

### (a) Washington State Auditor, 2020 general election

- **Statewide 0.72%, fewer than 30,000 of about 4.2 million: confirmed.** Note that late ballots are excluded.
  > For the 2020 general election, the overall ballot rejection rate in Washington was 0.72 percent, or fewer than 30,000 ballots out of about 4.2 million cast, excluding late ballots. County ballot rejection rates ranged from less than one-tenth of a percent to about 1.5 percent of ballots cast, listed in Exhibit 3.
  — Evaluating Washington's Ballot Rejection Rates (Report No. 1029711), Office of the Washington State Auditor, 2022-02-01, https://sao.wa.gov/sites/default/files/audit_reports/PA_Evaluating_WA_Ballot_Rejections_ar-1029711.pdf [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Rates by race and ethnicity: confirmed.** Black 2.49%, Native American 1.59%, Hispanic 1.57%, Asian/Pacific Islander 1.24%, white 0.63%. After controls, Black voters were about twice as likely as white voters to be rejected. The difference was not statistically significant for Native American voters, because the group was small.
  > We found a statistically significant difference in ballot rejection rates between white voters and voters from all other racial and ethnic groups, except Native Americans and multiracial voters. ... We also found the greatest disparity was between white and Black voters, with rejection rates at 0.63 percent and 2.49 percent, respectively (shown in Exhibit 6). Even when controlling for other variables, we estimated Black voters were twice as likely to have ballots rejected than white voters.
  — same report, pp. 18–19 [fetched 2026-10-26]. Exhibit 6 values as extracted: "2.49% ... 1.59% 1.57% ... 1.24% 0.63% ... Black Native American Hispanic Asian/ Pacific Islander White".
  > The rejection rates for ballots cast by Black, Native American, Hispanic, Asian and Pacific Islander Washingtonians were all higher than 1.2 percent, while the rejection rate for ballots cast by white Washingtonians was 0.63 percent.
  — "Few ballots rejected, but rejection rates vary by county, gender and race", The Audit Connection Blog, Washington State Auditor, 2022-02, https://sao.wa.gov/the-audit-connection-blog/2022/few-ballots-rejected-rejection-rates-vary-county-gender-and-race-performance-audit-finds [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Age: confirmed, with figures added.** Voters aged 18–21 were rejected at 2.68%, voters 65+ at 0.17%. After controls, the youngest voters were almost three times as likely to be rejected. First-time voters were rejected at more than five times the rate of experienced voters.
  > voters had a rejection rate of 2.68 percent, while the oldest voters had a rejection rate of just 0.17 percent. After accounting for other variables with our regression analysis, we estimated that Washington's youngest voters were almost three times more likely to have their ballots rejected than Washington's oldest voters. This was consistent when we considered only ballots rejected for mismatched signatures.
  — same report, pp. 17–18 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Sex: confirmed (men 42% more likely).**
  > We estimated that male voters were 42 percent more likely than female voters to have ballots rejected. Our result was inconsistent with two University of Florida studies of mail-in ballots that examined correlations between gender and ballot rejection.
  — same report, p. 18 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Counties "4–7 times": confirmed, with the wording made precise.** The controls were age, race, education and income. The outcome was rejection for a signature mismatch *or a missing signature*. For signature mismatches alone, the gap was up to 18 times.
  > After accounting for demographic variables like age, race, education levels and income in our regression analysis, we estimated that ballots submitted to some counties were four to seven times more likely to be rejected for signature mismatches or being unsigned than ballots submitted to other counties. The disparity was even greater when analyzing the likelihood a ballot would be rejected for just signature mismatches, with ballots from two counties having 18 times the likelihood of rejection as another county.
  — same report, p. 14 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Re-check of more than 7,200 signatures with more than 98% agreement: confirmed.** The method was software screening, then auditors trained by the State Patrol, then Secretary of State reviewers. Projected to the whole population, 98.7% of decisions were appropriate. The report also found that low-rejection counties accepted less conclusive signatures.
  > We agreed with county determinations for more than 98 percent of the signatures reviewed, but found counties with lower rejection rates were more likely to accept less conclusive signatures. When applying results from our sample to the entire population of signatures, we estimated that 98.7 percent of county decisions were appropriate.
  — same report, p. 15 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **"No evidence of bias": confirmed in substance; candidate wording corrected.** The candidate file quotes "did not find evidence of bias in the decisions made by elections workers". That string appears in neither the report nor the blog. Use one of these instead:
  > We found no evidence of bias when counties accepted or rejected ballots
  — same report, p. 23 (section heading) [fetched 2026-10-26]
  > Disparities in rejection rates for different racial and ethnic groups are unacceptable, and we explicitly looked for bias in the decisions to accept or reject individual ballots, to see if that could help explain the differences. We found no evidence of bias in those decisions. While that is good news, it also means we are not able to explain what causes rejection rates to vary for these groups of voters.
  — same report, State Auditor's Conclusions, p. 36 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Subjectivity (useful to both sides):**
  > Although trainings and statewide criteria may help standardize processes, we found that even experienced reviewers can come to different conclusions. ... Members of our own team participating in the review also disagreed on whether many of the signatures matched.
  — same report, p. 16 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Training (relevant to "untrained"):**
  > All 10 counties reported training employees before reviewing any signatures, and sometimes also taking subsequent trainings to increase their expertise.
  — same report, "Meeting state law" section [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Recommendations.** There are six, addressed to all counties: voter education about signature matching; faster, multi-channel cure contact, including in the voter's language; data-driven tracking of practices; targeted outreach; collecting several signatures per voter; and written policies.
  > Recommendations include: giving voters more information; improving efforts to reach voters whose ballots are challenged; tracking information about efforts taken to help determine effectiveness; and increasing the number of voter signatures collected and kept on file. We also recommend all counties develop written policies and procedures.
  — same report, Executive Summary [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Cure:** Lower-rejection counties cured more ballots (65% vs 50%).
  > Counties with lower rejection rates had higher cure rates (65 percent cured) than the other counties (50 percent cured).
  — same report, p. 22 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Scope note:**
  > Although county election officials can reject ballots for several reasons, 97 percent are rejected because they are late (postmarked after Election Day), unsigned, or the ballot signature does not match the voter signature on file.
  — same report, audit scope and methodology section [fetched 2026-10-26]

### (c) Florida 2018 (Baringer, Herron & Smith) — confirmed from the authors' abstract

> This motivates our examination of over 8.2 million ballots cast in the 2018 General Election in Florida, including 2.6 million VBM ballots, of which approximately 1.2 percent were rejected by local election officials. ... We find that younger voters and voters needing assistance are disproportionately likely to have their VBM ballots rejected. ... Lastly, we find significant variation in the rejection rates of VBM ballots cast across Florida's 67 counties, suggesting a non-uniformity in the way local election officials verify these ballots.
— "Voting by Mail and Ballot Rejection: Lessons from Florida for Elections in the Age of the Coronavirus" (draft abstract, posted by Rick Hasen), Election Law Blog, 2020-04-18, https://electionlawblog.org/?p=110834 via https://web.archive.org/web/2020id_/https://electionlawblog.org/?p=110834 [fetched 2026-10-26]. Published as *Election Law Journal* 19(3):289–320, doi:10.1089/elj.2020.0658.
- The abstract also reports high rejection for "out-of-state and military dependents".
- Note: the Florida 1.2% rejection covers *all* reasons, mostly late ballots. The full text (with separate signature figures) was not read.

### Other study: Cottrell, Herron & Smith (Florida, inexperienced mail voters)

> Our analysis of ballot rejections in the political battleground state of Florida shows that inexperienced mail voters were three and 2.75 times more likely, respectively, in the 2016 and 2018 General Elections to have their ballots rejected than experienced mail voters. ... Moreover, the effect of inexperience on vote-by-mail ballot rejection rates varies by party affiliation, race/ethnicity, age, and gender.
— "QSS Faculty Publish Paper on Voting By Mail" (abstract of "Vote-by-mail ballot rejection and experience with mail-in voting"), Dartmouth Program in Quantitative Social Science, 2020-10-12, https://qss.dartmouth.edu/news/2020/10/qss-faculty-publish-paper-voting-mail [fetched 2026-10-26]

- **Notes:**
  - The Washington audit both supports and undercuts `signature-discretion`:
    - It supports it: county is the strongest predictor of rejection, up to 18 times for mismatches alone; the judgment is "inherently subjective"; even auditors disagreed among themselves.
    - It undercuts it: no bias was found, 98.7% of decisions were appropriate, and every county trained its staff.
  - The racial gaps are total rejection rates, including unsigned ballots. Only the age gap is separately reported as holding "for mismatched signatures".
  - Washington's 0.72% excludes late ballots, so it is not directly comparable with EAVS rates, which include them.
  - The share of rejections caused by signatures swings widely by cycle (15.8% to 40.7%). Part of that is reporting: the "other" share is large and varies (Oregon reported 96.1% of its 2024 rejections as "other").

## D68. Signature-matching lawsuits — **corrected** (attribution of the Texas "5,000" figure)

- **New Hampshire (*Saucedo v. Gardner*, D.N.H., 14 Aug 2018): confirmed from the opinion itself.**
  > As will become evident, this signature-matching process is fundamentally flawed. Not only is the disenfranchised voter given no right to participate in this process, but the voter is not even given notice that her ballot has been rejected due to a signature mismatch. Moreover, moderators receive no training in handwriting analysis or signature comparison; no statute, regulation, or guidance from the State provides functional standards to distinguish the natural variations of one writer from other variations that suggest two different writers; and the moderator's assessment is final, without any review or appeal.
  — Saucedo v. Gardner, No. 17-cv-183-LM, Opinion No. 2018 DNH 160 (Doc. 72), U.S. District Court for the District of New Hampshire, 2018-08-14, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-nhd-1_17-cv-00183/pdf/USCOURTS-nhd-1_17-cv-00183-1.pdf [fetched 2026-10-26]
  - Scale, a new comparator:
    > In the 2016 General Election, .35% of all absentee ballots submitted were rejected due to a signature mismatch (275 rejections out of 78,430 absentee ballots).
    — same [fetched 2026-10-26]
  - Holding:
    > plaintiffs are entitled to summary judgment on their procedural due process claim. The court grants plaintiffs' request for declaratory relief insofar as RSA 659:50, III is unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment
    — same [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Texas (*Richardson v. Texas Sec. of State*, 5th Cir. No. 20-50774, 19 Oct 2020): confirmed, with one correction.**
  - Stay granted:
    > Because Texas's strong interest in safeguarding the integrity of its elections from voter fraud far outweighs any burden the state's voting procedures place on the right to vote, we stay the injunction pending appeal.
    — Richardson v. Texas Secretary of State, No. 20-50774, U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, 2020-10-19, https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/20/20-50774-CV0.pdf (Smith, J.) [fetched 2026-10-26]
  - Fraud language:
    > It seeks to stop voter fraud where the problem is most acute—in the context of mail-in voting. "[T]he potential and reality of fraud is much greater in the mail-in ballot context than with in-person voting."
    — same [fetched 2026-10-26]
    - This line is itself a quotation of *Veasey v. Abbott*, 830 F.3d 216, 239 (5th Cir. 2016) (en banc).
  - The due-process point is a prediction made on a stay motion, not a merits holding:
    > Thus, the Secretary is likely to show that voting does not implicate any state-created liberty interest under the Due Process Clause.
    — same [fetched 2026-10-26]
  - **Correction:** the "about 5,000 rejections in 2016 and 2018" figure is from Judge Higginbotham's *separate concurrence*, which concurred "only in the decision to stay". It is not from the majority opinion, and he uses it to stress the stakes for voters:
    > In 2016 and 2018, "approximately 5,000 [Texas] ballots were rejected on the basis of perceived signature mismatches." Such "small" differences have the potential to decide both local and national elections. And with the large increase in votes cast by mail in our ongoing pandemic that error rate would toss out far greater numbers.
    — same, Higginbotham, J., concurring in the stay [fetched 2026-10-26]
  - Later history (reversed in 2022 on sovereign immunity; cert. denied 21 Feb 2023) was not re-checked here.
- **Pennsylvania (J-113-2020, 23 Oct 2020): confirmed.**
  > For the reasons that follow, we conclude that the Election Code does not authorize or require county election boards to reject absentee or mail-in ballots during the canvassing process based on an analysis of a voter's signature on the "declaration" contained on the official ballot return envelope for the absentee or mail-in ballot. We, therefore, grant the Secretary's petition for declarative relief, and direct the county boards of elections not to reject absentee or mail-in ballots for counting, computing, and tallying based on signature comparisons conducted by county election officials or employees, or as the result of third-party challenges based on such comparisons.
  — In re: November 3, 2020 General Election, Petition of Kathy Boockvar, No. 149 MM 2020 [J-113-2020], Supreme Court of Pennsylvania (Todd, J.), 2020-10-23, https://www.pacourts.us/assets/opinions/Supreme/out/J-113-2020mo%20-%20104584871117842321.pdf [fetched 2026-10-26]
  - This is a statutory ruling, not a constitutional one. It explains why Pennsylvania appears in NCSL's "do not conduct signature verification" list.
- **Notes:** *Democracy N.C. v. NCSBE* was not checked (still unverified).

## D69. Cobb County, Georgia 2020 signature audit — **verified** (primary source found)

> A signature match audit in Cobb County found "no fraudulent absentee ballots" and found that the Cobb County Elections Department had "a 99.99% accuracy rate in performing correct signature verification procedures." ... Of the 150,431 absentee ballots received by Cobb County elections officials during the November elections, the audit "reviewed 15,118 ABM ballot oath envelopes from randomly selected boxes," or around 10% of the total. The sample size was originally chosen to meet the 99% confidence threshold.
— "3rd Strike Against Voter Fraud Claims Means They're Out After Signature Audit Finds No Fraud", Georgia Secretary of State, 2020-12-29, https://sos.ga.gov/news/3rd-strike-against-voter-fraud-claims-means-theyre-out-after-signature-audit-finds-no-fraud via https://web.archive.org/web/2024id_/https://sos.ga.gov/news/3rd-strike-against-voter-fraud-claims-means-theyre-out-after-signature-audit-finds-no-fraud [fetched 2026-10-26]

> The audit found that only two ballots should have been identified by Cobb County Elections Officials for cure notification that weren't. In one case, the ballot was "mistakenly signed by the elector's spouse," and in the other, the voter "reported signing the front of the envelope only." In both cases, the identified voters filled out the ballots themselves.
— same [fetched 2026-10-26]

- **Notes:**
  - The audit was triggered by "credible allegations that the process was not followed in the June primaries". It was run with the GBI.
  - Inspection teams were two people each. A referee broke ties "only ... on six occasions".
  - New detail that bears on detection power: one of the two missed envelopes was signed by the voter's *spouse* and still passed the county's check. Signature review missed a non-voter signature, though the ballot itself was the voter's own.
  - The sample covered *accepted* ballots only. It could find wrongly accepted ballots but not wrongly rejected ones.
