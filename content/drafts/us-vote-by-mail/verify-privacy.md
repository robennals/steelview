## D65. Rasmussen Reports–Heartland Institute survey, Dec 2023 — **verified** (with clarifications)

- **Sample, dates, mode, panel provider, margin of error:** 1,085 likely voters, 30 Nov–6 Dec 2023, "national telephone and online", fieldwork by Pulse Opinion Research, stated ±3 points.
  > A new national telephone and online survey by Rasmussen Reports and The Heartland Institute finds that 21% of Likely U.S. voters who voted by absentee or mail-in ballot in the 2020 election say they filled out a ballot, in part or in full, on behalf of a friend or family member, such as a spouse or child, while 78% say they didn't. ... The survey of 1,085 U.S. Likely Voters was conducted on November 30-December 6, 2023 by Rasmussen Reports and The Heartland Institute. The margin of sampling error is +/- 3 percentage points with a 95% level of confidence. Field work for all Rasmussen Reports surveys is conducted by Pulse Opinion Research, LLC.
  — One-in-Five Mail-In Voters Admit They Cheated in 2020 Election, Rasmussen Reports, 2023-12-12, https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/politics/partner_surveys/one_in_five_mail_in_voters_admit_they_cheated_in_2020_election [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Mail share (for the ~325 subsample):** 30% yes, 68% no, 2% not sure. 30% of 1,085 is about 326 (the weighted share; the page gives no unweighted count).
  > Did you vote with an absentee or mail-in ballot in the 2020 election? 30% yes 68% no 2% not sure
  — Toplines - Voters Fraud - Heartland December 2023, Rasmussen Reports, 2023-12-12, https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/politics/partner_surveys/toplines_2_voters_fraud_heartland_december_2023 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Someone else filled out yours: 19% yes, 79% no, 2% not sure.**
  > Answered by the 30% of respondents who voted by absentee or mail-in ballot: During the 2020 election, did a friend or family member fill out your ballot, in part or in full, on your behalf? 19% yes 79% no 2% not sure
  — Toplines (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **You filled out someone else's: 21% yes, 78% no, 0% not sure.**
  > During the 2020 election, did you fill out a ballot, in part or in full, on behalf of a friend or family member, such as a spouse or child? 21% yes 78% no 0% not sure
  — Toplines (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Out-of-state voting: 17% yes, 82% no.**
  > During the 2020 election, did you cast a mail-in ballot in a state where you were no longer a permanent resident? 17% yes 82% no 1% not sure
  — Toplines (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Signed for someone: 17% yes, 81% no.**
  > During the 2020 election, did you sign a ballot or ballot envelope on behalf of a friend or family member, with or without his or her permission? 17% yes 81% no 1% not sure
  — Toplines (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Other items (all respondents):** 8% said they were offered pay or a reward for voting; 10% know someone who admitted voting by mail in another state; 11% know someone who admitted filling out a ballot for another person.
  > Answered by all: During the 2020 election, did a friend, family member, or organization, such as a political party, offer to pay or reward you for voting? 8% yes 91%no 1% not sure
  — Toplines (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Subgroups (from the release):** mail voting by age was 42% (under 40), 22% (40–64), 30% (65+). Out-of-state voting was 31% among under-40 mail voters against 2% among those 65+.
  > For example, 31% of voters under 40 who voted by mail in 2020 say they cast a mail-in ballot in a state where they were no longer a permanent resident, compared to 11% of those ages 40-64 and just two percent (2%) of voters 65 and older.
  — Rasmussen release (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Rasmussen's own methodology:** automated landline calls plus an online panel, weighted, including weighting by party.
  > To reach those who have abandoned traditional landline telephones, Rasmussen Reports uses an online survey tool to interview randomly selected participants from a demographically diverse panel. After the surveys are completed, the raw data is processed through a weighting program to ensure that the sample reflects the overall population in terms of age, race, gender, political party, and other factors.
  — Methodology, Rasmussen Reports, undated, https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/about_us/methodology [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Critique: Krosnick (opt-in panel, no valid margin of error).**
  > "[T]he vast majority of Rasmussen data come from an ordinary opt-in online panel" in which participants actively seek to join rather than being randomly selected, "which AAPOR tells you cannot have a margin of error, because there is no systematic sampling involved," Krosnick said.
  — Trump's Latest Bogus Claim About Mail-In Vote Fraud in Pennsylvania, FactCheck.org, 2024-09-20, https://www.factcheck.org/2024/09/trumps-latest-bogus-claim-about-mail-in-vote-fraud-in-pennsylvania/ [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Critique: Krosnick ("very biased").**
  > According to Jon Krosnick, director of the Political Psychology Research Group at Stanford University, Rasmussen "is known to produce very biased results, typically pro-Republican."
  — FactCheck.org (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Critique: 30% mail share against about 43% (EAC).**
  > The poll purports to have a "Margin of Sampling Error, +/- 3 percentage points with a 95% level of confidence," but only 30% of those surveyed responded that they voted in 2020 via absentee or mail-in ballot. So those who said they voted by mail-in ballots represent a subset of the larger polling group. (The Election Assistance Commission reported that about 43% of the electorate voted by mail in 2020.
  — FactCheck.org (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Critique: Levitt (signature-mismatch rates).**
  > "[T]he results are wildly out of line with literally all other incoming information, including the rate of ballots flagged in any jurisdiction anywhere in the country for signature mismatches," Levitt told us via email.
  — FactCheck.org (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Critique: disability advocates (help is legal).**
  > "It is indeed perfectly legal in every state to help a disabled person to fill out a mail-in ballot. It's actually protected by federal law."
  — FactCheck.org quoting Michelle Bishop, National Disability Rights Network (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **FiveThirtyEight dropped Rasmussen, March 2024 (via FactCheck, citing the Washington Post).**
  > In March, the Washington Post reported that FiveThirtyEight "dropped the right-wing polling firm Rasmussen Reports from inclusion in its polling averages and forecasts."
  — FactCheck.org (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Notes:**
  - **The "answered by the 30%" label is printed only above the first and third ballot items.** The questions page and toplines label "Answered by the 30% of respondents who voted by absentee or mail-in ballot" above the "filled out your ballot" and "out-of-state" items. They do not label the "you filled out someone else's" and "signed" items. The release does describe all four as shares of mail voters, and the crosstab shows 0% for non-mail voters, so treating all four as mail-voter items is consistent with Rasmussen's own reporting.
  - **Heartland calls all of these practices illegal, which is wrong.** The release says: "All of these practices are illegal, Heartland Institute officials noted." But marking a ballot for, or with, a voter who needs help is lawful in many cases (Voting Rights Act Section 208). The wording "in part or in full" also counts a spouse who just helped.
  - **A demographic crosstab is public.** https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/politics/partner_surveys/crosstabs_2_voters_fraud_heartland_december_2023 gives weighted percentages by group, with no unweighted counts. For "did a friend or family member fill out your ballot", "yes" was 34% among 18–39s, 11% among 40–64s and 2% among those 65+ (read from the crosstab table). The release itself flags these age gaps.
  - **The questions were asked three years after the election.** Fielded Nov–Dec 2023 about 2020, with the preface "Your responses will remain anonymous, so please answer honestly."
  - **What it can support:** a claim that a widely cited poll reports high levels of shared ballot completion. It cannot support a fraud estimate (it is an opt-in panel, the wording mixes lawful help with forgery, the 30% mail share is well below 43%, and the results conflict with administrative data). Even as a measure of lost secrecy it is weak, because of how the sample was drawn.
- **Blocked:**
  - **The Washington Post articles were not fetched.** These were Philip Bump, 2023-12-12, https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2023/12/12/trump-rasmussen-poll-2020-election-fraud/ and 2024-03-08, https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2024/03/08/rasmussen-538-polling/. They are paywalled, and fetching an archived copy was refused by the permission policy (walled content). Both are cited here only as FactCheck quotes them.

## D70. Oregon surveys after universal vote by mail — **unverifiable at the primary; secondary verified, with a date correction**

- **"As many as a third with others present; 1% or fewer felt pressured"**: confirmed as worded in The Conversation, by Susan Orr and James Johnson.
  > There is not a lot of research about bribery and coercion in mail-in elections in the U.S. But two surveys undertaken taken after Oregon introduced universal vote by mail in 1998 found that as many as a third of voters completed their votes while others were present. Just 1%, or fewer, reported feeling pressured by the presence of another person.
  — Voting by mail is convenient, but not always secret, The Conversation (Susan Orr, SUNY Brockport; James Johnson, University of Rochester), 2020-08-24, https://theconversation.com/voting-by-mail-is-convenient-but-not-always-secret-144716 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **What the article cites:** the words "two surveys" link to a single paper, doi 10.2307/420670. That is Priscilla L. Southwell and Justin Burchett, "Survey of Vote-by-Mail Senate Election in the State of Oregon", *PS: Political Science & Politics* 30(1), 1997. Its abstract (Cambridge Core) says:
  > The special Senate election in Oregon, held during January 1996, was the first time in which a federal election was held using a vote-by-mail format. The purpose of this survey was to address most of the relevant issues and concerns involving vote-by-mail elections and to provide an assessment of this electoral method at a crucial time in the political process.
  — Survey of Vote-by-Mail Senate Election in the State of Oregon, PS: Political Science & Politics (Cambridge University Press), 1997, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/abs/survey-of-votebymail-senate-election-in-the-state-of-oregon/88E239F6F7199AE95B923280B2BD8C3F [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Correction:** the source the article links to surveyed the January 1996 special Senate election, before the 1998 ballot measure. The Conversation's "after Oregon introduced universal vote by mail in 1998", and the candidate's and outline's "after 1998", are therefore not supported by the cited source. Write instead: "Oregon surveys in the 1990s (as reported by Orr and Johnson)". The second survey may be Southwell's later "Five Years Later: A Re-Assessment of Oregon's Vote by Mail Electoral Process" (*PS* 37(1), 2004), but The Conversation does not link it, and its text was not reachable.
- **Southwell's 2007 open-access paper (2003 Oregon survey) has no presence or pressure item.** It covers preference and self-reported turnout only. It is still useful context, for example the response rate:
  > The response rate was 62.28%.
  — Vote-by-Mail: Voter Preferences and Self-Reported Voting Behavior in the State of Oregon, American Review of Politics, 2007, https://journals.shareok.org/arp/article/view/297 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Southwell's own view (useful counterpoint):**
  > Some are concerned that the system provides no guarantee of a secret ballot, but there has been no evidence that undue influence on voters – like bribes or threats – has been a problem in Oregon elections conducted by mail.
  — Mail-in voting lessons from Oregon, the state with the longest history of voting by mail, The Conversation (Priscilla Southwell), 2020-09-03, https://theconversation.com/mail-in-voting-lessons-from-oregon-the-state-with-the-longest-history-of-voting-by-mail-145155 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Notes:**
  - **What it can support:** lost secrecy ("others were present"), as reported second-hand.
  - **The "1% or fewer" figure is a self-report of feeling pressured,** not a measure of coercion. The exact question wording, sample size and per-survey figures could not be read.
  - **A stronger, primary-sourced national figure exists:** D72's PLOS ONE study (23% of mail voters had someone else in the room). Use that as the main "others present" figure, and cite Oregon only as "reported by Orr and Johnson".
- **Blocked:**
  - **The two Southwell articles in *PS* are paywalled.** Cambridge Core shows only an abstract for the 1997 paper and no abstract for the 2004 one (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/five-years-later-a-reassessment-of-oregons-vote-by-mail-electoral-process/B1132DCF91E347ABAEDDBBE041C80718).
  - **No open copy was found.** JSTOR (https://www.jstor.org/stable/420670) returned a script wall, and Semantic Scholar reports the paper as "CLOSED".

## D71. Oregon single-county survey (Portland State University) — **verified (traced to original press report; corrected detail)**

- **Heritage's wording:**
  > Furthermore, in a survey of voters in just one Oregon county by an assistant professor at Portland State University, 5 percent of voters admitted that "other people marked their ballots, and 2.4% said other people signed their ballot envelopes."
  — Four Stolen Elections: The Vulnerabilities of Absentee and Mail-In Ballots, Heritage Foundation (Hans von Spakovsky), 2020-07-16, https://web.archive.org/web/20250109031748/https://www.heritage.org/election-integrity/report/four-stolen-elections-the-vulnerabilities-absentee-and-mail-ballots [fetched 2026-10-26; the live page returned 403]
- **Heritage's footnote:** "A 'Modern' Democracy That Can't Count Votes, Los Angeles Times (Dec. 11, 2000), https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-dec-11-mn-64090-story.html (accessed July 9, 2020)."
- **The original source is the LA Times.** It was Melody Rose's pilot study in Washington County in 2000, with 818 respondents, giving about 5% "marked" and 2.4% "signed".
  > In a pilot study, Rose gathered preliminary survey data this year on voters in Washington County, outside Portland. About 5% of 818 respondents said other people marked their ballots, and 2.4% said other people signed their ballot envelopes. Rose suspects the real number is higher, because people are reluctant to admit being party to a crime.
  — A 'Modern' Democracy That Can't Count Votes, Los Angeles Times, 2000-12-11, https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2000-dec-11-mn-64090-story.html [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Same article, context:**
  > Part of the concern is about possible intimidation from family or friends when voters mark their ballots at home--or at "ballot parties," where group leaders might pressure others to vote as instructed. But a bigger worry is about forged signatures.
  — LA Times (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Corrections and notes:**
  - **County, year and sample were missing from the candidate entry.** They are Washington County, the November 2000 election, and 818 respondents.
  - **Heritage drops "About" and the qualifiers.** Heritage writes "5 percent of voters admitted" and leaves out "About", "pilot study" and "preliminary".
  - **The researcher is Melody Rose,** then an assistant professor of political science at Portland State University.
  - **No published version was found.** The figures exist only as an unpublished pilot study reported in a newspaper; no question wording or method is available.
  - **The extrapolation is the newspaper's.** The LA Times's own "36,000 of 1.5 million" figure is an extrapolation of this pilot ("If the trend holds").
  - **"Other people marked their ballots" does not mean fraud.** It covers lawful help and spouses filling in a ballot at the voter's request, so it measures lost secrecy or delegation, not coercion. "Signed their ballot envelopes" is closer to a violation; the LA Times notes "It is a felony to sign someone else's ballot."
  - **Status: usable only as a dated, unpublished pilot result,** attributed to the LA Times.
- **Blocked:** heritage.org returned 403 (the archive.org copy was used).

## D72. Gerber, Huber, Doherty, Dowling and Hill: ballot-secrecy research — **verified (with an important addition: a mail vs in-person breakdown exists)**

- **25% doubt secrecy; over 70% share their vote choice (BJPS 2013).** The authors are Gerber, Huber, Doherty and Dowling, not Hill.
  > Novel items added to a nationally representative survey show that 25 per cent of respondents report not believing their ballot choices are kept secret and over 70 per cent report sharing their vote choices with others. ... Consistent with this view, regression analysis shows that social forces appear to have a greater effect on vote choices among people who doubt the formal secrecy of the ballot.
  — Is There a Secret Ballot? Ballot Secrecy Perceptions and Their Implications for Voting Behaviour, British Journal of Political Science 43(1), 2013, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-political-science/article/abs/is-there-a-secret-ballot-ballot-secrecy-perceptions-and-their-implications-for-voting-behaviour/CD20CED9A450927354E8E754FC67D5E3 [fetched 2026-10-26; abstract only, full text paywalled]
- **Field experiment: reassurance letters raised turnout by about 3.4 points among people who had never voted, and had no effect on people who had voted before.** The experiment was run in Connecticut in 2010. The Conversation's "3.5%" rounds this.
  > In particular, the experiment shows that providing information about the anonymity of the ballot and the protections against intimidation at the polls to those who had not previously voted increased turnout in the November 2010 election by an estimated 3.4 percentage points. This represents a substantial increase in turnout for this group—proportionally the intervention increases turnout by about 20 percent.
  — Do Perceptions of Ballot Secrecy Influence Turnout? Results from a Field Experiment, NBER Working Paper 17673 (published in AJPS 2013), 2011-12, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17673/w17673.pdf [fetched 2026-10-26]
  > We find that these letters increased turnout for registered citizens without records of previous turnout, but did not appear to influence the behavior of citizens who had previously voted.
  — NBER w17673 abstract (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **A breakdown by voting method exists: mail voters doubt secrecy more.** In a December 2010 YouGov survey (2,876 analysed), mail voters were almost 10 points more likely than Election Day in-person voters to say their choices were not kept secret.
  > However, Fig 2 shows a clear pattern where those who reported voting by mail harbor greater doubts about secrecy (compared to those who voted in-person on Election Day). They were almost 10 percentage points more likely to say their choices were not kept secret, and approximately 4 percentage points more likely to say it would be not difficult at all for someone to find out about their choices. They were also 15 percentage points more likely to say they thought elected officials could access their voting records to find out who they voted for.
  — The voting experience and beliefs about ballot secrecy, PLOS ONE 14(1): e0209765 (Dowling, Doherty, Hill, Gerber, Huber), 2019-01-07, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0209765 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **The key figure for the outline: over 23% of mail voters had someone else in the room.** Of those, 85% had one other person present. Among all mail voters, 17.8% discussed their vote with someone in the room, 8.5% asked for advice, and more than 6% showed their ballot to someone.
  > For example, we asked these voters whether anyone else was in the room with them when they cast their ballot and over 23 percent of respondents said that at least one other person was in the room with them when they filled out their ballot. ... Additionally, 17.2 percent of all respondents who voted by mail said that at least one other person in the room with them while they filled out their ballot was also filling out a ballot, 17.8 percent said they discussed who they were voting for with someone else in the room, and 8.5 percent said they asked someone else for advice about how to vote on a particular race or issue while filling out their ballot. More than six percent of our mail voters reported showing their ballot to someone else after filling it out.
  — PLOS ONE (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **In-person comparator (same survey):**
  > Only three percent said someone at the polling place asked them who they were voting for and only five percent said that they were able to see who another voter was voting for. However, 22 percent said there was no curtain or privacy screen around their voting space and over 20 percent said that someone walking by could see who they were voting for.
  — PLOS ONE (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Survey method and the "12%" headline:**
  > To investigate these questions, we fielded a survey in December of 2010 through YouGov/Polimetrix, which uses a combination of sampling and matching techniques to approximate a random digit dialing sample. ... Approximately 12 percent of the weighted sample said their choices were not kept secret.
  — PLOS ONE (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Notes:**
  - **The 12% here and the 25% in BJPS come from different surveys and different wordings,** so don't treat them as the same measure. The NBER paper describes the 25% as "around one quarter of the population (Gerber et al. 2011a)".
  - **Mail voters were 19% of past voters in the PLOS sample,** so the 23% figure rests on a few hundred respondents. The paper gives no exact count in the text.
  - **The sample is an opt-in YouGov panel matched to a random-sample frame,** weighted, with 2010 midterm voters.
  - **The in-person "someone walking by could see" figure (over 20%) is not comparable with the mail "someone in the room" figure (over 23%).** One is a perceived exposure, the other the presence of a person. Mail voters were not asked the in-person items.
  - **What it can support:** strong primary-sourced evidence of lost secrecy in mail voting (others present, discussing, showing ballots), and that secrecy doubts change turnout among new voters. It is not evidence of coercion or fraud: the "in the room" and "discussed" items are mostly benign household voting.
- **Blocked:** BJPS full text is paywalled (abstract used).

## D73. UK family-voting research — **verified (with notes)**

- **Birch and Watt (2004), as summarised by Essex:**
  > In 2004 Birch produced an article for Political Quarterly (co-written with an Essex lawyer, Bob Watt) that considered family voting in the context of remote electronic voting and postal voting. This paper highlighted the potential problems with remote voting, including the loss of ballot secrecy and the possibility of undue influence.
  — Research on electoral malpractice (University of Essex), REF 2014 Impact Case Study 43401, undated (REF 2014), https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=43401 [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **The 2006 law change:**
  > The Government accepted the findings of the report and this led to the drawing up of a White Paper and a change in the law in 2006 on postal voting. Part 14 of The Electoral Administration Act 2006, which came into force in 2007, included the introduction of stricter measures to prevent the abuse of postal vote fraud through the requirement, among other things, of individual voter signature and signature verification for postal voters, and this has led to a reduction in the abuse of postal voting since its introduction.
  — REF case study (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Democracy Volunteers, 2024 general election: family voting seen in 21% of polling stations,** affecting 3.5% of the voters observed.
  > Our team saw family voting in 21% of all the polling stations that we observed. ... In total, 3.5% of all the 20,289 voters we observed voting at these elections were either causing, or were affected by, Family Voting, some 706 people. ... This compares to 4.3% in the 2023 local elections and 4.7% in the 2024 local elections and 5.5% in the 2023/24 parliamentary by-elections.
  — Final Report: Westminster General Election July 2024, Democracy Volunteers, 2024-07-22, https://democracyvolunteers.org/final-report-westminster-general-election-july-2024/ [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Democracy Volunteers on postal voting: they cannot measure it.**
  > As an unsupervised route for voting, postal voters are potentially subject to intimidation and coercion, family voting, in a similar fashion to what we can observe at polling booths. Due to the nature of this practice, we are unable to collect data on this issue, though the cross-party support for the Ballot Secrecy Act 2023, which attempts to prevent this in polling stations, indicates potential support for controls to prevent this during postal voting.
  — Democracy Volunteers GE Report 2024 (PDF), 2024-07, https://democracyvolunteers.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Democracy-Volunteers-GE-Report-2024.pdf [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Pickles review (2016) on postal-vote secrecy and household pressure:**
  > Some considered that postal voting should be restricted to those unable to get to a polling station to vote in person for reasons of health or disability. It was argued that unrestricted postal voting undermines the fundamental secrecy of the ballot and that despite existing safeguards can never guarantee that the voter has not been influenced in some way.
  — Securing the ballot: Report of Sir Eric Pickles' review into electoral fraud, UK Government (Cabinet Office), 2016-08, https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a8058bd40f0b62305b8a9d3/eric_pickles_report_electoral_fraud.pdf [fetched 2026-10-26]
  > The ability to prove undue influence within households is hampered by the reluctance of individuals to report it. Evidence to the review maintained the reasons were considered similar to those which prevent sufferers of domestic abuse from coming forward.
  — Pickles review (as above), para 192 [fetched 2026-10-26]
  > R18. The offences contained in section 66 of the Representation of the People Act 1983 which protect the secrecy of the ballot in relation to in person voting should be extended to postal ballots.
  — Pickles review (as above) [fetched 2026-10-26]
- **Notes:**
  - **The 2004 paper's title is about electronic voting.** It is "Remote electronic voting: Free, fair and secret?" (*Political Quarterly* 75: 60–72). It covers postal voting by extension.
  - **The REF case study is Essex's own impact claim,** not independent evidence that the 2006 Act reduced fraud.
  - **Democracy Volunteers' figures are for polling stations,** which supports the "hurts" side: family voting also happens in person. Their postal-voting concern is stated, not measured.
  - **Their definition is broad.** Family voting includes "General Oversight" and "Collusion" (the 2024 spread: clear direction 25.6%, general oversight 33.3%, collusion 35.6%, other 5.6%), so it is mostly evidence of lost secrecy, not coercion.
  - **Pickles reports concerns raised by respondents to the review,** not measured prevalence.
  - **The UK Electoral Commission was not separately fetched.**

## Other surveys asking mail voters about presence, help or pressure

- **Dowling et al. (PLOS ONE 2019, 2010 data)** is the best primary-sourced US item: over 23% of mail voters had someone else in the room, with in-person comparators (see D72). It was fielded through YouGov/Polimetrix and partly matched to the 2010 CCES validated-vote file, but it was a separate module, not a standing CES question.
- **SPAE (MIT):** the 2022 report ("How We Voted in 2022", https://electionlab.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-05/HowWeVotedIn2022.pdf, fetched 2026-10-26) has no item on whether others were present or helped. Its mail-voter items are about difficulty with requesting, receiving and completing the ballot. A search snippet suggests the 2008 SPAE final report gave about 3% of absentee voters "needed assistance". The source (https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/49847/Final%20report20090218.pdf) returned HTTP 429 twice, so this is **unverified**; don't use it.
- **Cooperative Election Study:** not checked at the questionnaire level. No standing presence or pressure item was found.
