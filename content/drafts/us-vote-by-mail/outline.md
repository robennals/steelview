## Topic

- **Slug:** `us-vote-by-mail`
- **Title:** Voting by mail in the US
- **What is being argued about:** Whether voting by mail should be available
  to everyone, sent to everyone, or kept for people who cannot vote in
  person; how mail ballots should be checked (signatures, ID numbers,
  witnesses) and who may return them; whether ballots arriving after Election
  Day should count; and who decides. The March 2026 executive order's Postal
  Service rule is blocked for November; *Watson v. RNC* (June 2026) left
  receipt deadlines to states and Congress; the House-passed SAVE America Act may
  limit mail voting, though whether it does is disputed (an advocacy outlet
  says yes; CRS called the mail limit a proposed amendment).
- **Scope boundaries:** Mail and absentee ballots from application to count.
  **Election subversion is in scope**: fraud by campaigns and aligned
  groups, and manipulation of which ballots count by officials, are the
  dangers both sides argue about. ID at the polls is a
  [separate topic](../us-voter-id/outline.md). Excludes redistricting and
  campaign finance.

## Viewpoints

### `mail-exception` — Mail voting should be the exception, kept for voters who need it

- **Arguments**
  - **The secret ballot exists so that no one can watch a vote, and a mail ballot gives that up.**
    - The Australian ballot was adopted because a vote buyer could otherwise "watch until he placed it in the polling box"; in Chile, the landlords' vote disappeared once ballots became secret (D56).
    - At a kitchen table that protection is gone, even when nothing illegal happens. In a 2010 national survey, over 23% of mail voters had someone else in the room and more than 6% showed their ballot to someone; mail voters were about 10 points likelier than Election Day voters to doubt their vote was secret (D72).
    - Germany's constitutional court, upholding postal voting, conceded that it reduces public control of how votes are cast (D64). Britain's 2016 fraud review recorded the argument that unrestricted postal voting "undermines the fundamental secrecy of the ballot" (D73).
    - The widely cited 2023 Rasmussen–Heartland poll put the share of mail voters whose ballot someone else filled out, in part or in full, at 19%. It is an opt-in poll of about 325 mail voters, so the reliable figure is the 2010 survey's (D65).
  - **Detected fraud is a floor, not a measure.**
    - Pressure inside a household is rarely reported: Britain's review compared the silence to that of domestic-abuse victims (D73).
    - A ballot signed by a spouse can pass a signature check, as one did in Cobb County's 2020 audit (D69).
    - So low case counts cannot show that household fraud is rare. How large the gap is, no study measures (a crux).
  - **Trust matters as much as honesty.**
    - Americans trust the count of mail ballots less than in-person ones in every Pew reading since 2020 (53% against 79% in October 2026), and the gap splits by party (D74).
    - Losers have disputed several recent elections; in 2020 and in Arizona in 2022 the dispute centred on mail ballots (D75).
    - A result the losing side rejects is destabilising whether or not any fraud occurred.
  - **The elections US courts have voided for fraud in recent decades mostly involved absentee ballots.**
    - They include East Chicago's 2003 mayoral primary, North Carolina's 9th district in 2018, Paterson in 2020, and Bridgeport's 2023 mayoral primary, where video showed party operatives stuffing drop boxes (D25, D51, D52, D76).
    - Case databases compiled by both sides find absentee fraud far more often than impersonation at the polls (D76). The Carter–Baker Commission called absentee ballots the largest source of potential fraud (D38).
  - **Most democracies do not offer postal voting on demand.**
    - International IDEA counts 11 of the 204 countries it codes that offer postal voting to all voters at home (D63).
    - France abolished it in 1975 after serious fraud and votes cast for fictitious voters, and rejected restoring it in 2022 (D61).
    - British election courts found mass postal fraud in Birmingham in 2005 and voided elections in Woking in 2013 and Tower Hamlets in 2015; Britain tightened postal voting in 2006 and 2022 (D62).
- **Empirical questions**
  - What is the documented history of mail and absentee fraud, in the US and abroad?
    - *Context:* cases against mail ballots cast over the same years; the in-person record over the same years; what remedies followed each case.
  - Why did the secret ballot emerge, and what happened to vote-buying and intimidation after it?
    - *Context:* Australia 1856, Britain 1872, US states 1888 onward; research on turnout and corruption before and after.
  - How do other democracies handle remote voting, and why did those that restricted it do so?
    - *Context:* the full set (International IDEA), not only the restrictive ones; countries that expanded postal voting (Switzerland, Germany) and their experience.
  - How many mail voters complete their ballots with others present, or report someone else filling in or pressuring their ballot, by surveys of what quality?
    - *Context:* Dowling et al. 2010 and its in-person comparators (D72); the Rasmussen–Heartland wording, sample and critiques (D65); the Oregon reports (D70, D71); UK family-voting research (D73).
  - How has confidence in vote counts changed, by party and voting method, and what happened after disputed results?
    - *Context:* Gallup and Pew series side by side (D18, D19, D74); each disputed outcome and its resolution (D75); the winner–loser gap in the US against other democracies (D41, D42).
- **Concedes:**
  - **All-mail states:** no voided election in Colorado, Oregon, Utah or Washington appears even in Heritage's own case file, and their rejection rates are no different (D76, D1).
  - **Scale:** fraud cases are few against the 47.6 million mail ballots counted in 2024 (D1), and the Paterson criminal case was dismissed in April 2026 (D52).
  - **Other democracies:** the US, Britain, Germany, Switzerland and Canada are among the 11 that offer postal voting to all voters. Germany cast 47% of its 2021 vote by post and Switzerland about 90%, and Britain finds "no evidence of large-scale electoral fraud" (D63, D64, D62).
  - **The secrecy figures:** most of the shared voting they record is benign, and few report pressure (1% or fewer in early Oregon surveys, D70). Polling places are not fully private either: 22% of in-person voters had no privacy screen (D72), and observers saw family voting at 21% of UK polling stations (D73).
  - **Confidence** follows who won more than how votes were cast (D41).
  - **Access:** mail serves disabled voters, more than half of whom voted by mail in 2020 (D26).
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; rules must mean what they say.

### `mail-works` — Voting by mail works, and the states that use it prove it

- **Arguments**
  - **Mail raises turnout a little and favours neither party.**
    - Studies of counties switching to universal mail found turnout up about two points and no partisan tilt (D10).
    - Letting 65-year-olds vote by mail without an excuse moved them between methods, not into or out of voting (D27).
  - **The all-mail states show none of the organised fraud found elsewhere.**
    - Even Heritage's own case file lists no voided election in Colorado, Oregon, Utah or Washington. Their cases are individuals, mostly casting a dead relative's ballot, and they were caught (D76).
    - Rejection rates in all-mail states are no different from elsewhere (D1).
  - **Mail has checks in-person voting lacks: a paper trail, signature checks and a chance to fix mistakes.**
    - 585,457 ballots entered the cure process in 2024, and 54% were cured (D1).
    - Cobb County's 2020 signature audit found no fraudulent ballots (D69), and NC-09 was caught through its own paper trail (D25).
  - **Mail serves voters who cannot easily get to a polling place.** More than half of voters with disabilities voted by mail in 2020, against 42% of other voters (D26).
  - **Mature democracies vote by post on demand without scandal.**
    - Germany cast 47% of its 2021 vote by post, and its constitutional court upheld it (D64). Switzerland votes about 90% by post (D64).
    - Britain finds no evidence of large-scale fraud since its reforms (D62).
- **Empirical questions**
  - How has the share of votes cast by mail changed since 2004, and who uses it?
    - *Context:* by age, party and disability; the 2020 spike against the trend.
  - What is the effect of universal mail voting on turnout and on each party's vote share?
    - *Context:* effect sizes with their uncertainty; studies before and in 2020.
  - How do rejection and cure rates compare between all-mail states and others?
    - *Context:* rejection reasons; the trend across EAVS years.
- **Concedes:**
  - **Rejections:** 584,463 returned mail ballots were rejected in 2024 (D1). Rejection falls unevenly by group and county (D66).
  - **Fraud:** the elections voided for fraud mostly involved absentee ballots (D76), and new ID rules for mail ballots can spike rejections, as in Texas in 2022 (D16).
  - **Partisanship:** Democrats now vote by mail far more than Republicans, 37% to 24% (D2).
  - **Utah,** the Republican-led all-mail state, makes mail opt-in from 2029 (D6).
  - **Trust:** confidence in counting mail ballots is lower than in-person (D74).
  - **Secrecy:** mail ballots are often filled in with others present (D72), and Germany's court conceded reduced integrity (D64).
  - **Practice abroad:** universal postal voting is unusual (D63).
- **Principles:** an equal voice for every citizen.

### `signature-discretion` — Signature checks let officials throw out valid ballots on a judgement call

- **Arguments**
  - **Standards for what counts as a match differ by state and county.**
    - 32 states check signatures, nine do not, and six check ID numbers instead (D67).
    - New Hampshire's process was struck down for having no notice, "no training in handwriting analysis", no standards and no appeal (D68). Pennsylvania's courts held its election code does not allow rejection on signature analysis at all (D68).
  - **Rejections fall unevenly, so the same ballot can count in one county and not the next.**
    - Washington's state auditor found Black voters' ballots rejected at 2.49% against 0.63% for white voters, and the youngest at 2.68% against 0.17% for the oldest. After demographic controls, some counties were four to seven times likelier than others to reject for signature reasons (D66).
    - Florida showed the same pattern for young voters and voters needing assistance (D66). The auditors themselves disagreed on whether many signatures matched (D66).
  - **Any discretionary veto over ballots is an opening for bias or for someone who wants a particular result.**
    - Arizona's losing candidate for governor went to court over signature review to try to overturn the 2022 result (D75), and county officials have refused to certify results (D55).
    - No case of signature review being used to change a result has been found. The fix is notice and a chance to cure, bipartisan review, or objective checks such as ID numbers.
- **Empirical questions**
  - How many mail ballots are rejected for signature reasons, by state, county and voter group?
    - *Context:* the trend over EAVS years; variation between neighbouring counties with similar voters; rates where cure is required.
  - What standards, training and appeal rights govern signature matching, and what have courts said?
    - *Context:* states that replaced signatures with ID numbers or witnesses, and their rejection rates.
  - Has signature review ever been used to change a result?
    - *Context:* the wider record of officials manipulating which ballots count.
- **Concedes:**
  - **No bias found:** Washington's auditor found "no evidence of bias" in the decisions and judged 98.7% of them appropriate (D66).
  - **Safeguards exist:** Washington requires training and Colorado bipartisan review, and 35 states plus DC have statewide cure (D67). Cure recovers over half of flagged ballots (D1).
  - **Objective checks reject valid ballots too:** Texas's ID-number rule rejected 12% in a primary (D16).
  - **Signature checks already miss household signing** (D69). They are one of the few safeguards on a ballot cast out of sight.
- **Principles:** an equal voice for every citizen; rules must mean what they say.

### `election-day-receipt` — Ballots should be in by Election Day, and counted fast

- **Arguments**
  - **A ballot arriving after Election Day was not in the count when the election was held.**
    - 15 states still count such ballots; Ohio, North Dakota and Utah ended that in 2025, and Kansas's change is blocked in court (D5). That is back to the 2016 count after 21 states in 2020 (D44).
    - The Supreme Court in *Watson* recognised avoiding "the appearance of fraud from late-arriving ballots" as a reason a legislature may choose (D24).
  - **Slow counts are a choice.**
    - Texas and Florida each counted more than 10 million votes within about a day, while California reported a third of its vote over a month (D22).
    - States that let officials prepare mail ballots early had counted more of their vote in the first hours (D43).
    - Days of counting leave room for suspicion; this is argued from values, since no study here links count length to confidence.
- **Empirical questions**
  - Which states count late-arriving ballots, and how many arrive late?
    - *Context:* late ballots against total cast and against margins; military and overseas ballots separated; changes since 2020.
  - How long do states take to count, and what explains the differences?
    - *Context:* whether mail ballots can be processed before Election Day, beside mail share.
- **Concedes:**
  - **Federal law:** *Watson* held it does not require receipt by Election Day (D24).
  - **Scale:** late ballots are a small share, under 1% of votes in Nevada's two largest counties (D23).
  - **Deadlines reject eligible ballots:** they caused 18% of 2024 rejections and three-quarters of some 2026 primary rejections, though the Postal Service says over 95% of ballots arrive on time (D1, D44).
  - **The early-processing advantage** fades within a day (D43).
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; results the losers can accept.

### `states-decide` — Mail-voting rules belong to the states, and presidents have no say in them

- **Arguments**
  - **The Elections Clause gives the president no role.** The Supreme Court kept the Postal Service rule under the March 2026 order blocked for November (D24).
  - **States choose and revise their own systems.**
    - Oregon's voters adopted all-mail voting by initiative in 1998 (D6); Utah's legislature is now making mail opt-in (D6).
    - Three states ended grace periods in 2025 (D5), and no-excuse mail voting has spread and partly retreated (D47).
    - *Watson* sends policy arguments "to legislatures, not courts" (D24).
- **Empirical questions**
  - What federal bills and orders on mail voting are live, and what has happened to each?
  - How much do state mail-voting rules vary, and how has that changed since 2016?
- **Concedes:**
  - **Congress:** it can set rules for federal elections — its power is "paramount" (D46). *Watson* says it could set a receipt deadline, and two justices say it "may create such rules" for mail ballots (D24).
  - **Federal floors already exist** for military and overseas voters (D45).
  - **The March 2026 order** itself was allowed to proceed (D24).
- **Principles:** decisions made closest to the people; democratic consent.

## Cross-cutting questions

- **Who has subverted American elections through absentee and mail ballots,
  and through control of which ballots count?**
  - *Fraud by campaigns and aligned groups:* NC-09 2018; Paterson 2020; East
    Chicago 2003; Bridgeport 2023; Philadelphia 1993; historical absentee fraud by machines.
  - *Manipulation by officials:* selective rejection or acceptance of
    ballots; refusals to certify; disputes over which ballots to count.
  - *Remedies:* the secret ballot, tracking, signature and ID checks,
    bipartisan review, audits — when each was adopted and what it changed.
  - *Context:* Britain's postal-fraud cases and reforms; France's 1975
    abolition; countries that run large postal votes without scandal.

## Crux candidates

Kind first; then the viewpoint ids divided.

- **`assumption` — How much mail fraud and household pressure goes
  undetected?** Detected cases are a floor; no study measures the gap. If it
  is large, `mail-exception`'s secrecy argument becomes a fraud argument; if
  small, it is a cost to weigh against access. `mail-exception`, `mail-works`.
- **`tradeoff` — Does ballot secrecy matter as much as access?**
  `mail-exception`, `mail-works`.
- **`tradeoff` — Can mail-ballot checks be both strict and fair?** Strict
  signature review catches more ballots cast by others and rejects more valid
  ones; ID-number checks reject valid ballots too. `mail-exception`, `mail-works`, `signature-discretion`.
- **`prediction` — Would restricting mail voting raise the losing side's
  confidence, or lower it?** No study settles it (R7). `mail-exception`,
  `election-day-receipt`, `mail-works`.
