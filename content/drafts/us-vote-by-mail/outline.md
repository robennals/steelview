## Topic

- **Slug:** `us-vote-by-mail`
- **Title:** Voting by mail in the US
- **What is being argued about:** Whether voting by mail should be available
  to everyone, sent to everyone, or kept for people who cannot vote in
  person; how mail ballots should be checked (signatures, ID numbers,
  witnesses) and who may return them; whether ballots arriving after Election
  Day should count; and who decides. The March 2026 executive order's Postal
  Service rule is blocked for November; *Watson v. RNC* (June 2026) left
  receipt deadlines to states and Congress; the SAVE America Act would, per
  coverage, end universal mail voting.
- **Scope boundaries:** Mail and absentee ballots from application to count.
  **Election subversion is in scope**: fraud by campaigns and aligned
  groups, and manipulation of which ballots count by officials, are the
  dangers both sides argue about. ID at the polls is a
  [separate topic](../us-voter-id/outline.md). Excludes redistricting and
  campaign finance.

## Viewpoints

### `mail-exception` — Mail voting should be the exception, kept for voters who need it

- **Arguments**
  - The secret ballot was adopted to stop employers, party agents and
    families watching and buying votes (D56). A ballot filled in at a kitchen
    table loses that protection, even when nothing illegal happens: in
    Oregon surveys after 1998 about a third of mail voters completed their
    ballots with others present (D70). A widely cited 2023 Rasmussen–Heartland
    poll put the share who said someone else filled out their ballot, in part
    or in full, at 19% (D65); it is a mostly opt-in poll of about 325 mail
    voters whose method has been criticised, but no better survey has asked
    the question. A spouse who sees the vote can punish it.
  - Detected fraud is a floor, not a measure. A ballot marked by a household
    member with the voter's acquiescence, or under pressure, passes every
    signature check and leaves no record to prosecute, so low case counts
    cannot show that it is rare.
  - Trust matters as much as honesty. Losers have disputed the results of
    several recent US elections (D75), confidence in mail ballots is lower
    than in in-person ones and split by party (D19, D74), and a result the
    losing side rejects is destabilising whether or not any fraud occurred.
  - The elections overturned for fraud in modern America were absentee
    cases: East Chicago's 2003 mayoral primary, North Carolina's 9th district
    in 2018 (ballot harvesting forced a new election), and Paterson, New
    Jersey in 2020, where a court voided a mail election over ballot handling
    (D25, D51, D52). The Carter–Baker Commission called absentee ballots the
    largest source of potential fraud (D38).
  - Other democracies have pulled back: France abolished postal voting in
    1975, citing fraud (D61); British election courts found large-scale
    postal fraud in Birmingham in 2005 and Slough in 2013, and Britain
    tightened postal voting afterwards (D62).
- **Empirical questions**
  - What is the documented history of mail and absentee fraud, in the US and abroad?
    - *Context:* cases against mail ballots cast over the same years; the in-person record over the same years; what remedies followed each case.
  - Why did the secret ballot emerge, and what happened to vote-buying and intimidation after it?
    - *Context:* Australia 1856, Britain 1872, US states 1888 onward; research on turnout and corruption before and after.
  - How do other democracies handle remote voting, and why did those that restricted it do so?
    - *Context:* the full set (International IDEA), not only the restrictive ones; countries that expanded postal voting (Switzerland, Germany) and their experience.
  - How many mail voters complete their ballots with others present, or report someone else filling in or pressuring their ballot, by surveys of what quality?
    - *Context:* the Rasmussen–Heartland question wording, sample and panel, and critiques (D65); the Oregon surveys (D70, D71); ballot-secrecy research (D72); UK family-voting research (D73); the same measures for in-person voting.
  - How has confidence in vote counts changed, by party and voting method, and what happened after disputed results?
    - *Context:* Gallup and Pew series side by side (D18, D19, D74); each disputed outcome and its resolution (D75); the winner–loser gap in the US against other democracies (D41).
- **Concedes:** all-mail states have not shown higher detected fraud (D2,
  D13); fraud cases are few against ballots cast; the Paterson criminal case
  was dismissed in April 2026 (D52); Germany and Switzerland run large postal
  votes (D64), so restriction is not the democratic norm; no reliable survey
  has measured coercion (D65 is a mostly opt-in poll of about 325 mail
  voters whose question counts lawful help, and in Oregon 1% or fewer said
  they felt pressured, D70); confidence follows who won more than how votes
  were cast (D41); mail serves voters who cannot get to a polling place.
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; rules must mean what they say.

### `mail-works` — Voting by mail works, and the states that use it prove it

- **Arguments**
  - Colorado, Utah, Washington and Oregon vote mostly by mail, with high
    turnout and no higher rate of detected fraud than other states (D2, D13).
  - Mail leaves a paper trail and adds ballot tracking, signature checks and
    a chance to fix mistakes: more than 585,000 ballots entered the cure
    process in 2024 and over half were cured.
  - Mail serves rural, disabled, elderly and shift-working voters.
  - Rejection rates in all-mail states are no higher than elsewhere.
- **Empirical questions**
  - How has the share of votes cast by mail changed since 2000, and who uses it?
    - *Context:* by age, party, rurality and disability; the 2020 spike against the trend.
  - What is the effect of universal mail voting on turnout and on each party's vote share?
    - *Context:* effect sizes beside other reforms; studies before and after 2020.
  - How do rejection and cure rates compare between all-mail states and others?
    - *Context:* rejection reasons; the trend across EAVS years.
- **Concedes:** mail ballots are rejected more often than in-person ones
  (D1); the elections overturned for fraud in modern America were absentee
  cases (D25, D51, D52); new ID rules for mail ballots can spike rejections,
  as in Texas in 2022 (D16, D31); Democrats now use mail more than
  Republicans.
- **Principles:** an equal voice for every citizen.

### `signature-discretion` — Signature checks let officials throw out valid ballots on a judgement call

- **Arguments**
  - Standards for what counts as a match differ by state and county (D67).
  - Rejections fall unevenly: Washington's state auditor found signature
    rejection rates differed by race, age and county (D66), so the same
    ballot can count in one county and not the next.
  - Any discretionary veto over ballots is an opening for bias, or for an
    official who wants a particular result, even where none has been shown.
    The fix is notice and a chance to cure, bipartisan review, or objective
    checks such as ID numbers.
- **Empirical questions**
  - How many mail ballots are rejected for signature reasons, by state, county and voter group?
    - *Context:* the trend over EAVS years; variation between neighbouring counties with similar voters; rates where cure is required.
  - What standards, training and appeal rights govern signature matching, and what have courts said?
    - *Context:* states that replaced signatures with ID numbers or witnesses, and their rejection rates.
  - Has signature review ever been used to change a result?
    - *Context:* the wider record of officials manipulating which ballots count.
- **Concedes:** Washington's auditor found no bias by election workers and
  agreed with counties' decisions 98% of the time (D66); Washington requires
  training and Colorado bipartisan review (D67); no case of signature review
  changing a result was found (D69, search incomplete); signature checks are
  one of the few safeguards on a ballot cast out of sight; cure processes
  recover over half of flagged ballots (D1).
- **Principles:** an equal voice for every citizen; rules must mean what they say.

### `election-day-receipt` — Ballots should be in by Election Day, and counted fast

- **Arguments**
  - A ballot arriving after Election Day was not in the count when the
    election was held. About 15 states still count such ballots; Ohio, North
    Dakota and Utah ended that in 2025, and Kansas's change is blocked in
    court (D5).
  - Slow counts leave days in which the lead changes and suspicion grows;
    Florida counts millions of mail ballots by election night, so slowness is
    a choice.
- **Empirical questions**
  - Which states count late-arriving ballots, and how many arrive late?
    - *Context:* late ballots against total cast and against margins; military and overseas ballots separated; changes since 2020.
  - How long do states take to count, and what explains the differences?
    - *Context:* whether mail ballots can be processed before Election Day, beside mail share.
- **Probably concedes:** *Watson* held federal law does not require receipt
  by Election Day; late ballots are a small share; postal delays are not the
  voter's fault.
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; results the losers can accept.

### `states-decide` — Mail-voting rules belong to the states, and presidents have no say in them

- **Arguments**
  - The Elections Clause gives the president no role; courts have blocked the
    Postal Service rule under the March 2026 order.
  - Oregon's all-mail system and Mississippi's excuse-only system were each
    chosen by their own voters.
- **Empirical questions**
  - What federal bills and orders on mail voting are live, and what has happened to each?
  - How much do state mail-voting rules vary, and how has that changed since 2000?
- **Concedes:** Congress can set rules for federal elections, and *Watson*
  says it could set a receipt deadline; the Supreme Court let the March 2026
  order itself proceed, blocking only the Postal Service rule for November
  (D24, D46).
- **Principles:** decisions made closest to the people; democratic consent.

## Cross-cutting questions

- **Who has subverted American elections through absentee and mail ballots,
  and through control of which ballots count?**
  - *Fraud by campaigns and aligned groups:* NC-09 2018; Paterson 2020; East
    Chicago 2003; historical absentee fraud by machines.
  - *Manipulation by officials:* selective rejection or acceptance of
    ballots; refusals to certify; disputes over which ballots to count.
  - *Remedies:* the secret ballot, tracking, signature and ID checks,
    bipartisan review, audits — when each was adopted and what it changed.
  - *Context:* Britain's postal-fraud cases and reforms; France's 1975
    abolition; countries that run large postal votes without scandal.

## Crux candidates

Kind first; then the viewpoint ids divided.

- **`assumption` — How much mail fraud and household pressure goes
  undetected?** Detected cases are a floor; no study measures the gap.
  `mail-exception`, `mail-works`.
- **`tradeoff` — Does ballot secrecy matter as much as access?**
  `mail-exception`, `mail-works`.
- **`tradeoff` — Can mail-ballot checks be both strict and fair?** Strict
  signature review catches more ballots cast by others and rejects more valid
  ones. `mail-exception`, `mail-works`, `signature-discretion`.
- **`prediction` — Would restricting mail voting raise the losing side's
  confidence, or lower it?** No study settles it (R7). `mail-exception`,
  `election-day-receipt`, `mail-works`.
- **`assumption` — Is the bigger danger fraud by outsiders or manipulation
  by officials?** `mail-exception`, `signature-discretion`.
