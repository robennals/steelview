## Topic

- **Slug:** `us-voting-rules`
- **Title:** US voting rules
- **What is being argued about:** How Americans should be allowed to register
  and cast a ballot: whether to require photo ID and documentary proof of
  citizenship, how to check the voter rolls, how widely to allow voting by
  mail, and whether ballots arriving after Election Day should count — and
  whether Congress, the president or the states decide. The fights are live:
  the House has passed the SAVE America Act and the Senate has not; courts
  have blocked parts of two executive orders; *Watson v. RNC* (June 2026) left
  late-ballot deadlines to states and Congress.
- **Scope boundaries:** Rules for registering, casting and counting ballots,
  and who sets them. Excludes redistricting, campaign finance, the Electoral
  College, who certifies results and the risk of partisan election officials
  ("election subversion"), and the 2020 result itself except where it bears
  on mail-voting evidence. The subversion argument is real but is about who
  runs elections rather than the rules voters face; it deserves its own topic.

## Viewpoints

### 1. Voters should prove who they are and that they are citizens

- **Arguments**
  - Photo ID is ordinary in most democracies and in daily American life.
  - The Supreme Court upheld Indiana's ID law in *Crawford* (2008) on the
    state's interest in deterrence and public confidence, without requiring
    proof of impersonation; a safeguard need not wait for a scandal.
  - Non-citizens do reach the rolls, mostly through administrative error:
    Ohio referred 1,084 apparent non-citizen registrants in October 2025, 167
    of whom apparently voted. Checking citizenship at registration closes
    that gap.
  - Large majorities across parties and races support photo ID.
- **Empirical questions**
  - What ID do other democracies require to vote, and how do they issue it?
    - *Context:* whether those countries have national ID cards or automatic registration; the US states' own range of rules.
  - How many non-citizens have been found on the rolls and voting, by systematic audits that looked for them?
    - *Context:* the total registrants checked in each audit (the denominator); how many flags were later confirmed or reversed; how the figures compare with winning margins.
  - What share of Americans, by party and race, support photo ID and proof of citizenship?
    - *Context:* the same polls' support for access measures; how support has moved over time.
  - Does strict ID raise public confidence in elections?
    - *Context:* see viewpoint 7's confidence questions.
- **Probably concedes:** detected impersonation is rare; the best national
  study finds ID laws have no measurable effect on turnout either way, which
  also means no measurable effect on fraud.
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; rules must mean what they say.

### 2. These rules turn away eligible citizens to fix a problem that barely exists

- **Arguments**
  - Kansas's proof-of-citizenship law blocked 31,089 applicants between 2013
    and 2016, against at most 39 non-citizens found on its rolls in 19 years.
  - Texas's 2021 ID rules for mail ballots led to about 23,000 rejections in
    the March 2022 primary; Arizona's 2024 database error left about 218,000
    long-registered voters without proof of citizenship on file.
  - The burden falls hardest on young, new and Black registrants, as GAO
    found in Kansas and Tennessee — even where total turnout barely moves.
  - Database matching wrongly flags citizens: Texas reinstated about a fifth
    of the voters the federal SAVE system flagged.
- **Empirical questions**
  - How many registrations and ballots have been rejected under ID and proof-of-citizenship rules, and how many of those people were later shown to be eligible?
    - *Context:* rejection rates before the rule and in states without it; how many rejections were cured.
  - What happened to turnout, by group, after strict ID or proof-of-citizenship rules?
    - *Context:* the national studies (Cantoni & Pons) beside the state studies (GAO); whether campaigns' mobilisation offset the effect.
  - How many eligible citizens lack current photo ID or ready proof of citizenship?
    - *Context:* "no ID" separated from "outdated ID"; who ran each survey; the cost and travel time to obtain documents.
  - How accurate is citizenship matching against federal databases?
    - *Context:* each state's flags against confirmed non-citizens.
- **Probably concedes:** aggregate turnout effects of ID laws are small or
  nil; non-citizens have occasionally registered and voted.
- **Principles:** an equal voice for every citizen; priority to the worst off.

### 3. Ballots should be in by Election Day, and counted fast

- **Arguments**
  - A ballot that arrives after Election Day was not in the count when the
    election was held; 15 states still count such ballots, and Ohio, North
    Dakota, Utah and Kansas ended that in 2025.
  - Slow counts leave days in which the lead changes and suspicion grows.
    Florida counts millions of mail ballots by election night; slowness is a
    choice.
  - The clearest modern fraud case, North Carolina's 9th district in 2018,
    was absentee-ballot harvesting, and it forced a new election.
  - Mail ballots are rejected more often than in-person ones.
- **Empirical questions**
  - Which states count ballots arriving after Election Day, and how many arrive late?
    - *Context:* late ballots against the total cast and against margins; military and overseas ballots separated out; how the rules changed since 2020.
  - How long do states take to count, and what explains the differences?
    - *Context:* pre-processing rules (whether mail ballots can be opened before Election Day) beside mail share.
  - Where have proven fraud cases come from, by voting method?
    - *Context:* cases against ballots cast by each method; how the databases are compiled.
  - How often are mail ballots rejected, and why?
    - *Context:* in-person provisional rejections; first-time against repeat mail voters; how many were cured.
- **Probably concedes:** all-mail states have not shown higher detected
  fraud; *Watson* held federal law does not require receipt by Election Day.
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; results the losers can accept.

### 4. Voting by mail works, and the states that use it prove it

- **Arguments**
  - Colorado, Utah, Washington and Oregon vote mostly by mail, with high
    turnout and no fraud scandals.
  - Mail leaves a paper trail and adds signature checks, ballot tracking and a
    chance to fix mistakes: in 2024 more than 585,000 ballots entered the
    cure process and over half were cured.
  - Mail serves rural, disabled, elderly and shift-working voters.
  - Rejection rates in all-mail states are no higher than elsewhere.
- **Empirical questions**
  - How has the share of votes cast by mail changed since 2000, and who uses it?
    - *Context:* by age, party, rurality and disability; the 2020 spike against the trend.
  - What is the effect of universal mail voting on turnout and on each party's vote share?
    - *Context:* the studies that find small or no partisan effects, and the turnout effect's size against other reforms.
  - How do rejection and cure rates compare between all-mail states and others?
    - *Context:* rejection reasons; the trend across EAVS years.
- **Probably concedes:** mail ballots are rejected more often than in-person
  ones; counting takes longer where processing cannot start early; Democrats
  now use mail more than Republicans.
- **Principles:** an equal voice for every citizen.

### 5. Clean the rolls, rather than burdening voters at the door

- **Arguments**
  - Most ineligible registrations are stale entries — people who moved or
    died — and administrative mistakes, which ID at the polls does not catch.
  - Regular list maintenance, cross-state matching and checks against
    federal records find them before Election Day.
  - DHS's revamped SAVE system has checked more than 65 million voters in 26
    states.
- **Empirical questions**
  - How many registrations are inaccurate or out of date, and how many are removed each cycle?
    - *Context:* removals by reason; the share later reinstated; states' list-maintenance rules.
  - What has SAVE and other database matching found, and how often was it wrong?
    - *Context:* flags against confirmed cases; see viewpoint 2's accuracy question.
- **Probably concedes:** matching makes errors that fall on citizens; federal
  law (the NVRA) limits removals close to elections.
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; rules must mean what they say.

### 6. Election rules belong to the states, and presidents have no say in them

- **Arguments**
  - The Elections Clause gives states the first say and Congress the power to
    alter it; it gives the president none. Courts have blocked the
    proof-of-citizenship part of the March 2025 order and the Postal Service
    rule under the March 2026 order.
  - Fifty systems let states try rules and learn which work.
  - Federal mandates from either party override what each state's voters chose.
- **Empirical questions**
  - What federal laws, bills and executive orders on voting rules are live, and what has happened to each?
    - *Context:* earlier federal action (Voting Rights Act, NVRA 1993, HAVA 2002) and the Freedom to Vote Act.
  - How much do state rules vary, and have states copied rules that worked elsewhere?
    - *Context:* NCSL tables over time.
  - What have courts said about the limits of federal and presidential power here?
- **Probably concedes:** Congress can override states on congressional
  elections (*Arizona v. ITCA*, 2013); federal law already governs
  registration; national standards from both parties have broad public
  support.
- **Principles:** decisions made closest to the people; democratic consent.

### 7. Require ID and guarantee access, so both sides trust the result

- **Arguments**
  - The Carter–Baker Commission in 2005 proposed free photo ID together with
    measures to make registration easy; a bargain both sides can accept.
  - Pew found 83% support photo ID and 80% support two weeks of early voting:
    the combination is what the public wants.
  - Confidence in elections now depends on which party won. Rules each side
    chose would leave the loser with fewer grounds to reject the result.
- **Empirical questions**
  - What share of Americans support each rule, and how much do supporters of ID and of access overlap?
    - *Context:* the same questions over time; by party.
  - How has confidence in vote counts changed over time, by party, and does it track who won or what the rules are?
    - *Context:* Gallup and Pew series side by side, with their different wordings; the winner-loser gap in other democracies.
  - Has any state combined strict ID with broad access, and what happened to turnout and confidence there?
    - *Context:* Georgia's 2021 law and Indiana, against states that did one without the other.
- **Probably concedes:** confidence follows who won more than what the rules
  are; neither party's leadership has offered this trade recently.
- **Principles:** results the losers can accept; an equal voice for every citizen.

## Crux candidates

Drafted now so stage 3 gathers what they need; written properly in stage 5.

- **Should Congress set one national standard?** Both the SAVE America Act
  (viewpoint 1's supporters) and the Freedom to Vote Act (viewpoint 2's) are
  national mandates; viewpoint 6 rejects both. Divides 1, 2 and 6.
- **Is a rejected eligible voter a worse error than an ineligible vote
  counted?** Divides 1, 2 and 5.
- **Does a rule that does not move turnout still burden voters?** Divides 1
  and 2.

## Revision notes

What changed from draft 1, by critique point.

- **C1 (national standard)** — a crux candidate rather than a viewpoint.
  Both parties argue for national mandates with opposite content, so it
  divides existing viewpoints rather than standing as one. *Decision for the
  human.*
- **C2 (trade ID for access)** — now viewpoint 7, replacing draft 1's "both
  sides should trust the result", which was a meta-position.
- **C3 (clean the rolls)** — now viewpoint 5. Its opposite, that database
  matching wrongly flags citizens, is an argument in viewpoint 2.
- **C4 (election subversion)** — excluded in Scope, with the reason.
- **Viewpoint 3 bundling** — narrowed to receipt by Election Day and fast
  counting; in-person-only dropped, since few argue it at length now.
- **Weaker arguments** — applied: *Crawford* and administrative-error
  findings (1); documented rejections, the GAO distributional finding and the
  answer to Cantoni & Pons (2); NC-09 (3); curing and rejection parity, and
  "both parties use it" moved to a concession (4); the Elections Clause
  corrected, the argument now aimed at presidential power (6).
- **Values vs claims** — "decentralised is harder to hijack" dropped, as it
  has no measure; "only citizens may vote" treated as settled law, with the
  contested question being how often non-citizens register.
- **Missing principles** — written as shared principles:
  [an equal voice for every citizen](../../principles/an-equal-voice-for-every-citizen.md),
  [verifiably honest elections](../../principles/verifiably-honest-elections.md),
  [results the losers can accept](../../principles/results-the-losers-can-accept.md)
