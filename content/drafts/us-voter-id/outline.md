## Topic

- **Slug:** `us-voter-id`
- **Title:** Voter ID and citizenship checks in the US
- **What is being argued about:** Whether Americans should have to show photo
  ID to vote and documentary proof of citizenship to register, how voter
  rolls should be checked, and who sets those rules. The House has passed the
  SAVE America Act (national photo ID and proof of citizenship); the Senate
  has not. Courts blocked the proof-of-citizenship part of the March 2025
  executive order. The Supreme Court let DHS's revamped SAVE citizenship
  database be used again in September 2026.
- **Scope boundaries:** Identity and eligibility checks at registration and
  at the polls, roll maintenance, and who sets the rules. **Election
  subversion is in scope**: fraud by political machines and aligned groups,
  and manipulation of who may vote by officials themselves, are the dangers
  both sides say these rules guard against or create. Mail-ballot rules are
  a [separate topic](../us-vote-by-mail/outline.md) except where ID rules apply
  to mail ballots. Excludes redistricting and campaign finance.

## Viewpoints

### `prove-identity` — Voters should prove who they are and that they are citizens

- **Arguments**
  - Impersonation, repeat voting and voting in dead people's names were
    standard practice for American political machines — Tammany Hall, and
    Chicago, where the 1982 federal investigation produced 58 convictions by
    1985 (D48, D49). Fraud of that kind is the reason to check identity
    before a ballot is cast, not only prosecute afterwards.
  - Northern Ireland adopted photo ID after personation became widespread
    (D58); Britain (2023) and Canada (2007) now require ID too (D57, D59).
    The burden can be small: in Britain's 2024 general election, 0.08% of
    in-person voters were turned away for lack of ID (D57).
  - *Crawford* (2008) upheld Indiana's ID law on the state's interest in
    deterrence and confidence, without requiring proof of impersonation.
  - Non-citizens do reach the rolls, mostly by administrative error: Ohio
    referred 1,084 apparent non-citizen registrants in October 2025, 167 of
    whom apparently voted. Checking at registration closes that gap.
  - Large majorities across parties and races support photo ID.
- **Empirical questions**
  - What is the documented history of in-person and registration fraud in the US, and how did it end where it ended?
    - *Context:* the scale in its era (votes against margins); what reforms were adopted in response (registration, the secret ballot, ID) and in what order; prosecutions over time.
  - **How was documented fraud actually carried out**, case by case — voters impersonating others, repeat voting, padded registrations, election officials stuffing or miscounting ballots, absentee fraud, vote buying — and which of those methods a voter-ID check would have stopped?
    - *Context:* the method in each prosecuted case (Chicago 1982, Kansas City 1936, Illinois 1960, East Chicago 2003, NC-09 2018); the share of convictions by method; the widespread assumption that historical fraud was mostly impersonation at the polls, tested against the cases.
  - What ID do other democracies require, when and why did they adopt it, and what happened afterwards?
    - *Context:* whether they have national ID cards or automatic registration; the UK 2023 rollout evaluation (how many turned away); Northern Ireland's personation record before and after.
  - How many non-citizens have systematic audits found on the rolls and voting?
    - *Context:* registrants checked (the denominator); flags later confirmed or reversed; margins of close races.
  - What share of Americans, by party and race, support photo ID and proof of citizenship?
    - *Context:* the same polls' support for access measures; change over time.
- **Concedes:** detected impersonation today is rare, and Britain had one
  personation conviction in the year before its law (D57); the Chicago fraud
  was caught by prosecutors, not ID checks, and the modern cases (East
  Chicago, Paterson) were absentee fraud that polling-place ID would not
  reach (D48, D51, D52); the best national study finds ID laws do not
  measurably change turnout (D8), so the case rests on deterrence and
  confidence.
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; rules must mean what they say.

### `turned-away` — These rules turn away eligible citizens to fix a problem that barely exists

- **Arguments**
  - Kansas's proof-of-citizenship law blocked 31,089 applicants between 2013
    and 2016, against at most 39 non-citizens found on its rolls in 19 years.
  - Arizona's 2024 database error left about 218,000 long-registered voters
    without proof of citizenship on file.
  - When Texas began requiring an ID number on mail ballots, rejections rose
    to 12.4% in the March 2022 primary before falling to about 3.5% (D16,
    D31).
  - The burden falls hardest on young, new and Black registrants, as GAO
    found in Kansas and Tennessee, even where total turnout barely moves.
  - Officials have used eligibility rules to exclude voters before — poll
    taxes, literacy tests and selective purges (D53, D54) — so any rule that
    officials administer carries that risk.
  - Database matching wrongly flags citizens: about 2,000 of the 3,251 people
    Alabama flagged as non-citizens in 2024 were citizens (D30); Iowa
    confirmed 277 of 2,176 flags (D29).
- **Empirical questions**
  - How many registrations and ballots have been rejected under ID and citizenship rules, and how many of those people were eligible?
    - *Context:* rates before the rule and in states without it; how many were cured.
  - What happened to turnout, by group, after strict ID or proof-of-citizenship rules?
    - *Context:* national studies (Cantoni & Pons) beside state ones (GAO); whether mobilisation offset the effect.
  - How many eligible citizens lack current photo ID or ready proof of citizenship?
    - *Context:* "no ID" separated from "outdated ID"; who ran each survey; cost and travel time to obtain documents.
  - How have officials used eligibility rules to exclude eligible voters, historically and recently?
    - *Context:* Jim Crow rules and their effect on registration (the before-and-after of the Voting Rights Act); recent purge litigation and outcomes.
- **Concedes:** aggregate turnout effects of ID laws are small or nil (D8);
  non-citizens have occasionally registered and voted (D12, D28, D29); fraud
  by machines was real (D48–D50); Jim Crow tools were discretionary or wealth
  tests that a free, uniform ID is not, so how far that history applies is a
  judgement (crux).
- **Principles:** an equal voice for every citizen; priority to the worst off.

### `clean-rolls` — Clean the rolls, rather than burdening voters at the door

- **Arguments**
  - Most ineligible registrations are stale entries — people who moved or
    died — and administrative mistakes, which ID at the polls does not catch.
  - List maintenance, cross-state matching and checks against federal
    records find them before Election Day; DHS's SAVE system has checked more
    than 65 million voters in 26 states.
- **Empirical questions**
  - How many registrations are inaccurate or out of date, and how many are removed each cycle, for what reasons?
    - *Context:* removals over time; the share later reinstated; states' rules.
  - What has SAVE and other database matching found, and how often was it wrong?
    - *Context:* flags against confirmed cases, state by state.
- **Concedes:** matching makes errors that fall on citizens (D29, D30);
  federal law limits removals close to elections; aggressive purges have
  been used against eligible voters (D54). The only direct measure of how
  many registrations are inaccurate is Pew's 2012 study (D35), so the size
  of the problem is dated.
- **Principles:** verifiably honest elections; rules must mean what they say.

### `id-plus-access` — Require ID and guarantee access, so both sides trust the result

- **Arguments**
  - The Carter–Baker Commission in 2005 proposed free photo ID together with
    easy registration: a bargain each side can accept.
  - Pew found 83% support photo ID and 80% support two weeks of early voting.
  - Confidence in elections now depends on which party won; rules both sides
    chose leave the loser fewer grounds to reject the result.
- **Empirical questions**
  - How much do supporters of ID and of access measures overlap?
  - How has confidence in vote counts changed, by party, and does it track who won or what the rules are?
    - *Context:* Gallup and Pew series side by side; the winner–loser gap in other democracies.
  - Has any state combined strict ID with broad access, and what happened to turnout and confidence?
- **Concedes:** confidence follows who won more than the rules (D18–D20);
  the only state test of the combination is Georgia (D39), where mail
  rejections stayed near the national rate (D31) but confidence moved with
  the result; neither party's leadership has offered this trade recently.
- **Principles:** results the losers can accept; an equal voice for every citizen.

### `states-decide` — Election rules belong to the states, and presidents have no say in them

- **Arguments**
  - The Elections Clause gives states the first say and Congress the power to
    alter it; it gives the president none. Courts have blocked the
    citizenship part of the March 2025 order.
  - Fifty systems let each state's voters choose their own rules. (A value:
    the empirical form, that states learn which rules work, has adoption
    counts but nothing linking adoption to results, D47.)
- **Empirical questions**
  - What federal laws, bills and orders on ID and citizenship are live, and what has happened to each?
    - *Context:* earlier federal action (Voting Rights Act, NVRA, HAVA).
  - How much do state ID rules vary, and how has that changed since 2000?
- **Concedes:** Congress can override states for federal elections
  (*Arizona v. ITCA*, 2013); federal law already governs registration; the
  Supreme Court let the March 2026 order's citizenship lists proceed (D24,
  D46).
- **Principles:** decisions made closest to the people; democratic consent.

## Cross-cutting questions

- **Who has subverted American elections, how, and what stopped them?**
  Viewpoints 1, 2 and 3 all argue from this history, in opposite directions.
  - *Fraud by machines and aligned groups:* Tammany Hall; Chicago, including
    1960 and the 1982 investigation; Kansas City's Pendergast machine; East
    Chicago 2003.
  - *Exclusion by officials:* poll taxes, literacy tests and white primaries;
    selective purges; recent cases of officials refusing to certify.
  - *Remedies:* registration systems, the secret ballot, ID, the Voting
    Rights Act, paper trails and audits — when each was adopted and what it
    changed.
  - *Context:* comparable histories elsewhere (Britain's corrupt boroughs,
    Northern Ireland personation), and how each country's remedies differed.

## Crux candidates

Kind first; then the viewpoint ids divided.

- **`tradeoff` — Is a rejected eligible voter a worse error than an
  ineligible vote counted?** `prove-identity`, `turned-away`, `clean-rolls`.
- **`assumption` — How much impersonation and non-citizen voting goes
  undetected?** No data exists (3d). `prove-identity` assumes detected cases
  are a floor well below the true rate; `turned-away` assumes they are close
  to it.
- **`prediction` — Would a national ID rule change turnout, fraud or
  confidence?** `prove-identity`, `turned-away`, `id-plus-access`.
- **`assumption` — Is the bigger danger fraud by machines or exclusion by
  officials?** The two case histories have no common measure.
  `prove-identity`, `turned-away`.
- **`tradeoff` — Does a rule that does not change turnout still burden
  voters?** `prove-identity`, `turned-away`.
- **`priority` — Should Congress set one national standard?** Both the SAVE
  America Act and the Freedom to Vote Act are national mandates;
  `states-decide` rejects both.
