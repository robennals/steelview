## Topic

- **Slug:** `uk-immigration` (kept, so existing links still resolve)
- **Title:** UK immigration: how many, and on what terms
- **What is being argued about:** How many people should be allowed to move
  to Britain to work, study and join family; which routes they should come
  by; and what they must do to stay for good — including whether new
  settlement rules should apply to people already here.
  - Net migration rose to about 944,000 in the year to March 2023 and fell
    to about 171,000 in the year to December 2025 (D1, provisional). The
    year to June 2026 is due on 26 November 2026.
  - Care-worker visas fell by about 90% after the March 2024 ban on care
    workers' dependants; the route closed to new overseas recruits on 22
    July 2025 (D101, D118). The May 2025 white paper raised skill and
    salary thresholds (D118).
  - The government (Andy Burnham Prime Minister since 20 July 2026, Shabana
    Mahmood Home Secretary) has proposed "earned settlement": a ten-year
    baseline wait for indefinite leave to remain (ILR), longer for
    lower-paid routes, applying to people already here without ILR (CP
    1448, November 2025; D6). It is not yet in the Immigration Rules (D30).
  - Reform UK proposes abolishing ILR for non-EU migrants, including those
    who already hold it, in favour of renewable five-year visas, with no
    benefits for non-citizens and "net negative" migration; EU Settlement
    Scheme status is exempt (D13). The Conservatives propose a binding
    cap, ten years before ILR, and no benefits for non-citizens.
- **Scope boundaries:**
  - **In:** legal migration levels and routes (work, study, family,
    humanitarian schemes such as Ukraine and Hong Kong); settlement and
    citizenship rules; the economic, fiscal, housing and social effects
    people argue about; integration.
  - **Out, to [`uk-asylum`](../uk-asylum/outline.md):** asylum claims,
    Channel crossings, removals, the ECHR and refugee settlement. Asylum
    appears here only as part of the net migration total and as the
    "visa-to-asylum" route.
  - **Out entirely:** EU free movement and Brexit as such (history only);
    the Northern Ireland border; US or EU debates.

## Viewpoints

Ordered roughly from most to least restrictive. `economic-cost` argues from
money, housing and services; `cultural-cost` argues from culture and
cohesion. Holders often make both cases, but they stand or fall on
different evidence.

### `end-settlement` — Abolish permanent settlement, even for people who already have it, and aim for more people leaving than arriving

Reform UK's position (D13).

- **Arguments**
  - A settlement wave is coming: the Home Office expects about 1.6 million
    people (range 1.3–2.2m) to qualify for ILR in 2026–30, peaking at about
    450,000 in 2028 (D6).
  - Many of them came on routes that cost the public over a lifetime. The
    Migration Advisory Committee estimates each care worker at −£36,000 and
    each Health and Care dependant at −£67,000 (D55). Median earnings were
    £23,100 for care workers and £20,200 on family visas, and about half of
    Skilled Worker dependants and family entrants had no PAYE earnings
    (D60).
  - Settlement makes those costs permanent: ILR brings full access to
    benefits and social housing. ILR holders were already about 213,000
    people on Universal Credit in July 2025 (D14).
  - A renewable visa keeps everyone who works and contributes, and lets
    the country stop renewing those who do not. The Immigration Rules on
    settlement have been changed for people mid-route before (D21), and
    the guidance always said migrants "may be able to apply", not that they
    would (D26).
  - Citizenship, not residence, should be the line for welfare. (The
    comparison with countries that never grant permanence, such as the Gulf
    states and Singapore, has no report behind it; see candidate-data
    coverage note 1.)
  - Lower inflows alone leave the 2021–24 arrivals in place; only net
    emigration for a time reverses the scale of recent change. That is a
    choice voters are entitled to make (`democratic-consent-over-membership`).
- **Concedes**
  - Reform's £234bn saving is not a saving: it was a think tank's estimate
    of the cost of *settling* about 801,000 people, which its author has
    withdrawn; Reform's later £622bn is undiscounted, and £154bn on its own
    discounted basis (D18, D59).
  - The plan exempts EU Settlement Scheme holders, about 4.6 million grants
    against 0.6–0.8 million non-EU ILR holders (D9, D12, D13).
  - Nothing official is published on ILR holders' work, earnings or
    benefits by route (D14 is a proxy), so the claim about the people the
    plan would hit rests on visa-holder data.
  - Skilled Workers as a cohort are a large net gain, about +£47bn (D55).
  - The UK's years of negative net migration came from emigration, when
    arrivals were about 200,000 a year, not from removing residents (D1).
    Zero net migration gives the oldest population of any ONS variant
    (D69).
  - Revoking existing ILR is opposed 58% to 29%; ending it for new arrivals
    splits 44% to 43% (D25).
- **Principles:** `first-duty-to-its-own-people`;
  `democratic-consent-over-membership`.

### `economic-cost` — Mass immigration has left Britain poorer per head and short of homes: cut it hard and keep it down

The Conservative position, and the economic case made by Migration Watch
and the Centre for Policy Studies.

- **Arguments**
  - About 4.7 million people arrived in 2022–25, and net migration over
    2021–24 was about 2.5 million; migration accounted for about 98% of
    population growth to mid-2025 (D1, D3).
  - GDP per head was £40,546 in 2025 against £40,125 in 2019 (D50). The OBR warns that if capital does not keep pace with a rising
    population, GDP per person falls (D52).
  - The composition matters: care workers and Health and Care dependants
    are lifetime net costs (D55), and care and family-route earnings are
    low (D60). The 1.6 million due to settle in 2026–30 make that cost
    permanent unless the wait is lengthened first (D6).
  - Public spending did not rise with the population, so real departmental
    spending per person fell (D52).
  - Migration accounts for just over half of projected household growth:
    with zero net migration, the ONS projects about 1.3 million fewer
    households in England by 2032 (D65). Rent growth more than halved from
    its 2024 peak as net migration fell (D63).
  - The "tens of thousands" pledge was made in 2010, 2015 and 2017 and met
    only during Covid (D1, D10). Numbers have fallen through rule changes a
    future government could reverse, so only a binding cap set by
    Parliament makes the fall last.
- **Concedes**
  - Net migration has already fallen by about four-fifths under current
    policy (D1).
  - The OBR finds migration's effect on GDP per person small and of
    uncertain sign, and Canada and Germany were also flat in 2019–24
    (D51, D52).
  - Immigration explains about 4–6% of the real rise in house prices over
    three decades (D62); interest rates moved at the same time as rents,
    and rents are still rising (D63).
  - Pupil numbers in England are falling (D68) and GP numbers are up since
    2019 (D67), so pressure on schools and GPs is local, not national;
    real school funding per pupil is above its 2010–11 level after a dip.
  - In the OBR's scenarios, lower migration raises borrowing over five
    years (D52); every UK resident on average is a lifetime net cost
    (D55), so "net cost" needs that comparator.
- **Principles:** `first-duty-to-its-own-people`;
  `democratic-consent-over-membership`.

### `cultural-cost` — Large-scale immigration from very different cultures is eroding Britain's shared way of life

The cultural case (Rupert Lowe and Restore Britain at its sharpest; in
milder forms David Goodhart, Paul Collier, and many Reform and Conservative
politicians). It argues from who comes, not only how many.

- **Arguments**
  - **Grooming gangs.** In the places investigated, networks of mostly
    Pakistani-heritage men abused children for years: about 1,400 victims
    in Rotherham, more than 1,000 estimated in Telford (D151, D152); in
    Greater Manchester, 52–54% of group-exploitation suspects were Asian,
    against 21% of the population, and in West Yorkshire 35% against 16%
    (D150); about two-thirds of Operation Stovewood's Rotherham suspects
    were Pakistani, against 4% of the town (D150). The Jay, Telford and
    Casey reports found that fear of being thought racist stopped officials
    acting (D150–D152), and the state still does not record offenders'
    ethnicity for about two-thirds of suspects (D150). The Home Office's
    2020 paper, which found no link, was not supported by its own data
    (Casey; crime report).
  - **Outcomes differ by origin and do not always fade.** Countries that
    publish fiscal data by origin find immigrants from the Middle East,
    North Africa, Pakistan and Turkey net costs on average where others are
    not (Denmark, the Netherlands, Norway; D160–D162). In Denmark, the
    children of non-Western immigrants offend more than their parents (D172).
  - **Attitudes.** British Muslims are markedly more conservative than the
    public on homosexuality and gender roles (D167, D168).
  - **Islamist extremism** is the largest group of terrorist prisoners
    (57%; D174) and most of MI5's caseload (D176); since most are British
    nationals (D174), it is a failure of integration across generations.
  - **Integration takes time and language.** The foreign-born share of
    England and Wales rose from 4.3% in 1951 to 16.8% in 2021 (D177), and
    11% of 2011–21 arrivals do not speak English well or at all (D179).
- **Concedes**
  - Ethnicity is unrecorded for most suspects nationally, so neither "mostly
    Pakistani" nor "mostly white" can be stated for England and Wales; in
    West Yorkshire Asian and White suspects were about equal in number
    (D150). Most child sexual abuse is by friends, acquaintances and
    strangers outside any gang (D156).
  - The Danish figures are for one year (2019), not a lifetime, and the
    cost fell sharply in 2014–19 as more people found work; the Dutch study
    is by independent academics, not the government's planning bureau, and
    its method is disputed (D160, D161).
  - In the UK, non-citizens' share of convictions (13.1%) and prisoners
    (12.1%) is about their share of the population (D171).
  - 82% of terrorist prisoners are British nationals (D174), and Prevent
    referrals now run two to one extreme right-wing over Islamist (D175).
  - Residential segregation fell for every ethnic group from 1991 to 2021
    on the standard index (D180).
  - Children of most minority groups outperform White British pupils at
    GCSE and enter university at higher rates (D184, D185).
  - Trust: a small negative association with diversity in studies
    worldwide, none in London once deprivation is allowed for (D183);
    81% say people from different backgrounds get on well together in
    their area, in a range of 80–84% since 2013/14 (D182).
  - Across all child sexual abuse recorded in Greater Manchester, offenders'
    ethnicity is close to the population's; the over-representation is in
    group-based cases (crime report).
  - The attitude evidence is two polls, one commissioned by a partisan
    think tank, and one small academic survey (D167–D169).
- **Principles:** `continuity-of-a-shared-way-of-life`;
  `democratic-consent-over-membership`; `rules-must-mean-what-they-say`
  (equal enforcement of the law, whoever the offenders are).

### `contribute-and-earn` — Choose migrants for what they contribute, and make settlement something they earn

The government's position.

- **Arguments**
  - The fiscal gain is concentrated: the top 10% of Skilled Worker earners
    provide 39% of the route's fiscal gain, while care workers and Health
    and Care dependants are net costs (D55).
  - The care route grew far beyond plan, many with dependants (D100, D104,
    D118), and sponsor licence revocations reached a record number (D107).
  - Domestic workers have been replaced in care: British-held posts fell by
    130,000 since 2020/21 while overseas recruitment rose (D103).
  - Routes must be policed for their purpose: 34,999 asylum claims (41% of
    all claims) in the year to June 2026 came from people who arrived on
    visas, and Graduate-route claims more than doubled (D115).
  - After the route closed, care vacancies fell to 6.1%, the lowest in a
    decade, rather than rising (D103).
  - Settlement unlocks the welfare state, so it should follow
    contribution: hundreds of thousands would not yet meet a £12,570
    earnings test (D27, D29) and would have more time to do so.
- **Concedes**
  - Care-worker visas had already collapsed by about nine-tenths between
    late 2023 and early 2024, which the Home Office attributes to scrutiny
    of sponsors, before the dependant ban took effect; the 2025 closure was
    expected to cut only about 7,000 a year (D101, D118).
  - As a share of registered sponsors, revocations are back to their
    mid-2010s level rather than a record (D107).
  - Training substitution fails for nursing: UK-educated nurses joining
    the register rose 11.9% at the peak of overseas recruitment (D110), and
    medical school places are capped by government (D113). In care, low
    pay is an equally good explanation (D104).
  - The high-skilled routes are small (about 3.6% of work visas) and
    Innovator Founder has fallen by about 90% since 2019 (D100).
  - Visa-to-asylum claims are about 1% of Health and Care visas and 2–3%
    of study visas (D115, D100).
  - British-held care posts are still falling, so domestic recruitment has
    not replaced overseas staff (D103).
- **Principles:** `first-duty-to-its-own-people`;
  `contribution-earns-a-share` (proposed).

### `keep-our-word` — People who came lawfully on a path to settlement should be allowed to finish it

- **Arguments**
  - About 2.2 million people were on a five-year path to settlement at the
    end of 2024 (D27). When the Home Office applied tougher rules to highly
    skilled migrants already admitted in 2006, the High Court held it an
    abuse of power, because the guidance had promised them the terms in
    force when they joined (*HSMP Forum*, 2008; D21). Transitional
    protection is the usual practice, and the Home Affairs Committee
    recommends it here (D29).
  - For Hong Kong British Nationals (Overseas) the promise was explicit: a
    "historic and moral commitment" (D26).
  - Most main applicants work: at least 75% of Skilled Workers and 81% of
    Health and Care workers have PAYE earnings, with medians of £56,600 and
    £30,900 (D60). Settling them keeps that contribution.
  - Migrants already pay heavily for settlement: ILR costs £3,226 against a
    processing cost of £310 (D119).
  - The Home Affairs Committee found that a ten-year baseline would make
    the UK an outlier among comparable countries, where five years is
    usual (D29).
  - Insecure status does lasting harm: the Windrush scandal has paid
    £130.9m on 4,131 claims so far (D23).
  - Revoking existing ILR is opposed by 58% to 29% (D25).
- **Concedes**
  - The route guidance said migrants "may be able to apply" after five
    years; only the BN(O) statement was framed as a commitment (D26), and
    the Committee called mid-route changes "not unprecedented" (D29).
  - There is no evidence on whether longer waits deter skilled workers or
    prompt them to leave (D28).
  - About half of Skilled Worker dependants and family entrants show no
    PAYE earnings (D60); care workers and Health and Care dependants are
    lifetime net costs (D55).
  - Ending ILR for new arrivals splits the public evenly (D25).
- **Principles:** `rules-must-mean-what-they-say`.

### `open-and-build` — Immigration makes Britain richer; build the homes and services, rather than shut the door

- **Arguments**
  - Immigration is a small part of the housing problem: about 4–6% of the
    real rise in house prices over three decades (D62). Net migration has
    fallen by about four-fifths and rents are still rising (D63).
  - Migration slows ageing. With zero net migration there would be 641
    people over 65 for every 1,000 of working age by 2074, against 525 in
    the principal projection and 457 with high migration (D69).
  - In the OBR's scenarios, 200,000 fewer migrants a year raises borrowing
    by £13–20bn within five years (D52).
  - Skilled Workers are a large net gain to the public finances, about
    +£47bn for one year's cohort (D55), and earn well above the UK median
    (D60).
  - About a fifth of NHS hospital and community staff are non-UK nationals
    (D75); universities depend on international fees, and 36% of English
    providers were in deficit in 2024–25 (D71, D72).
  - Average wage effects on UK-born workers are small (D56, D77); migrants
    found a large share of high-growth start-ups (D78).
  - GDP per head stalled in countries with high and low migration alike
    (D51).
- **Concedes**
  - Rent growth more than halved as migration fell, and migration accounts
    for just over half of projected household growth (D63, D65).
  - Migration slows ageing but does not stop it (D69), and in the long run
    the fiscal effect of migration shrinks and reverses as migrants age (D54).
  - The OBR's March 2026 revision to lower migration cut potential output
    by only 0.25% (D53).
  - Low-paid routes with dependants are lifetime net costs (D55); wages at
    the bottom fall slightly (D56).
  - The start-up figure is one think tank's count of 100 companies (D78).
- **Principles:** `gains-from-open-exchange`.

### `blame-underinvestment` — Migrants are blamed for what underinvestment broke; end the hostile environment

The left case (the Greens under Zack Polanski, Labour rebels on the
Immigration and Asylum Bill, the Joint Council for the Welfare of
Immigrants).

- **Arguments**
  - Britain under-invested for decades: government investment was below
    the OECD median in every year from 1995 to 2024 (D126), and social-rent
    building fell after 2010–11 (D124). The OBR notes spending was not raised for a larger population
    (D52).
  - Migrants staff what remains: a fifth of NHS staff, and 150,000 care
    posts filled by visa holders (D75, D103).
  - Fees are a tax on migrants: ILR costs ten times its processing cost
    (D119).
  - "No recourse to public funds" leaves councils supporting destitute
    families: £94m in 2024/25 (D29, D122). No one counts how many people
    are subject to it (D123).
  - Tied visas enable exploitation: the care-route abuses (D105, D106) and
    record sponsor revocations (D107) are the result of tying workers to a
    single employer.
  - Hate crime spikes follow events and the mobilisation around them, as
    after the Southport murders (D128).
- **Concedes**
  - Day-to-day spending was not cut overall: NHS spending per head rose
    throughout, and school spending per pupil is back to its 2010 level
    (D125, D133).
  - The health surcharge is set to estimated average health use, not above
    it (D120).
  - Council housebuilding is at a record, from a low base (D124).
  - There is no current official count of people with no recourse to
    public funds (D123).
  - Exploitation's prevalence is not measured, and revocations are not all
    for abuse (D107).
  - No UK study isolates politicians' rhetoric as a cause of hate crime;
    spikes follow events (D129, D131).
  - Population growth does add to housing demand, if modestly (D62).
- **Principles:** `priority-to-the-worst-off`;
  `rules-must-mean-what-they-say`.

## Crux candidates

Kind first; then the viewpoint ids divided.

- **`prediction` — Does lower migration make Britain richer per head, or
  poorer?** `economic-cost`, `open-and-build`, `contribute-and-earn`. The
  OBR cannot sign the effect (D52); a different prediction about capital
  and composition.
- **`prediction` — If low-wage routes close, will wages and training rise,
  or will care go short?** `contribute-and-earn`, `open-and-build`,
  `blame-underinvestment`. Vacancies fell after closure but British-held
  posts kept falling (D103).
- **`assumption` — How much of the pressure on housing and services is
  population, and how much investment?** `economic-cost`, `open-and-build`,
  `blame-underinvestment`. No official split of household growth by
  migration exists (3d).
- **`assumption` — Do differences in outcomes and attitudes by origin
  persist across generations in Britain?** `cultural-cost`,
  `open-and-build`. Britain publishes no outcomes by origin and generation;
  Danish data say some persist (D172), British school data say many
  reverse (D184).
- **`tradeoff` — When faster growth and slower cultural change conflict,
  which comes first?** `cultural-cost`, `open-and-build`.
- **`tradeoff` — Do settlement terms bind future governments, or can
  Parliament revise them for people already here?** `end-settlement`,
  `keep-our-word`, `contribute-and-earn`, `economic-cost`.
- **`priority` — Should welfare and social housing follow citizenship, or
  lawful residence and contribution?** `end-settlement`,
  `contribute-and-earn`, `blame-underinvestment`.
- **`prediction` — Would lower numbers restore trust, or only a system
  that visibly works?** `economic-cost`, `contribute-and-earn`,
  `blame-underinvestment`. Shared with `uk-asylum`.

Dropped: "Is there a pace of change a community can absorb?" — the
research found no study linking the pace of migration to trust (C9), and
the trust evidence is about levels of diversity; the generation crux above
carries the argument `cultural-cost` actually makes.
