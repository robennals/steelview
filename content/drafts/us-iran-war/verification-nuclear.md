# Verification — nuclear and proliferation candidates

Checked on 2026-10-26 against primary sources fetched with curl. Every quote
below was copied from the raw fetched text (PDFs via `pdftotext`; HTML with
tags stripped). Line breaks from two-column PDF layouts have been rejoined;
nothing else was changed. "As of" is the date the figure describes, not the
date it was fetched.

**Summary of status:** 11 verified (C3, C4, C27, C28, C47, C60, C64, C66,
C72, C76, C80), 7 corrected (C1, C2, C5, C6, C65, C71, C75), 0 unverifiable,
0 dropped. Several sections have unverified sub-claims, flagged inline (C1's
Aug 2024 point, C3's "first since 2005", C6's "3–6 months", C66's 39%/66%,
C76's 2023 quote, C80's Fortna N).

**Corrections that change the outline's wording**
- **440.9 kg is an estimate, not a verified figure.** The IAEA *estimates*
  440.9 kg of 60% uranium as of 13 June 2025; it *verified* 432.9 kg of it.
  The outline's "last verified in June 2025" should say "the IAEA's last
  estimate, as of 13 June 2025 (432.9 kg of it verified)" (C1).
- **Syria's programme did not "end" in Bleek's coding.** Bleek codes Syria's
  *pursuit* as 2002–07, but Syria "returned to exploring in 2007" and the
  exploration column is open-ended (2000–). Iraq's pursuit ended in 1991
  after the Gulf War *plus* "an intensive verification regime" (C71).
- **"Strikes usually delay rather than end programmes" is Kreps & Fuhrmann
  (C28), not Bleek (C71).** The outline cites C71 for it.
- **The 2026 ATA says nothing either way about a weapons decision.** CRS:
  "the ATA does not address Iran's nuclear weapons activities." "US
  intelligence did not assess a decision" holds for the 2025 ATA only (C4, C5).
- **"Has not resumed enriching" is officials' statements, not a CRS finding.**
  CRS: "U.S. and Iranian officials have said that Iran has not resumed
  enriching uranium" (C5).
- **The DoD briefing said "hit", not "destroyed".** Caine: "nearly 80% of
  Iran's nuclear industrial base was hit" (C6).

### C27 — Braut-Hegghammer, "Revisiting Osirak" — verified

- "The attack had mixed effects: it triggered a covert nuclear weapons program
  that did not previously exist, while necessitating a more difficult and
  timeconsuming technical route to developing nuclear weapons. Notwithstanding
  gross inefficiencies in the ensuing program, a decade later Iraq stood on the
  threshold of a nuclear weapons capability. This case suggests that
  preventive attacks can increase the long-term proliferation risk posed by
  the targeted state." *International Security* 36:1 (Summer 2011), pp.
  101–132; Belfer abstract.
  https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/revisiting-osirak-preventive-attacks-and-nuclear-proliferation-risks
- Note: Bleek (C71) follows this reading and quotes her phases: "a drift
  toward a nuclear weapons option from 1975 to 1981 and a covert nuclear
  weapons program from 1981 to 1991".

### C28 — Kreps & Fuhrmann, "Attacking the Atom" — verified

- **16 attacks, 1942–2007; peacetime delay modest; wartime less successful.**
  "This article addresses these questions by conducting an analysis of 16
  attacks against nuclear facilities from 1942 to 2007. … There is evidence
  that the peacetime attacks delayed the target's nuclear program, although
  the size of this effect is rather modest. The wartime cases were less
  successful, as attacks often missed their targets either due to operational
  failure or limited intelligence on the location of critical targets."
  *Journal of Strategic Studies*, April 2011; Belfer abstract.
  https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/attacking-atom-does-bombing-nuclear-facilities-affect-proliferation
- **Extra, cuts against viewpoint 1/7:** "In our concluding section we show
  that many of the conditions that were conducive to past success are not
  present in the contemporary Iran case." And: "The historical cases that have
  successfully delayed proliferation are those when the attacking state struck
  well before a nuclear threat was imminent."
- This is the source for the outline's "strikes usually delay programmes
  rather than end them" — the outline attributes it to C71.

### C64 — Downes, foreign-imposed regime change — verified (one coverage caveat)

All from H-Diplo/RJISSF Jervis Forum Roundtable 17-6 on Downes, *Catastrophic
Success* (Cornell UP, 2021), posted 3 Oct 2025.
https://issforum.org/roundtables/jervis-forum-roundtable-17-6-on-downes-catastrophic-success
These are reviewers' summaries with book page numbers, not Downes's text.
- **120 FIRCs, 1816–2008.** "Marshaling a new dataset of 120 cases of FIRC
  (1816–2008)". (One reviewer writes "1816–2011"; three others say 2008.)
- **One-third civil war; "more than doubles".** "One-third of all FIRCs
  descend into civil war in the first post-intervention decade (94),
  especially in low-income countries, where FIRCs increase the risk of civil
  war by eight percentage points (100)." "Foreign-imposed regime change more
  than doubles the likelihood of civil war in the decade that follows (94-96)."
- **Leadership FIRC 4.5x; 47% removed; two-thirds for leadership.** "Civil
  war is four and a half times more likely to follow a leadership regime
  change than in other cases (98). And, whereas 47 percent of installed leaders
  have been forcibly removed from power, two-third of leaders installed
  through leadership regime change have lost power through irregular means
  (168)."
- **Disputes and votes.** "Regime change also appears to have no significant
  effect on the probability of a future militarized dispute between the target
  and intervener (251-2). And indeed, leadership regime changes more than
  double the risk of a dispute occurring (252)." "These states are no more
  likely to vote together in the United Nations General Assembly or to share
  alliance portfolios than other types of states (254-5)."
  - Extra: another reviewer: "the evidence suggests that FIRC actually
    increases the risk of militarized interstate dispute initiation by these
    states (248)" — disputes with anyone, not only the intervener.
- **Restoration best.** "Regime changes that seek to restore previously
  ousted rulers to power tend to have best outcomes".
- **Base-rate caveat (cuts slightly for viewpoint 2):** a reviewer notes "the
