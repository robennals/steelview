---
question: How much ballot secrecy is worth giving up to make voting easier?
kind: tradeoff
divides: [mail-exception, mail-works]
positions:
  - viewpoint: mail-exception
    holds: Very little. The booth protects the voter who most needs protecting, the one whose spouse, employer or carer wants a say, and a small turnout gain does not buy that back. Access should come from early voting, not from the kitchen table.
  - viewpoint: mail-works
    holds: Some, knowingly. A ballot filled in beside family is usually a choice, not coercion, and polling places are not perfectly private either. For a disabled or elderly voter the alternative to a mail ballot is often no vote at all.
---
Both viewpoints accept the facts. [Nearly a quarter of mail voters have someone else in the room](#fact-secret-ballot-and-postal-voting-abroad/mail-voters-often-not-alone), [few voters report pressure](#fact-secret-ballot-and-postal-voting-abroad/pressure-rarely-reported), and [mail voting raises turnout only slightly](#fact-mail-voting-use-and-effects/universal-mail-raised-turnout-modestly) while [mattering most to voters with disabilities](#fact-mail-voting-use-and-effects/disabled-voters-use-mail-more). Even [the courts and reviews that defend postal voting concede it gives up supervised secrecy](#fact-secret-ballot-and-postal-voting-abroad/secrecy-trade-off-conceded).

What divides them is the weight of two goods. [Verifiably honest elections](#principle-verifiably-honest-elections) ask for a vote no one can watch; [an equal voice for every citizen](#principle-an-equal-voice-for-every-citizen) asks for a vote everyone can use.

Evidence can sharpen the stakes but not settle them. A measure of coercion among mail voters would tell the first side how much it is protecting, and a measure of who stops voting when mail is restricted would tell the second how much it is giving up.
