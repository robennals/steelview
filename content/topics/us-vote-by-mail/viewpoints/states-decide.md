People should have a say in the rules that govern them, and each state's rules for voting are made closest to its own voters. The Constitution gives those rules to state lawmakers and to Congress. It gives the President no part beyond signing or vetoing bills.

### The President is trying to set mail-ballot rules himself

The President has signed two orders setting rules for mail ballots. One addresses when ballots must arrive. The other orders the Postal Service to carry ballots only for voters on a state's list. Neither came from a state legislature or from Congress. A court ruled that the first order's deadline rules went beyond his power, and a court blocked the Postal Service rule for the November 2026 election. While the Supreme Court let the second order go ahead, it did not decide whether the order is lawful.

### The Constitution gives election rules to state lawmakers and Congress

The Constitution's Elections Clause gives the "manner of holding elections" to each state's legislature, and lets Congress "make or alter such regulations". It names no part for the President beyond signing or vetoing bills. When the Supreme Court ruled on ballot deadlines in 2026, it said arguments over them belong to lawmakers, not courts. So a President who writes election rules by order takes a power the Constitution gave to others.

### Each state has chosen its own mail system, and changed it when it chose

States have moved both ways. The number offering mail voting to every voter rose from 30 in 2016 to 38 in 2020, fell to 35 in 2022, and is expected to reach 37 in 2026. Oregon's all-mail system came from its own voters, through a ballot initiative. Utah, after years of mailing every voter a ballot, is moving to mail ballots only on request. Three states ended their extra days for late ballots in 2025. Each state can change course when its voters want.

### Voters back the system their own state has chosen

Support for letting anyone vote by mail is highest in states that mail every voter a ballot and lowest in states that require an excuse, and that holds within each party. The country is also split by region. Most voters in the West used mail even before the pandemic, while in the South mail stayed under a quarter of votes even in 2020. Any one national rule, for more mail or for less, would override what one region or the other has chosen.

### Congress may set national rules, but has done so only where states cannot

Congress has strong power over federal elections, but on mail voting it has used it only for voters no single state can serve. Federal law makes every state let service members and Americans abroad vote by mail. Bills now in Congress would push mail voting in opposite directions, and national voting bills from both parties have failed in the Senate. If Congress set one rule for everyone, it could swing each time control of Congress changed.

### So each state's voters, through their lawmakers, should set their mail rules

Fifty states, each able to change its own rules, give voters more say than one national rule. Congress should step in only where states cannot serve voters, as it has for those serving or living abroad. And the President should leave election rules to the lawmakers the Constitution names.
