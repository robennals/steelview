Election Day should be the day the voting ends. Every ballot should be in officials' hands when the polls close, so the count can start at once and finish soon.

### A ballot should be in by the time the polls close

The fairest deadline is one every voter can know and every official can check: the ballot is in by the time polls close. The Supreme Court left the deadline to lawmakers, and named a reason they may choose Election Day: it "avoids the appearance of fraud from late-arriving ballots". A postmark is a weaker test. Since 2024 the Postal Service has stamped mail when it reaches a regional centre, not when it is posted, and that has hit states that count by postmark hardest.

### Late ballots keep the count open, and become something to fight over

Ballots that arrive up to about three weeks late still count in Washington, and fifteen states count some ballots that arrive after Election Day. Late ballots can end up in court. In 2020, Pennsylvania's top court extended the deadline by three days. The 10,097 ballots that came in late were kept apart on a Supreme Court justice's order, left out of the certified count, and fought over into 2021. They were far fewer than the winning margin. But in a race closer than the number of late ballots, the winner would have turned on how the courts ruled.

### Slow counts make people trust the result less

A long count costs trust. In a survey experiment, people told that a race took six days to call trusted the result less than people told nothing. Late ballots are one cause of slow counts: in 2020, counts were slower where states let mail ballots arrive after Election Day. While explaining the count in advance undid most of the drop, a count that ends sooner leaves less to explain.

### Most states already require ballots by Election Day, and more are returning to it

This is the common rule, not a harsh one. The number of states counting late ballots peaked at 21 in 2020 and is down to 15, against 13 in 2016. North Dakota, Ohio and Utah went back to an Election Day deadline in 2025. Nor is it one party's rule: the states still counting late ballots include Texas, Alaska and Mississippi as well as California and New York.

### Some voters miss the deadline, so states should help them meet it

While late arrival has caused between about one in eight and more than one in four rejected mail ballots in each election since 2016, the answer is to help voters meet the deadline, not to move it. States can mail ballots out early, offer drop boxes and say plainly when to post a ballot. A later deadline does not end late ballots either. Washington counts ballots that arrive weeks after Election Day, yet late rejections rose in its 2026 primary, as they did in Wisconsin and Michigan. And while late rejections were lowest in 2020, when the most states had grace periods, much else about voting changed that year.

### Big states show a count can finish within hours

A fast count is a choice states can make. In 2024, five of the seven swing states counted 95% of their vote within nine hours of polls closing, and Pennsylvania cut its count from 49 hours to 8. Texas and Florida each counted more than ten million votes in about a day. Arizona and Nevada still count slowly though they prepare ballots early, so late ballots are not the only cause of delay. But they are one cause a state controls.

### So every state should require ballots by poll close, and count fast

Every state should require mail ballots to arrive by the time polls close, and tell voters plainly when to post them. It should let officials prepare mail ballots before Election Day, which speeds the first hours of the count, and staff the count to finish within a day or two. Then the result is known while voters are still watching, and no late ballots are left to fight over.
