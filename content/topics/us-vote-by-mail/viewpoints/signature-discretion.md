Every citizen should have an equal voice. A mail ballot should not be thrown out because an election worker thinks a signature looks wrong, without the voter being told and given a chance to fix it.

### Signature checks now reject more mail ballots than anything else

A signature that an official judges not to match is now the most common reason given for rejecting a mail ballot. In 2024 it caused two in five rejections, more than twice as many as late arrival. The true share is probably higher, since some states file signature rejections under "other". So an official's judgement of a signature now decides more lost ballots than any other reason.

### The same check gives very different results from county to county

Matching signatures is a judgement call. In Washington in 2020, county rejection rates ran from 1.5% to under a tenth of a percent. The state auditor called matching "inherently subjective", and its own reviewers disagreed about many signatures. After controls, two counties were 18 times as likely as another to reject a ballot for a mismatch. Counties that rejected fewer ballots accepted less certain matches. While the auditor found no bias in single decisions, a rule that honest officials apply this differently gives voters unequal chances.

### Signature checks reject young, first-time and Black voters far more often

The lost ballots are not spread evenly. In Washington, Black voters' ballots were rejected at four times white voters' rate, counting unsigned envelopes too. The youngest voters were rejected most, and that gap held for mismatches alone. First-time voters were rejected at more than five times the rate of voters who had voted before. Studies in Florida found the same for young and inexperienced voters. A check that falls this unevenly does not give every citizen an equal voice.

### Telling voters of a problem saves most of the ballots it flags

Most flagged ballots belong to real voters, who can show it if asked. Where states let voters fix a problem, more than half the ballots set aside were fixed and counted. Without that step, all of them would have been rejected. Yet a federal court found that New Hampshire voters were not told their ballot had been rejected, and that the officials checking had no handwriting training or standards. While a federal appeals court let Texas keep rejecting ballots without notice, citing the risk of fraud, that was an early ruling, not a final one.

### The answer is a check that needs no judgement, not no check

Dropping checks is not the answer: in Cobb County, Georgia, an envelope signed by the voter's spouse passed the signature check. Georgia replaced signature matching with an ID number in 2021, and its rejection rate has stayed below its 2016 and 2018 levels. Design still matters. Texas's rate rose when it added an ID number on top of signatures, and many voters missed the new field on the envelope. Britain pairs the signature with something that needs no judgement, the voter's date of birth.

### So voters must be told of a problem and allowed to fix it

Every state that compares signatures should tell voters of a problem in time to fix it, as 35 states and DC must already. It should train its staff, and have two reviewers from different parties check every mismatch, as Colorado does. Pennsylvania's top court has already ruled that its election law does not allow rejecting ballots because signatures do not match. And states should move toward checks that need no judgement, so that whether a ballot counts depends on what the voter did, not on who checked it.
