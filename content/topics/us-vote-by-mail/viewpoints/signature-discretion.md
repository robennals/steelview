Every citizen should have an equal voice. A mail ballot should not be thrown out because an election worker thinks a signature looks wrong, without the voter being told and given a chance to fix it.

### Signature checks now reject more mail ballots than anything else

A signature that an official judges not to match is now the most common reason given for rejecting a mail ballot. In 2024 it caused two in five rejections, more than twice as many as late arrival. Some states also file signature rejections under "other", which this share leaves out. So an official's judgement of a signature now decides more lost ballots than any other reason.

### The same check gives very different results from county to county

Matching signatures is a judgement call. In Washington in 2020, county rates of rejection for any reason but lateness ran from 1.5% to under a tenth of a percent. The state auditor called matching "inherently subjective", and its own reviewers disagreed about many signatures. After controls, two counties were 18 times as likely as another to reject a ballot for a mismatch. Counties that rejected fewer ballots accepted less certain matches. While the auditor found no bias in single decisions, a rule that honest officials apply this differently gives voters unequal chances.

### Mail-ballot checks reject young, first-time and Black voters far more often

The lost ballots are not spread evenly. In Washington, Black voters' mail ballots were rejected at four times white voters' rate, counting unsigned envelopes too. The youngest voters were rejected most, and that gap held for mismatches alone. First-time voters were rejected at more than five times the rate of voters who had voted before. Studies in Florida found the same for young and inexperienced voters. A check that falls this unevenly does not give every citizen an equal voice.

### Telling voters of a problem saves most of the ballots it flags

Most flagged ballots belong to real voters, who can show it if asked. Where states let voters fix a problem, more than half the ballots set aside were fixed and counted. Without that step, all of them would have been rejected. Yet a federal court found that New Hampshire voters were not told their ballot had been rejected, and that the officials checking had no handwriting training or standards. While a federal appeals court let Texas keep rejecting ballots without notice, citing the risk of fraud, that was an early ruling, not a final one.

### The answer is a better check, not none

Signature checks miss things too: in Cobb County, Georgia, an envelope the voter's spouse signed by mistake passed the check. So the answer is a better check, not none, and its design matters. Georgia replaced signature matching with an ID number in 2021. Its rejection rate has been roughly half its 2018 level since, though it was above the national rate in 2024, and the largest group of county officials surveyed said it had not cut rejections. Texas's rate rose when it added an ID number on top of signatures, as many voters missed the new field on the envelope. Britain pairs the signature with something that needs no judgement, the voter's date of birth.

### So voters must be told of a problem and allowed to fix it

Every state that compares signatures should tell voters of a problem in time to fix it, as 35 states and DC must already. It should train its staff, and have two reviewers from different parties check every mismatch, as Colorado does. And states should move toward checks that need no judgement, so that whether a ballot counts depends on what the voter did, not on who checked it.
