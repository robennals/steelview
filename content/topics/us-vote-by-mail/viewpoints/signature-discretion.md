Mail ballots should not be thrown out just because a signature looks wrong to an official.

### Matching signatures is a judgement call, yet it is the top reason ballots are rejected

A signature that does not match is now the most common reason given for rejecting a mail ballot. Yet Washington's state auditor called signature matching "inherently subjective", and its own reviewers disagreed about many signatures. Many flagged ballots come from the real voter: where voters are told of a problem, more than half the ballots set aside are corrected and counted. Where they are not told, those ballots are simply lost. And the check can miss a wrong signature: in Cobb County, Georgia, an envelope signed by the voter's spouse passed.

### The same rule gives very different results from county to county

In Washington, the county a ballot was cast in mattered more than anything else the auditor measured. For mismatches alone, two counties were 18 times as likely as another to reject a ballot. That falls short of an equal voice for every citizen.

While the auditor found no sign of bias in individual decisions, honest judgement still differs. Counties that rejected fewer ballots accepted less certain matches. A standard that honest people apply this differently is not a standard. Officials have tampered with counts through absentee ballots before. While no case was found of signature checks used to change a result, rules should bind officials before any abuse, not after.

### Signature checks reject young, first-time and Black voters far more often

In Washington, Black voters' ballots were rejected at four times white voters' rate, counting unsigned envelopes too. The youngest voters were rejected most, and that gap held for mismatches alone. First-time voters were rejected far more than others. Studies in Florida found the same for young and inexperienced voters. Every citizen should have an equal voice, and a check that falls this unevenly does not give it.

### Courts have found signature rejections unfair, or not allowed by law

A federal court found that New Hampshire voters were not told their ballot had been rejected, and that the officials checking had no handwriting training or standards. Pennsylvania's top court ruled that its election law does not allow rejecting ballots because signatures do not match.

While a federal appeals court let Texas go on rejecting ballots without notice, citing the risk of fraud, that was a short-term order, not a final ruling.

### The answer is a better check, not no check

Dropping checks altogether is not the answer. Texas's rejections rose after it added an ID-number check, while Georgia's stayed below its 2016 and 2018 levels after it swapped signatures for an ID number. So the design of a check matters.

### So voters must be told of a problem and allowed to fix it

Where voters can fix a problem, more than half the ballots set aside were corrected and counted. So every state that compares signatures should let voters fix problems, as 35 states and DC must already. States should also train staff and have two reviewers from different parties check every mismatch, as Colorado does, and pair the signature with a fact that needs no judgement, as Britain does with the date of birth.
