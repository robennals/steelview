Figures are for November general elections, from the federal Election Administration and Voting Survey (EAVS) unless stated. A rejected ballot is a returned ballot that was not counted because it failed a check: it arrived late, was unsigned, its signature did not match, or similar.

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-rejection-rate}

### About one returned mail ballot in a hundred is rejected {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--rejection-about-one-percent}

Since 2012 the national rate has stayed between 0.8% and 1.5% of returned ballots. [10](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-10) [6](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-6) [5](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-5) Even though twice as many mail ballots were sent out in 2020 as four years earlier, the EAC found no significant change from 2016 to 2020 or across the three elections from 2020 to 2024. [6](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-6) [1](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-1)

### In 2024 that was 584,463 ballots, or 0.34% of all votes cast {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--rejections-against-all-votes}

The same rejections look large as a count and small against all votes cast, since most voters did not vote by mail. [1](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-1) [11](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-11) Which denominator is quoted changes the impression, so both are given here.

## Subtleties {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--subtleties-rejection-rate}

### In-person votes have no matching rejection rate {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--no-in-person-equivalent}

A ballot cast at a polling place is checked before the voter votes, not afterwards, so the survey has no in-person rate to set beside these. Its nearest counterpart, provisional ballots, is cast only when a voter's registration or eligibility is already in doubt, so it is a different group. [1](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-1)

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-rejection-reasons}

### Mismatched signatures are now the most common listed reason {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--signatures-largest-reason}

A non-matching signature was the most common listed reason in four of the five elections from 2016 to 2024, and reached 40.7% of rejections in 2024. [8](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-8) [6](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-6) [5](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-5) [2](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-2) Late arrival led in 2012 and 2014 and again in 2018. [10](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-10) [9](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-9) [7](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-7)

### Missing signatures have become a smaller share {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--missing-signatures-smaller-share}

Envelopes with no signature were about a fifth of rejections in 2016 and about a tenth in 2022 and 2024. [8](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-8) [5](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-5) [2](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-2)

### Ballots arriving after the deadline remain a leading reason {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--late-arrival-a-leading-reason}

Late arrival was the third-largest reason in 2024, behind signatures and "other". [2](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-2) How many late ballots states count depends on their receipt deadlines, which a separate report covers.

## Subtleties {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--subtleties-rejection-reasons}

### Part of the swing in the signature share is how states report {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--signature-share-reporting}

Each reason's share is worked out only over states that reported it, so shares do not add to 100% and can move when different states report. [2](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-2)

Much of the remainder sits in "other". In 2022 about a quarter of "other" rejections were "All Signature Issues", and in 2024 Oregon put almost all its rejections under "other". [5](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-5) [2](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-2) The true signature share is probably higher than charted in some years, by an unknown amount.

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-tx-ga-rate}

### Texas's rejections rose under its ID-number check and stayed above the national rate {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--texas-rose-under-id-number}

Texas's line rises after SB 1 required an ID or Social Security number on the envelope, and has stayed above both its 2020 rate and the national line. [13](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-13) [14](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-14) In its first primary under the rule, many voters missed the new field on the envelope. [12](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-12)

### Georgia's rate fell after it replaced signature matching with an ID number {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--georgia-fell-after-sb202}

Georgia's line, high under signature matching in 2016 and 2018, has sat near the national line since SB 202. [39](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-39) [7](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-7) [15](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-15) [16](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-16) Those earlier rates count every reason, not just signatures. In a survey of county election offices, a plurality said the new check had not reduced rejections. [17](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-17)

### An ID-number check is not a rejection-free alternative {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--id-number-checks-also-reject}

The two states moved in opposite directions under similar-sounding rules: Texas added a number check, Georgia used one to replace signature matching. [12](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-12) [17](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-17) So what a number check does to rejections depends on its design and on what it replaces.

## Subtleties {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--subtleties-tx-ga-rate}

### Texas's 2020 rate was its low point {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--texas-2020-low-point}

Texas rejected about twice the 2020 share in 2016 and 2018, before SB 1. [7](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-7) [6](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-6) A comparison with 2020 alone makes the rule's effect look larger than a comparison with the earlier years. Texas officials credited redesigned envelopes and voter education for the fall after the 2022 primary. [12](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-12)

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-reasons-2024}

### One rejected ballot in nine came from a voter who had already voted {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--already-voted-another-ballot}

In 2024, 11.1% of rejected mail ballots were from voters who had already cast another ballot that was accepted. [2](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-2) The check stops a second ballot being counted; the survey does not say whether the second ballot was a mistake or an attempt to vote twice.

### Other envelope requirements account for smaller shares {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--other-requirement-rejections}

A missing witness signature was the next-largest specific reason, at 5.6%, and each remaining requirement accounted for less. [2](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-2) None of the survey's reasons is fraud: every category is a failed procedural check.

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-states-2024}

### State rates ranged from 0.2% to 8.8% {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--state-rates-range-widely}

Arkansas, Delaware and Oklahoma rejected the largest shares in 2024; Vermont, Maine and Indiana the smallest. [4](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-4) Where a voter lives makes a large difference to the chance their ballot is not counted.

### All-mail states were not significantly different from the rest {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--all-mail-states-not-different}

The EAC found the counted and rejected shares "not significantly different" between states that held all-mail elections and those that did not. [1](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-1) The highlighted all-mail states sit throughout the middle and lower part of the chart. [4](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-4) [31](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-31)

### The highest rates were not all in signature-matching states {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--highest-rates-other-rules}

Delaware checks only that the envelope is signed, Arkansas also requires a witness, and Oklahoma a notary; Texas checks an ID number as well as signatures. [28](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-28) [4](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-4) High rejection is therefore not a feature of signature matching alone. State rates by reason are not charted here.

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-washington-race}

### In Washington, Black voters' ballots were rejected at four times white voters' rate {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--washington-race-gaps}

Every group shown was rejected more often than white voters, and the gap was statistically significant for all but Native Americans. After controlling for other factors, the auditor estimated Black voters were twice as likely as white voters to have a ballot rejected. [19](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-19)

### The racial gaps include unsigned ballots, not just mismatches {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--race-gaps-not-mismatch-only}

These rates cover every rejection except late ballots, and almost all such rejections are for a mismatched or missing signature. [18](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-18) The audit reports the age gap for mismatches alone, but not the racial gaps. [19](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-19)

### Men and voters using non-English ballots were also rejected more {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--men-and-non-english-ballots}

Men were 42 percent more likely than women to have a ballot rejected. In King County, voters casting non-English ballots were 47 percent more likely to be rejected than those casting English ones. [20](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-20)

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-washington-age}

### Young and first-time voters were rejected most {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--washington-age-gaps}

After controls, the youngest voters were almost three times as likely as the oldest to be rejected, and this held for mismatched signatures alone. [19](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-19) First-time voters were rejected at more than five times the rate of voters who had voted before. [20](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-20)

### Florida studies found the same pattern {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--florida-young-and-inexperienced}

In Florida's 2018 election, younger voters and voters needing assistance were "disproportionately likely" to have mail ballots rejected, with "significant variation" across counties. [26](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-26) Inexperienced mail voters were about three times as likely to be rejected in 2016 and 2018. [27](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-27) Both come from the authors' summaries; the full papers were not read.

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-washington-counties}

### The county a ballot was cast in mattered more than anything else {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--county-strongest-predictor}

After controlling for age, race, education and income, ballots in some counties were four to seven times as likely as in others to be rejected for a mismatched or missing signature. For mismatches alone, two counties were 18 times as likely as another. [21](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-21)

### Low-rejection counties accepted less conclusive signatures {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--low-rejection-counties-accept-more}

Counties with lower rejection rates were more likely to accept less conclusive signature matches, which could explain part of the difference. [22](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-22) Some of the gap is a choice about how much doubt to allow, not only error.

### Low-rejection counties also cured more ballots {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--low-rejection-counties-cure-more}

Among the ten audited counties, those with lower rejection rates got 65 percent of flagged ballots cured, against 50 percent in the others. [25](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-25)

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-cure-2024}

### More than half the ballots set aside for curing were cured {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--more-than-half-cured}

In states that reported curing, more than half the ballots flagged for a fixable problem were corrected by voters and counted. [3](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-3) Without curing, those ballots would have been rejected.

### Not every state cures, or reports it {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--cure-coverage}

The EAC counted 43 states that let voters cure in 2024, but twelve of them could not report how many ballots entered curing, including Georgia, Oregon and Pennsylvania. [3](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-3) The national figures cover only the states that reported.

### Texas sent a larger share of ballots into curing {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--texas-cure-share}

In 2024 Texas sent 5.8% of its mail ballots into curing, against 1.5% nationally. [14](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-14) [3](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-3)

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-state-rules}

### States check mail ballots in very different ways {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--checks-differ-by-state}

Most states compare signatures, but nine and DC check only that the envelope is signed, and six ask for an ID number. [28](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-28) The groups overlap: Ohio and Texas compare signatures and also check a number. Utah will drop signature matching for ID digits in 2029. [28](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-28)

### Some states require training and bipartisan review {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--training-and-bipartisan-review}

Washington requires everyone who checks signatures to be trained on statewide standards, and Colorado has two more judges, of different parties, review any mismatch. [29](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-29) All ten Washington counties the auditor visited had trained their staff. [25](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-25)

### Most states must let voters fix signature problems {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--most-states-cure}

Thirty-five states and DC require officials to tell voters of a missing signature or a discrepancy and give them a chance to correct it. [30](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-30) The count includes states that do not compare signatures, which can only be curing missing ones.

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-washington-recheck}

### The auditor agreed with almost all county decisions {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--auditor-agreed-with-counties}

Re-checking a random sample, the auditor agreed with counties on more than 98 percent of signatures. [22](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-22) Most errors that remain are matters of degree: lower-rejection counties accepted less conclusive signatures.

### The audit found no evidence of bias in individual decisions {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--no-evidence-of-bias}

In the auditor's words: "We found no evidence of bias in those decisions. While that is good news, it also means we are not able to explain what causes rejection rates to vary for these groups of voters." [24](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-24)

### Trained reviewers, the auditors included, disagreed about matches {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--reviewers-disagree}

The audit calls matching "inherently subjective". It found that "even experienced reviewers can come to different conclusions", that its own team disagreed on many signatures, and that counties read the state's criteria differently. [23](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-23)

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-courts}

### A federal court required notice before a mismatch rejection in New Hampshire {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--new-hampshire-due-process}

The court found that voters were not told their ballot had been rejected and moderators had no handwriting training or standards. [32](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-32) The court noted that 275 of 78,430 absentee ballots there were rejected for a mismatch in 2016. [32](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-32)

### The Fifth Circuit let Texas keep rejecting without notice, citing fraud risk {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--fifth-circuit-fraud-risk}

The majority held Texas's interest in stopping fraud "far outweighs" the burden, and quoted an earlier ruling that fraud risk is "much greater in the mail-in ballot context than with in-person voting". [33](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-33) Its view that voting by mail carries no due-process interest was a prediction at the stay stage ("likely to show").

### A concurring judge pointed to thousands of perceived mismatches {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--higginbotham-perceived-mismatches}

Judge Higginbotham, who concurred "only in the decision to stay", wrote that such "small" differences "have the potential to decide both local and national elections". [34](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-34) The 5,000 figure is in his separate opinion, not the majority's.

### Pennsylvania's top court barred rejection on signature comparison {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--pennsylvania-no-signature-rejection}

The ruling interpreted the state's own election law, not the Constitution. Pennsylvania is now listed among states that do not compare signatures. [35](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-35) [28](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-28)

### A losing candidate's claim about signature checks failed {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--lake-signature-claim-failed}

Kari Lake, who lost Arizona's 2022 governor's race by about 17,000 votes, asked the courts to install her or order a new election, claiming signatures had not been checked as the law required. The trial court found she had not proved it. [36](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-36)

## Observations {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--observations-cobb-audit}

### Cobb County's 2020 audit found no fraudulent absentee ballots {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--cobb-no-fraudulent-ballots}

Georgia's Secretary of State and the state Bureau of Investigation re-examined 15,118 envelopes, about a tenth of the county's absentee ballots, and found "no fraudulent absentee ballots". [37](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-37) Georgia used signature matching in 2020; SB 202 replaced it the next year.

### A ballot signed by the voter's spouse had passed the county's check {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--cobb-spouse-signature-passed}

The audit found two envelopes that should have been flagged. One was "mistakenly signed by the elector's spouse"; in both cases the voter had filled in the ballot. [37](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-37) So a signature that was not the voter's got through, which shows a check can miss one.

### The audit sampled only accepted ballots {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--cobb-accepted-ballots-only}

The envelopes came from boxes of "accepted" ballots. [37](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-37) The audit could find wrongly accepted ballots, but not valid ballots wrongly rejected, and finding no fraud in a sample shows little about how many forged signatures checks miss.

### No case was found of signature review being used to change a result {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--no-case-signature-review-changed-result}

The sources gathered for this report contain no case of officials using signature review to change an election result. The one attempt found to overturn a result over signature checks, Arizona's 2022 governor's race, failed in court. [36](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-36) The search was not exhaustive; see Data still needed.

## Subtleties {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--subtleties}

### A rejected ballot is not a fraudulent ballot, and a counted one is not proven genuine {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--rejected-is-not-fraud}

Rejection records a failed procedural check, such as lateness or a signature that does not match, not a finding about who filled in the ballot. [2](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-2) Detected problems are also a floor: Cobb's spouse-signed envelope shows that checks miss some. [37](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-37)

### Washington's rates are not comparable with the national ones {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--washington-excludes-late}

Washington's 0.72 percent excludes late ballots; the EAVS rates include them. [18](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-18) Washington is also one state in one year, the only state with a published audit of this kind found for this report.

## About this data {#mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks--about}

**National series.** Each point is the rate the EAC printed in that cycle's report, as a share of ballots returned (for 2012 and 2014, "submitted for counting"). The EAC calculates national rates only over states that reported both numbers, so the rates are not simply the national totals divided.

**Reasons.** Each report's own reasons table is used. Categories changed: "Lack of valid signature" (2012, 2014) is drawn as a missing signature, and the signature reason became "non-matching or incomplete" in 2022. These are marked as method changes on the chart.

**Texas and Georgia.** 2016 rates are calculated from the counts in the 2016 overview table, because its percentage column divides by ballots sent out. The 2024 state points are 100 minus the counted share in the state data briefs, and match the report's own "not counted" column.

**Cure rate.** Dividing the published totals, 317,191 cured of 585,457 set aside, gives 54.2%. [3](#source-mail-ballot-rejection-and-signature-checks-3) The EAC prints 54.3%, because it divides state by state and leaves out states missing either number; New Jersey, for example, reported cured ballots only. The prose says "more than half", which both support.

**Washington.** The auditor estimated each voter's race from surname and address (a method called BISG), because Washington does not record race, and used a regression model to compare groups and counties with other factors held constant. Late ballots were excluded because county data on them were inconsistent.

**Florida.** Baringer, Herron and Smith's full paper could not be fetched (the journal blocks automated access and the draft link is dead), so only its abstract is used.

**State rules.** The verification and cure lists are NCSL's tables as updated on 26 October and 31 August 2026. They change often, and the 3 November 2026 election may be run under rules that differ in detail.
