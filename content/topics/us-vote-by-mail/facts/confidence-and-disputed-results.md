      Prior research demonstrates voters’ confidence in elections depends on
      whether their preferred candidate wins, but does agreement with the
      electoral rules being used also matter? Leveraging growing procedural
      polarization in election laws, public opinion data from 2008 to 2024,
      and a large-scale survey experiment conducted before the 2024 election,
      I show that agreement with election procedures matters for voter
      confidence beyond well-established effects related to partisan control
      and electoral outcomes. Observational and experimental analyses

## Observations {#confidence-and-disputed-results--observations-gallup-accuracy-party}

### Confidence in the count follows the party in power {#confidence-and-disputed-results--confidence-follows-power}

Republicans were more confident than Democrats under George W. Bush, and Democrats more confident under Barack Obama and Joe Biden (changes of president are marked under the chart). [2](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-2) [3](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-3) The gap reached a record 56 points in 2024. [2](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-2)

A study pooling about 30 national surveys from 2000 to 2012 finds "a consistent relationship between voting for the winner and confidence in election administration". [6](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-6)

### In 2020 Republican confidence fell before the count, amid attacks on mail voting {#confidence-and-disputed-results--mail-claims-2020}

Gallup's 2020 reading was taken before the election. It ties that year's fall in Republican confidence, under a Republican president, to "then-President Donald Trump's public questioning of the validity of mail voting". [3](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-3)

After the result, Republican confidence fell further, to 22% in MIT's post-election survey (chart below). [5](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-5)

### The share with no confidence at all has grown {#confidence-and-disputed-results--not-at-all-confident-grew}

More than half of Americans have been confident in every Gallup reading since 2004. [3](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-3) But the share "not at all confident" rose from 6% in 2004 to 19% in 2024. [2](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-2)

## Observations {#confidence-and-disputed-results--observations-pew-count-by-mail}

### Voters trust the mail count less than the in-person count {#confidence-and-disputed-results--mail-below-in-person}

In each of Pew's four October readings since 2020, fewer voters were confident that mail and absentee votes would be counted as intended than votes cast in person (chart, both panels). [22](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-22)

### Republicans have stayed low on the mail count; Democrats fell in 2026 {#confidence-and-disputed-results--mail-confidence-by-party}

At most 42% of Republican candidates' supporters have been confident in the mail count in any reading. [22](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-22)

Democratic confidence in it fell from 85% among Harris voters in 2024 to 64% in October 2026. [1](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-1)

In-person confidence also rose among Republicans and fell among Democrats between 2024 and 2026. [22](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-22)

## Observations {#confidence-and-disputed-results--observations-mail-count-by-voting-method}

### People who vote by mail trust the mail count {#confidence-and-disputed-results--mail-voters-trust-mail}

About three in four mail and absentee voters are confident their kind of ballot will be counted as intended, against about half of in-person voters (chart). [1](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-1) Confidence in the mail count is highest among the people who use it.

## Observations {#confidence-and-disputed-results--observations-spae-nationwide-party}

### After each election the winner's side becomes more confident {#confidence-and-disputed-results--winner-swing-after-elections}

In MIT's post-election survey, each party's confidence in the nationwide count rises when its candidate wins the presidency and falls when he or she loses (chart). [5](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-5)

In surveys from the 2000s, winners' confidence in their own vote rose about 20 points after an election, against a fall of about 2 points among losers. [6](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-6)

### The winner effect has outweighed what leaders say, with exceptions {#confidence-and-disputed-results--winner-effect-exceptions}

A study of 2016 finds the winner effect outweighed pre-election "rigged" messaging from elites. [8](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-8) In 2018 the usual winner effect did not appear, and in 2008 both parties' confidence fell to 57%. [7](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-7) [3](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-3)

### The series cannot separate counting time from who won {#confidence-and-disputed-results--count-length-not-separated}

Republican confidence was already low before the 2020 count and fell further after it. [3](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-3) [5](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-5) Because the same voters also lost, these series cannot show whether a long count or a changing lead lowers confidence on its own; see Data still needed.

## Observations {#confidence-and-disputed-results--observations-gallup-honesty-total}

### US confidence in the honesty of elections has been low for two decades {#confidence-and-disputed-results--honesty-long-run}

US confidence in the honesty of elections has averaged 45% since 2006, with its low in 2016 (chart). [4](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-4) This is a different question from Gallup's vote-count item and gives lower figures.

Party was recorded only from 2024. The Democratic–Republican gap was 55 points that year, closed to 4 points in 2025 and reopened in 2026. [4](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-4)

## Observations {#confidence-and-disputed-results--observations-winner-gap-worldwide}

### A winner–loser gap is the global norm; in 2026 the US ran the other way {#confidence-and-disputed-results--us-breaks-global-pattern}

Across more than 100 countries, people who approve of their national leadership are far more confident in election honesty than those who disapprove (chart). [4](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-4)

In 2026 American disapprovers were the more confident group, which Gallup says no other country has shown in 15 years. [4](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-4)

## Observations {#confidence-and-disputed-results--observations-disputes}

### Six disputed US election results, and how each ended {#confidence-and-disputed-results--disputes-table}

These are prominent recent cases in which a losing side challenged a result, not a complete list.

### Mail ballots were central in the three most recent disputes {#confidence-and-disputed-results--mail-central-in-recent-disputes}

The 2020 dispute followed months of questioning mail voting, Arizona's centred on signature checks of mail ballots, and Bridgeport's on absentee ballots in drop boxes (see the timeline). [3](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-3) [18](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-18) [19](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-19) The three earlier disputes were about a recount, irregularities at the polls and the voter rolls.

### Every dispute ended with the original winner in office {#confidence-and-disputed-results--every-dispute-resolved}

Five disputes were settled by a court or by Congress; in Georgia the losing candidate ended her campaign without conceding. [13](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-13) In every case the original winner ended up in office. [11](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-11) [12](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-12) [14](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-14) [18](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-18) In Arizona, the losing candidate "did not contest whether voters' signatures on ballot envelopes matched". [18](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-18)

### Bridgeport's dispute was about real ballot misconduct {#confidence-and-disputed-results--bridgeport-misconduct-found}

Unlike the others, Bridgeport's challenge succeeded: the judge found the miscounted ballots left the court "unable to determine the result of the primary". [19](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-19) The incumbent won the court-ordered rerun primary and the later general election. [20](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-20)

### No survey measured confidence just before and after each dispute {#confidence-and-disputed-results--no-before-after-measure}

The Gallup, Pew and MIT series above are annual, pre-election or post-election snapshots, and Florida 2000 predates them. None measures confidence immediately before and after a dispute, so whether disputes themselves lower trust cannot be read from them.

## Observations {#confidence-and-disputed-results--observations-rule-changes}

### No study tests whether restricting mail voting would raise losers' confidence {#confidence-and-disputed-results--would-restriction-raise-confidence}

No study found tests whether tighter mail rules raise the losing side's confidence in results. The nearest evidence points both ways at once.

Clinton finds voters "less likely to express confidence in election results conducted using procedures they oppose", beyond who wins. [9](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-9) In Georgia, 59.1% of Republicans said the state's 2021 election law, which tightened ID rules for mail ballots, raised their confidence, while a 46.8% plurality of Democrats said it lowered theirs. [10](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-10)

### A rule's confidence effect is hard to separate from winning {#confidence-and-disputed-results--rule-effect-confounded}

Georgia Republicans' confidence in the statewide count rebounded in 2022, the first general election under that law (SB 202). The authors caution this may be "the noted winner's effect", since the party won almost every statewide office that year. [10](#source-confidence-and-disputed-results-10)

## About this data {#confidence-and-disputed-results--about}

**What this measures.** Opinion surveys of adults (Gallup) or registered voters (Pew, MIT), and the court and congressional record of six disputed results. Pew counts independents who lean to a party with that party; Gallup does not, so each pollster's series is shown separately.

**What it does not measure.** Confidence is belief, not evidence about whether ballots were counted correctly. For what is known about late ballots and counting, see [ballot deadlines and counting speed](#fact-ballot-deadlines-and-counting-speed).

**How it compares.** The only consistent cross-country measure here is Gallup's World Poll honesty question, published as a global median rather than country by country.

**Why these six disputes.** They are prominent recent cases in which a losing side challenged a result, not a complete list. Bridgeport is included because a court voided the result; most local disputes are not shown.

**Common mistakes.** Lower confidence in mail ballots is often read as evidence of mail fraud; it measures belief, and the people who use mail ballots trust them most.
