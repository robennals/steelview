Britain's asylum system has a real problem. Too many people claim, and too few of those who are refused ever leave. A refusal should mean leaving, or rules do not mean what they say. But most of the causes lie in British law and British delay, and Parliament can fix both. It does not need to leave the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) to do it.

### Too many people claim asylum, and too few who are refused leave

Asylum claims passed their 2002 peak and reached record levels in 2024 and 2025. Supporting the people who wait costs about £4 billion a year. And a refusal rarely ends with the person leaving: since 2015, under a third of each year's refused or withdrawn claims have ended in a recorded return. A refusal that nobody enforces is a refusal in name only. While claims have begun to fall, no one has shown what caused this year's fall in crossings, so it is no reason to ease off.

### When refusals were quick and returns were real, Albanian claims fell

Britain has already seen claims fall this way. Once nearly all Albanian claims were refused and returns to Albania rose sharply, Albanian asylum claims fell to their lowest since 2012. They fell by every route, so people did not just switch from boats to another way of claiming, though the count covers only people who claim asylum, not anyone who comes without claiming. And Britain was in the ECHR throughout. While the first fall in Albanian boats came before the returns deal, and no source separates the deal from faster decisions and more returns, the case does not rest on which one did most. All three were done inside the Convention.

### Britain cleared its decision queue by deciding faster and refusing more

The queue for a first decision is down by more than three-quarters from its peak, and hotel use is down by over two-thirds. That was not done by waving people through: the share of claims granted fell from 76% in 2022 to 42% in 2025. The delay has now moved to appeals, so appeals come next. A Bill before Parliament replaces the appeals tribunal and rewrites how judges weigh family life, all inside the ECHR. While the Bill's own assessment says many of those refused may still not leave, no one can be removed until they have been refused.

### Most of what stops removals is British law, not the ECHR

Look at what people held for removal actually raised to stop it. In 2019, the most common were fresh asylum claims, medical reports, slavery referrals and court challenges. Human-rights applications were raised in only 5% of detentions. British law governs most of these, so Parliament can change them. Other counts are small too. Foreign offenders who won appeals on human-rights grounds alone equalled about 3% of those deported, and the Strasbourg court granted one of 346 UK requests to pause a removal in 2023 to 2025. While no published figure counts the removals the ECHR stops, these counts point the same way.

### Leaving the ECHR would risk a lot to gain little

Leaving would not remove the rule that matters most. The Refugee Convention's ban on sending refugees back to danger would still apply. And when the Supreme Court stopped the Rwanda plan in 2023, it relied on Acts of Parliament that give effect to that Convention, not only on the Human Rights Act. Against that small gain stands the risk. The EU could end police and criminal-records cooperation with Britain, and whether leaving would break the Good Friday Agreement is disputed. Britain could pay a real price and still not be free to return refugees to danger.

### So Britain should refuse weak claims fast, rewrite appeals, and keep the Convention

Britain should keep going with what it has begun: decide claims fast, refuse the weak ones, return the people it refuses, and finish rewriting appeals and the family-life rules in its own law. It should stay in the Convention while it does.

This works where most claims fail. It does not answer for Sudanese and Eritrean claimants, most of whom are granted protection, and Britain has an obligation to people in danger. For them, protection should last 30 months and be renewed only if going home is still unsafe. Danger can pass: after Syria's government fell, Syrians' grant rate dropped from 98% to 10% in a year.
