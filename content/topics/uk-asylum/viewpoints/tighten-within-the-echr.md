Britain takes more asylum claims than it can handle, and too few people whose claims fail ever leave. A refusal should mean leaving, or rules do not mean what they say. British law can fix most of this without leaving the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR).

### Too many people claim asylum, and too few who are refused leave

Asylum claims passed their 2002 peak to reach record levels in 2024 and 2025, and since 2015, under a third of each year's refused or withdrawn claims have ended in a recorded return. A refusal that does not lead to leaving is not a refusal in practice, and supporting asylum seekers costs about £4 billion a year. Claims have begun to fall, but no one has settled what caused this year's fall in crossings, so it is no reason to stop.

### Albanian claims fell by every route once refusals and returns sped up

Once nearly all Albanian claims came to be refused and returns to Albania rose sharply, Albanian asylum claims by every route fell to their lowest since 2012. They did not just switch to claiming another way, and Britain was in the ECHR throughout. The first fall in Albanian boats came before the returns deal, and no source separates the deal from faster decisions and more returns. The chart counts only people who claim asylum, not anyone who comes without claiming.

### Britain cleared its decision queue by refusing more, and appeals are next

The queue for first decisions is down by more than three-quarters from its peak. It was not cleared by saying yes: the share of claims granted fell from 76% in 2022 to 42% in 2025, and hotel use is down by over two-thirds. But the backlog has moved to appeals. A Bill now before Parliament replaces the appeals tribunal and rewrites how judges weigh family life, inside the ECHR. While the Bill's own assessment says many of those refused may not leave, each refusal makes removal possible.

### Most of what stops removals is not the ECHR

In 2019, people held for removal most often raised fresh asylum claims, medical reports, slavery referrals and court challenges. Human-rights applications were raised in only 5% of detentions. British law governs most of these, so Parliament can change them. Foreign offenders who won appeals on human-rights grounds alone equalled about 3% of those deported, and the Strasbourg court granted one of 346 UK requests to pause a removal in 2023 to 2025. While no published figure counts the removals the ECHR stops, these counts point the same way.

### Leaving the ECHR could cost police cooperation, and the ban on return would remain

If Britain left, the EU could end police and criminal-records cooperation. And the Refugee Convention's ban on sending refugees back to danger would still apply. Britain could pay a real price and still not be free to return refugees to danger.

When the Supreme Court stopped the Rwanda plan in 2023, it relied on Acts of Parliament that give effect to the Refugee Convention, not only the Human Rights Act. Whether leaving would break the Good Friday Agreement is disputed.

### So Britain should refuse weak claims fast, rewrite appeals, and keep the Convention

Britain has cut claims from Albania, cleared its decision queue while refusing more, and is rewriting appeals, all inside the ECHR. It should press on with each, tie protection to continuing danger, and stay in the Convention.

This works where most claims fail. Most Sudanese and Eritrean claimants are granted protection, and Britain has an obligation to people in danger. For them, the rule should be that protection lasts 30 months, renewed only if return is still unsafe: after Syria's government fell, Syrians' grant rate dropped from 98% to 10% in a year.
