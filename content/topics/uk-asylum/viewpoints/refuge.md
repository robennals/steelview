A person running from a war or a prison cell matters as much as anyone else, wherever they were born. When that person asks Britain for safety, Britain should give it. And it can. The numbers are manageable, and people come by boat because Britain offers them no safe way to come.

### Most people in the boats are fleeing real danger, by Britain's own judgment

Start with who is in the boats. Of the people who arrived by small boat since 2018 and got a decision, most were granted protection. Those decisions were made by Britain's own officials, and each grant means they judged the person would face danger if sent back. Eritreans and Sudanese are now the largest groups crossing, and most of them were granted protection in 2025. Officials do say no: people who arrived in 2023 and 2024 were refused slightly more often than granted, and after Syria's government fell, Syrians' grant rate dropped from 98% to 10% in a year. A system that says no when the danger ends can be believed when it says yes.

### Britain is asked to do its share, and it has managed far more

Britain receives about 15 asylum claims a year for every 10,000 people who live here. That is the same rate as the European Union as a whole, and in every year from 2014 to 2024 Britain's rate was lower. In 2025 Britain ranked thirteenth of 32 European countries for claims per head, and most of the world's refugees never reach Europe at all: they stay in the poorer countries next to the ones they fled. Few refugees can reach an island without crossing another country first. If each had to stop in the first safe country they reached, Britain would protect almost no one who came overland. Nor are the numbers beyond it. Small boats have brought between 29,000 and 46,000 people a year since 2021. In 2022 alone, Britain gave a safe route to six times as many people as came by boat, most of them from Ukraine and Hong Kong. Asylum seekers are a small part of all the people who move to Britain, and refugees who stay go on to work, about half of them within two years.

### People come by boat because war drove them out and there is no safe way in

Claims rose because the danger rose. Migration researchers at Oxford link the rise in asylum claims since 2022 to conflict in the Middle East and instability in the Horn of Africa and South Asia. The arrivals follow the danger: Eritreans, Sudanese and Somalis are now among the largest groups crossing, and Syrian arrivals fell by two-thirds after Assad's government fell. Boats also took over from older ways of arriving, such as hiding in lorries, which were detected less and less as the boats grew. What a refugee cannot do is come safely. The one route meant for refugees is resettlement, and Britain's main scheme has resettled 4,589 people since 2021. It took no new referrals in 2025/26, and it is reopening at a few hundred places at first, against 33,374 people who came by boat in the year to June 2026. The boats are what a refugee uses when nothing else is on offer.

### A safe route lets Britain choose who comes and how many

Hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians and Hong Kongers came to Britain on visas. Britain chose who could come, almost none came by boat, and no one needed a smuggler. The resettlement scheme, now reopened, has a yearly cap. While a safe route is not shown to stop the boats on its own, since Afghans had the largest resettlement route and still crossed, the reason to open one is control. On a safe route, Britain chooses who comes. On a boat, a smuggler does.

### Deterrence has cost a fortune and achieved little

Britain spent £715 million on the Rwanda scheme and a law to support it, and sent no asylum seekers there. Australia tried threats too. When it ruled that no one arriving by boat would ever settle there, more than 1,500 people arrived in the next 16 days. Its offshore camps have cost billions, and 18 people sent to them had died by 2021. While Australia's arrivals did fall to almost none once it turned boats back at sea, that means handing people back to the country they left, and Britain dropped its own turnback policy because it is rarely safe in the Channel. Turning boats back, likely the main reason Australia's boats stopped, is not open to Britain.

### Britain pays most for keeping people waiting and idle

Asylum support costs about £4 billion a year, and much of that buys waiting. In 2024, hotels housed about a third of the people in asylum accommodation and took about three-quarters of its cost. People wait because decisions are slow and are often overturned. In every year since 2007, between a quarter and a half of asylum appeals have succeeded, and an appeal takes over a year on average. While they wait, most are kept from working. Studies across Europe find that work bans leave refugees less likely to have a job for years afterwards, and the one study of where people choose to claim finds that bans are not a strong deterrent. A decision that is right the first time is both the fair one and the cheap one. Yet ministers plan a new appeals body whose decision-makers need not be lawyers.

### So Britain should open safe routes, decide claims fairly, and let people work

Britain should set resettlement numbers to match the need, let refugees bring their husbands, wives and children, let people work while they wait, and pay for decisions that are right the first time. People refused after a fair hearing should then leave.

Leaving the European Convention on Human Rights would not change the duty. The Refugee Convention's ban on sending refugees back to danger would still apply, and leaving could cost Britain its police cooperation with the EU.

Britain has done this before. It took in hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians and Hong Kongers, on terms it chose, and almost none came by boat. It can do the same for the far smaller number who now come by boat.
