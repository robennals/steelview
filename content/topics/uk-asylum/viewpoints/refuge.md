Britain has an obligation to people in danger. Britain should give refugees safe ways to come, in numbers it chooses, fair hearings, and the right to work while they wait.

### Most people crossing the Channel have been found to need protection

Of small-boat arrivals since 2018 who got a decision, most were granted protection, though arrivals in 2023 and 2024 were refused slightly more often than granted. Most Eritreans and Sudanese were granted in 2025, and they are now the largest groups crossing. By Britain's own decisions, most would face danger if sent back. Part of the recent fall in grants reflects real change: after Syria's government fell, Syrians' grant rate dropped from 98% to 10% in a year.

### Britain has closed the safe routes most of them could use

Family reunion, joining a refugee already here, became the largest route open to any nationality, and was then paused. The UK Resettlement Scheme took no new referrals in 2025/26. For Eritreans, Sudanese and Iranians, family reunion had been the main safe route, and in the year to June 2026, boat arrivals far exceeded safe-route grants for all three. Some of the largest groups who cross had almost no safe route at all. That leaves the people now crossing little other way in.

### A safe route lets Britain choose how many it protects; a boat does not

Hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians and Hong Kongers came on visas, and almost none came by boat. Britain set those numbers, and no one paid a smuggler. The reopened resettlement scheme has a yearly cap. Safe routes do not mean fewer people come: Afghans had the largest resettlement route and still crossed, and comparing routes with boats side by side is not a test of cause. The case is that Britain, not smugglers, decides who comes and how many.

### Many refusals are overturned on appeal, so appeals must stay fair

Since 2007, between a quarter and a half of asylum appeals each year have succeeded. Counting appeals and second decisions, final grant rates rose by 20 points on average for claims made in 2007–2022. A wrong refusal can send someone back to danger, and a 2022 law raised the standard of proof, which makes fair appeals matter more. Yet ministers plan a new appeals body whose decision-makers need not be lawyers, chaired by a Home Secretary appointee. If rules must mean what they say, the Refugee Convention's promise needs a fair hearing.

### Britain paid £715 million for a deterrent that sent no asylum seekers

Britain spent £715 million on the Rwanda scheme and a related law, and sent no asylum seekers there. So Britain paid for a deterrent that never ran.

Australia's offshore camps cost billions, and 18 people sent there had died by 2021. While Australia's arrivals fell to almost none after it turned boats back, handing people back at sea needs the country they left from to take them.

### Work bans hurt refugees and do little to deter claims

Studies across Europe find that work bans leave refugees less likely to have a job for years afterwards. The one study of where people choose to claim finds that bans are not a strong deterrent. So a ban holds refugees back without keeping many people away.

While shorter bans are linked to slightly more claims, the study's authors judge the link too small to make bans a strong deterrent.

### So Britain should open capped safe routes, decide claims fairly, and let people work

This meets Britain's duty to people in danger, in numbers Britain chooses, without a smuggler. People refused after a fair hearing should then leave.

Leaving the European Convention on Human Rights would not remove the Refugee Convention's ban on sending refugees back to danger, and could cost Britain police cooperation with the EU. Britain should reopen family reunion, set resettlement to match the need, let people work, and fund hearings that get decisions right.
