No one who enters illegally should be allowed to stay: bar their asylum claims, remove them, and leave the ECHR to make it possible.

### By 2025, Britain was seeing more asylum claims than ever before

Asylum claims passed their 2002 peak and reached record levels in 2024 and 2025. In 2018, small boats carried a few hundred people across the Channel. They went on to carry tens of thousands a year. Housing and supporting the people who wait costs about £4 billion a year. Crossings fell sharply in 2026. But crossings into Europe fell too, and no one has shown what caused the fall. Britain cannot rely on a fall it cannot explain.

### Some of the groups who come commit far more crime

Record numbers would matter less if the people arriving settled in and lived by Britain's laws. The figures that exist say some groups do not. Afghans, Iraqis and Iranians are among the nationalities that claim asylum. After adjusting for age and sex, Afghan nationals were convicted of sex offences at seven times the British rate. Across all offences, Iraqi, Afghan and Iranian citizens were convicted at four to six and a half times the British rate. People who grow up where violence is common and the law is weak can bring that with them. While no statistics record whether those offenders had claimed asylum, that gap is the state's to fill, and the Ministry of Justice is appealing an order to publish more detailed figures. Britain cannot weigh the risk of whom it admits without counting it.

### Everyone on a small boat has already reached a safe country

The boats set off from France and Belgium. So whatever a person first fled, the Channel crossing is a choice about where to claim, and British law already expects a refugee who stopped in another country to seek protection there. The Refugee Convention, the United Nations treaty on refugees, makes two demands: do not send a refugee back to danger, and do not punish one for entering illegally. Neither says Britain must hear every claim itself. While few refugees could reach Britain without passing through another country, the duty is to keep people out of danger, and that duty is met wherever they are safe.

### Claiming asylum means staying, even for people who are refused

An asylum system only works if a refusal means leaving. In Britain it usually does not. Up to 2013, over a quarter of each year's asylum claims ended with the person recorded as returned. Since 2015 it has been under one in seven, and under a third even of the claims that were refused or withdrawn. A refused person can also challenge the decision, and stays while the challenge runs. An asylum appeal takes over a year on average. While most challenges failed in 2017, each one still bought time, and for someone who means to stay, time is the prize.

### Human-rights law stops removals, and it cannot be fixed from inside

The Home Office itself says people use the right to family life, Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), to stop their removal. It says 86% of people who made a human-rights claim while held for removal in 2022 were released. While other counts look small, with foreign offenders who won on human-rights grounds alone equal to about 3% of those deported, they leave out claims of danger made inside asylum cases, and no published figure counts the removals the ECHR stops. Nor can the problem be solved by staying in and writing tighter rules. Lord Wolfson, advising the Conservative leader, judged that such limits would likely fail in court, and that Britain can lawfully leave and still keep the Refugee Convention.

### Where the crossing stopped working, people stopped crossing

From late 2013, Australia turned asylum boats back at sea, and arrivals fell to almost none. Months earlier it had banned boat arrivals from settling, and the boats had kept coming. The rule on paper changed nothing. The journey that failed did. Even the Kaldor Centre, a refugee-law institute that opposes offshore processing, ties the fall to turning boats back. Britain withdrew its own turnback policy as rarely safe in the Channel, so here the crossing has to fail on land, through removal. Albania points the same way. In December 2022 Britain signed a returns deal with Albania, then began refusing nearly all Albanian claims and sending people home. Albanian claims stayed low, and by 2026 were the lowest since 2012. While the first fall in crossings came before the deal, the deal is plausibly a major reason the numbers stayed low.

### So Britain should bar these claims, remove those who come, and leave the ECHR

A government's first duty is to its own people, and that includes deciding who may come to live among them. Britain should refuse asylum to anyone who enters illegally, and remove them. If crossing always ended in removal, smugglers would have nothing left to sell.

This asks a lot. People who cannot safely be sent home would need another safe country willing to take them, and Rwanda took no asylum seekers. The Conservatives' pledge of 150,000 removals a year is about 15 times today's enforced returns, though most returns are already voluntary, and those cost far less.

It also needs law the courts cannot undo. Leaving the ECHR is one part. The other is rewriting the Acts of Parliament that give effect to the Refugee Convention, because the Supreme Court relied on those too when it stopped the Rwanda plan. Parliament can change British law. It cannot change the ECHR.
