Anyone who enters Britain illegally should not be allowed to stay. Britain should refuse their claims, remove them, and leave European human-rights law if it stands in the way.

### Far more people now claim asylum than Britain can absorb

Asylum claims passed their 2002 peak to reach record levels in 2024 and 2025. Small-boat crossings went from a few hundred in 2018 to tens of thousands a year, and housing and supporting asylum seekers costs about £4 billion a year. Claims have since begun to fall, and crossings fell sharply in 2026. But no one has settled what caused the fall, and crossings into Europe fell too. Nothing yet shows that Britain has fixed the problem.

### People crossing the Channel have already passed through safe countries

The boats leave from France and Belgium, so everyone on them has already been in another country, and few refugees can reach Britain without passing through one. British law expects a refugee who stopped in another country to have sought protection there. Crossing on is a choice of where to claim. The Refugee Convention, the United Nations treaty on refugees, forbids sending a refugee back to danger and bars penalties for entering illegally. Neither rule says Britain must decide every claim itself, so long as no one is sent back to danger.

### People who claim asylum can expect to stay, even if they are refused

Up to 2013, over a quarter of each year's asylum claims ended with the person recorded as returned. Since 2015, under one in seven have, and under a third even of refused or withdrawn claims. As far as the records show, most stay. In 2021–2025, refusals far outran returns. Challenges keep people here while they run: in 2019 the commonest challenge from people held for removal was a fresh asylum claim, and an asylum appeal takes over a year on average. In 2017, most challenges failed, but they take time.

### Human-rights claims stop removals, the Home Office says

The Home Office says people use the right to family life, Article 8, to stop removals. It says 86% of people who made a human-rights claim while held for removal in 2022 were released. On the government's own account, the ECHR is being used to stop removals. Other counts look smaller: foreign offenders who won on human-rights grounds alone equalled about 3% of those deported. But that count misses claims of danger made inside asylum cases, and no published figure counts the removals the ECHR stops. The Strasbourg court itself seldom orders a removal paused: the claims are made under the Human Rights Act and heard in Britain.

### Limits written inside the ECHR would likely fail, so Britain should leave it

Lord Wolfson, advising the Conservative leader, judged that new limits written within the ECHR would likely fail in court. He also advised that Britain can lawfully leave, and keep the Refugee Convention. If limits within the ECHR fail, leaving is the way to make them hold.

When the Supreme Court stopped the Rwanda plan, it relied on Acts that give effect to the Refugee Convention, not only the Human Rights Act. So leaving the ECHR would not be enough on its own: Parliament would also have to rewrite those Acts. It can change British law. It cannot change the ECHR.

### Where crossing reliably failed, people stopped coming

From late 2013, Australia turned asylum boats back at sea, and arrivals fell to almost none. Months earlier it had banned boat arrivals from settling, and the boats had kept coming. The Kaldor Centre, a refugee-law institute that opposes offshore processing, also ties the fall to turning boats back. Britain withdrew its own turnback policy as rarely safe in the Channel, so removal has to make the crossing fail here. Albania points the same way: nearly all Albanian claims came to be refused, returns to Albania rose sharply, and Albanian asylum claims by every route fell.

### Nationalities that claim asylum are convicted more often, and the state does not count who claimed it

After adjusting for age and sex, Afghan nationals were convicted of sex offences at seven times the British rate, and Iraqi, Afghan and Iranian citizens were convicted of crimes at four to six and a half times the British rate. No statistics record whether those offenders had claimed asylum, and the Ministry of Justice is appealing an order to publish more detailed figures. Britain cannot weigh the risk of whom it admits without counting it.

### So Britain should bar these claims, remove those who come, and leave the ECHR

If everyone who crossed illegally were removed, crossing would no longer pay. Leaving the ECHR would let Parliament set those rules and make them stick. A government's first duty is to its own people.

People who cannot safely be sent home would need a safe country willing to take them, and Rwanda took no asylum seekers. The Conservatives' pledge of 150,000 removals a year would be about 15 times today's enforced returns. But most returns are already voluntary, and those cost far less.
