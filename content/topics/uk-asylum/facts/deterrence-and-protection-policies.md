## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-arrivals}

### Arrivals peaked in 2012 and 2013, the highest years in the series {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--australia-peak}

Boat arrivals were low for most of the period from 1976, with a smaller peak around 2001, then rose from 2009 to the two highest years in the series, 2012 and 2013. More than 24,000 people arrived between 13 August 2012, when offshore processing restarted, and 18 July 2013. [1](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-1) [5](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-5)

### Offshore processing and the no-settlement rule did not stop arrivals on their own {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--offshore-processing-alone}

During the first phase of offshore processing, which still allowed eventual settlement in Australia, the Kaldor Centre found "no noticeable change in the rate of arrivals". After the government announced on 19 July 2013 that no one arriving by boat would ever settle in Australia, more than 1,500 people on at least 21 boats arrived in the first 16 days. [6](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-6) [7](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-7)

### Arrivals fell to almost none after boats began to be turned back {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--australia-arrivals-fell-after-turnbacks}

Operation Sovereign Borders began in September 2013, and the first recorded turnback was on 19 December 2013. Arrivals fell to 160 people in 2014 and none in 2015 or 2016. By October 2016, 740 people on 29 vessels had been returned to their country of departure since December 2013. [1](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-1) [2](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-2)

The no-settlement rule (July 2013), the start of Operation Sovereign Borders (September 2013) and the first turnback (December 2013) all came in the same year, so annual figures alone cannot separate them. Monthly figures were not found (see Data still needed).

### Which measure caused the fall is disputed {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--australia-cause}

The Kaldor Centre, which opposes offshore processing, reads the fall in arrivals as following the start of maritime interceptions, and attributes the end of deaths at sea to turnbacks and takebacks rather than offshore processing. It points out that Australia stopped sending new arrivals offshore in 2014 and returned boats instead. [10](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-10) [9](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-9) [11](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-11)

The Australian government described offshore processing as part of a "broad chain of measures end to end", and as minister Peter Dutton argued that letting people move from Manus to Australia would bring the boats back. Both readings fit the annual data. [12](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-12) [13](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-13)

### Takebacks rely on the country of departure's cooperation {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--turnbacks-need-departure-country}

Operation Sovereign Borders defines a turnback as removing a vessel from Australian waters with passengers returned to their country of departure, and a takeback as a transfer to that country's authorities "where Australia works with a country of departure". [3](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-3)

### Australia did this while remaining in the Refugee Convention {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--australia-stayed-in-refugee-convention}

Australia has ratified the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol. The Kaldor Centre argues that offshore processing breached those obligations. Australia has no bill or charter of rights, so the policy could not be challenged in Australian courts on the ground that it broke international law. [19](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-19) [20](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-20)

## Subtleties {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--subtleties-arrivals}

### Arrivals are not attempts {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--arrivals-are-not-attempts}

A boat turned back is not counted as an arrival; the Parliamentary Library notes, for example, that one boat in 2014 was turned back and not counted. The zero arrivals for 2015 and 2016 therefore mean no boat landed, not that no boat set out. The next chart shows the boats intercepted. [4](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-4) [8](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-8)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-after-sovereign-borders}

### Boats kept coming at a lower rate, and from 2015 every one was returned {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--returned-not-landed}

Of the 1,241 people on boats from September to December 2013, 1,107 were brought to Australia or sent offshore and 134 returned. In 2014, 157 were brought in and 293 returned. From 2015 to 2019, all 385 people on 23 boats were returned (217 + 51 + 60 + 24 + 33 people; 9 + 6 + 3 + 2 + 3 boats). These are Home Affairs figures as reproduced by the Kaldor Centre. [8](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-8)

### Offshore transfers resumed in late 2023 {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--nauru-transfers-resumed}

The Refugee Council of Australia reports that transfers to Nauru resumed in late 2023, for the first time since 2014, with around 105 people held there in February 2026. [22](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-22)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-offshore-cost}

### Offshore processing cost about a billion Australian dollars a year at its height {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--offshore-cost-per-year}

Two sets of government figures compiled by the Kaldor Centre put the cost at an average of at least A$1 billion a year, peaking at A$1.49 billion in 2017-18. The Refugee Council of Australia puts total spending since 2012 at more than A$13 billion. [17](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-17) [21](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-21)

### Costs stayed high as the number of people held fell {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--offshore-fixed-costs}

The number held on Nauru and Papua New Guinea fell from a peak of 2,450 in April 2014 to 239 in May 2021, while annual spending stayed in the hundreds of millions. The Kaldor Centre reads this as evidence of large fixed costs. [18](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-18)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-australia-outcomes}

### Most people sent offshore did not stay offshore {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--australia-outcomes}

Of approximately 4,180 people transferred from 2012 to 2014, 2,043 were back in Australia by January 2021, 921 had been resettled in a third country and 935 had returned to their country of origin; 18 had died. [14](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-14) [15](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-15) [16](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-16)

The Refugee Council of Australia's count to December 2025 covers 4,359 people transferred, including those sent since 2023: 1,547 resettled in third countries, 978 returned or removed, and 21 dead. [23](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-23)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-rwanda-spend}

### The UK spent £715 million and sent four volunteers {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--rwanda-cost-and-relocations}

The Home Office spent £715 million on the Rwanda partnership and the Illegal Migration Act up to June 2024, including £290 million paid to Rwanda. Four volunteers and no asylum seekers were sent to Rwanda. Ending the partnership avoided over £220 million in scheduled payments. [25](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-25) [26](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-26) [24](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-24) [28](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-28)

The £715 million covers the Illegal Migration Act as well as the partnership, so it is not all the cost of relocating four people. The payments to Rwanda were mostly fixed sums due whatever the number relocated, and the other categories are largely fixed costs; per person, the agreement also provided up to £150,874 for processing and support over five years. [27](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-27) [25](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-25) [30](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-30)

### The scheme was never tested as a deterrent {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--rwanda-untested}

The National Audit Office did not judge value for money, because that rests on whether the partnership deterred journeys. With no asylum seekers relocated, there is no evidence on that either way. [29](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-29) [24](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-24)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-per-head}

### Denmark's intake is now among the lowest per head in Europe {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--denmark-intake-low}

Denmark had 1,835 first-time asylum applicants in 2025, 3.1 per 10,000 residents, against 14.9 across the EU27. The UK received 15 per 10,000 in 2025 on the Migration Observatory's count of all applicants. [33](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-33) [64](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-64) [35](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-35)

### Denmark's 2016 fall was shared with Sweden, which also tightened {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--denmark-2016-fall-matched-sweden}

Denmark's first-time applicants fell from 20,825 in 2015 to 6,055 in 2016, 29% of the year before. Sweden's fell further, from 156,115 to 22,335, 14% of the year before. Sweden also moved from permanent to temporary residence permits in 2016. The EU27 total in 2016 was still 96% of 2015 and fell sharply only in 2017, so the Danish and Swedish falls came about a year ahead of the wider European one. [33](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-33) [41](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-41)

### Denmark changed several rules at once {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--denmark-several-changes}

Since 2015 Denmark has made refugee status temporary with review every two years, restricted family reunion, and lengthened the wait for permanent settlement to eight years, with integration and employment conditions. Its applicants peaked in 2015, the year temporary status began, during the Europe-wide rise. The data cannot attribute the later fall to any one of these measures. [32](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-32) [33](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-33)

### The "40-year low" cannot be checked against Eurostat {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--forty-year-low}

The Home Secretary's March 2026 statement said Denmark had cut claims to a 40-year low while UK claims rose 13% and EU claims fell 22% in the year to September 2025. Eurostat's series starts in 2008. Within it, Denmark's 2025 total of 1,835 is the second-lowest year, above 2020 (1,420), the first year of the pandemic. [31](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-31) [33](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-33)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-grant-rate}

### Denmark grants protection at about the EU rate {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--denmark-grant-rate}

In 2025, 36% of Denmark's first-instance decisions were positive, against 39% across the EU27. In 2016 the figures were 68% and 62%. Denmark's low intake is not explained by a lower share of positive decisions at first instance. [34](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-34)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-eu-per-head-2025}

### Denmark ranks 23rd of 31 EU+ countries per head {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--denmark-rank-2025}

On first-time applicants per 10,000 residents in 2025, Denmark sits in the bottom third of the 31 EU and EFTA countries, below Sweden and well below Germany, which is close to the EU27 average. [63](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-63) [64](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-64)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-residence-status}

### Less-secure status has not been found to raise employment {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--less-secure-status-no-employment-gain}

Studies of Denmark's 2002 reform and Germany's 2016 reform found no employment gain from making permanent residence harder to get. Denmark's 2007 reform reduced work hours, driven by refugees who had been doing worst before it. Sweden's 2016 switch to temporary permits had no effect on employment in the first year. [36](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-36) [38](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-38) [40](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-40) [41](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-41)

### One Swedish study found a short-run gain on temporary permits {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--sweden-short-run-exception}

Jutvik and Robinson found that refugees on temporary permits had higher incomes and less unemployment in the short term than those given permanent residence, but spent less time in education. [37](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-37)

### None of these studies covers the UK {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--no-uk-evaluation}

All five findings come from Denmark, Germany and Sweden, and they measure employment rather than the number of people who claim asylum. No evaluation of the UK's 30-month protection, which began on 2 March 2026, was found. [51](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-51)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-work-rights}

### Work rights show a modest association with where people claim {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--work-rights-modest-association}

The one cross-country study of destinations finds that shorter work bans go with more applications, but the association is about a quarter the size of the association with earlier applications from the same country (0.18% against 0.66% for each one per cent change). Its authors conclude that work bans "are not a strong deterrence". It is a working paper on EU countries, not the UK. [43](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-43) [42](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-42)

### Work bans reduce later employment {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--work-bans-reduce-employment}

Studies across Europe, Germany and Switzerland find that delaying work or decisions lowers refugees' employment for years afterwards. In 2021 the Migration Advisory Committee urged the government to review the UK ban, citing "clear evidence of the harm" and "little evidence" of significant benefits. [44](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-44) [45](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-45) [46](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-46) [47](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-47)

### Ministers argue earlier work rights would draw more crossings {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--uk-work-rule}

In Parliament the Home Secretary said in November 2025 that a right to work while waiting "would be a huge pull factor", and in September 2025 a Home Office minister said cutting the wait to three months "could act as a pull factor". No published Home Office estimate of the size of that effect was found (see Data still needed). [49](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-49) [50](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-50)

## Observations {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--observations-uk-2026}

### The government presents the changes as following Denmark {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--inspired-by-denmark}

The March 2026 statement calls the reset "inspired by Denmark’s success" and aims to "reduce the pull factors driving illegal migration". The Danish evidence above shows a low and falling intake, alongside a Swedish fall of similar timing and a grant rate near the EU's. [31](#source-deterrence-and-protection-policies-31)

### The Bill is not yet law {#deterrence-and-protection-policies--bill-not-law}

The Immigration and Asylum Bill was introduced on 30 June 2026 and was still progressing through Parliament in July 2026. The 30-month rule already applies through the Immigration Rules.
