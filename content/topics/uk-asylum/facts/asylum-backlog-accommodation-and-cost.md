## Observations {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--observations-queue}

### The first-decision backlog and hotel use are far below their peaks {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--backlog-and-hotels-below-peaks}

40,168 people were waiting for an initial decision at the end of June 2026, 77% fewer than at the June 2023 peak of 175,457 and the lowest number since June 2019. The queue is still longer than it was through the 2010s: it grew from 7,240 in December 2010 to 35,855 in December 2018. [1](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-1) [2](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-2)

Hotel use fell by the same order: 16,021 supported asylum seekers were in hotels at the end of June 2026, 71% below the peak of 56,018 in September 2023 (see the accommodation charts below). [17](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-17)

### The queue has moved into the appeals system {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--queue-moved-to-appeals}

As the first-decision queue shrank, the tribunal's queue grew. Open asylum and protection appeals at the First-tier Tribunal rose from 6,319 at the end of June 2022 to 90,341 at the end of June 2026, more than fourteen times as many. By June 2026 the appeals line was more than twice the first-decision line, though the two count different things (see Subtleties). [3](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-3) [4](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-4)

The National Audit Office traces this to the push to clear the legacy backlog: the Home Office made more than four times as many initial decisions in 2023 as in 2022, and "this shifted pressure into the appeals stage where constraints on legal and judicial capacity created another backlog". The tribunal dates the steep rise in new asylum appeals to late 2023 and the same backlog clearance, and the Home Office's accounts attribute it to "higher volumes of initial asylum decisions flowing into the tribunal system". [8](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-8) [7](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-7) [14](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-14)

Most refusals are appealed: the Home Office's evidence to the NAO is that 68% of people refused at initial decision in 2024-25 lodged an appeal. An asylum or protection appeal took a mean of 72 weeks to clear in April to June 2026. New appeals have started to fall in the latest two quarters, but the open caseload was still rising in June 2026. [9](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-9) [5](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-5) [7](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-7) [4](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-4)

### Why the first-decision queue rose, and why it fell {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--why-the-queue-rose-and-fell}

- **2021 to 2023: more claims, few decisions.** Claims rose between the years to June 2021 and June 2023 while initial decisions stayed comparatively low, so the queue grew. [50](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-50)
- **March 2023 to mid-2024: decisions on hold.** Many claims made on or after 7 March 2023 could not be decided under the Illegal Migration Act, until the government removed its retrospective application in July 2024. The queue rose again in late 2024 before falling. [13](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-13)
- **From late 2024: more decisions.** The Home Office attributes the fall to more decision makers and higher productivity; the queue has fallen every quarter since decision making fully resumed in October to December 2024. [12](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-12) [13](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-13)

### About two in five asylum appeals decided by a judge are allowed {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--two-in-five-appeals-allowed}

39% of asylum and protection appeals determined at a hearing or on the papers in April to June 2026 were allowed. That share excludes appeals that ended without a judge's decision: across all appeal types, 29% of the quarter's disposals were withdrawn and 17% struck out for an unpaid fee. [6](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-6)

## Subtleties {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--subtleties-queue}

### People and appeals are different counts {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--people-and-appeals-are-different-counts}

The first-decision line counts people, including dependants; the tribunal line counts open appeals in the asylum, protection and revocation-of-protection category. Human-rights appeals are a separate category (31,459 open in June 2026), and some of them arise from asylum cases. Adding the two lines would mix units, so the chart shows where the queue sits rather than one total. [4](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-4)

### The whole asylum caseload is larger than either line, and its latest count is from 2024 {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--whole-system-is-larger}

The Home Office's count of all cases in the asylum system, including people awaiting an appeal outcome or removal, rose from 55,814 in June 2014 to 224,742 in June 2024, when 40% were awaiting an initial decision. It has not been published beyond June 2024 because of a change of casework system. The NAO also found that 41% of a sample of people who claimed in January 2023 had open claims that were neither awaiting an appeal nor further submissions, and had not been removed. [10](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-10) [11](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-11) [15](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-15)

## Observations {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--observations-support}

### Fewer people are on support, but not in step with the first-decision queue {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--support-fell-less-than-the-queue}

93,293 people were on asylum support at the end of June 2026, 25% below the September 2023 peak of 123,758, a much smaller fall than the 77% in the first-decision queue. The Home Office notes that with 40,168 people awaiting an initial decision, most people on support are in other parts of the system, such as awaiting an appeal or refused. 5% (4,987) were on Section 4 support, which is for people whose claim has been finally refused but who cannot yet leave. [16](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-16) [19](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-19) [20](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-20) [2](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-2)

### Dispersal accommodation kept growing as hotel use fell {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--dispersal-kept-growing}

The number of people in dispersal housing, the longer-term homes the contracts provide, has risen in most years since 2014 and did not fall with the queue. At the end of June 2026, 73,068 people (78%) were in non-hotel accommodation, 3% more than a year earlier. Part of the fall in hotel use is people moving to other accommodation rather than leaving support. [18](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-18)

## Observations {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--observations-hotels}

### Hotel use peaked in September 2023 and has fallen by 71% {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--hotel-use-far-below-peak}

16,021 people were in hotels at the end of June 2026, 17% of everyone on support, half the number a year earlier and 71% below the peak of 56,018. The government said in August 2026 that fewer than 160 asylum hotels remained in use, against 400 at the 2023 peak. Those are hotel counts from a ministerial announcement, not from the statistics. [17](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-17) [41](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-41) [42](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-42)

### Large sites hold a small share so far {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--large-sites-small-share}

At the end of June 2026, 744 people were at Wethersfield and 418 at Crowborough, the two former military sites in use. They sit in the second line of this chart, with initial and other contingency accommodation. [21](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-21)

## Observations {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--observations-support-spending}

### Asylum support costs around £4.0 billion a year {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--support-costs-about-4bn-a-year}

The Home Office spent around £4.0 billion on asylum support in 2024-25, down from £4.7 billion in 2023-24. It had spent around £739 million in 2019-20 and £3.6 billion in 2022-23. Counting casework, appeals, detention and unaccompanied children as well, the NAO puts Home Office and Ministry of Justice spending on asylum at around £4.9 billion in 2024-25, without local authorities' costs. The government expects reforms to cut asylum costs by at least £1 billion a year by 2028-29 against 2024-25. [22](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-22) [26](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-26) [23](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-23) [25](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-25)

### Hotels drove both the rise and the fall {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--hotels-drove-the-rise-and-fall}

Hotels took £2.3 billion of the £3.6 billion spent in 2022-23. Hotel spending was £3.0 billion in 2023-24 and £2.1 billion in 2024-25, and the Home Office's accounts say asylum support spending rose "due to the increased use of hotel accommodation, peaking in 2023-24, with year-on-year reductions in 2024-25 and 2025-26". [26](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-26) [27](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-27) [30](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-30)

## Observations {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--observations-asylum-budget-line}

### The asylum line is about a quarter of the Home Office's net day-to-day budget {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--share-of-home-office-budget}

The asylum business group was 29% of the Home Office's Resource DEL outturn in 2023-24 and 23% in 2025-26 (asylum line divided by the total, from the core table). The total is net of visa and passport fee income, which makes the asylum share larger than it would be against gross spending, and the asylum line includes resettlement, such as £222.1 million for Afghan resettlement in 2025-26. Plans to 2028-29 have the line falling every year. [28](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-28) [29](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-29) [31](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-31)

## Observations {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--observations-system-costs-2024-25}

### Accommodation dominates; appeals and removals are small lines {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--accommodation-dominates-system-cost}

Accommodation and support was £3,406 million of the NAO's estimate for 2024-25. Appeals cost about £102 million and asylum detention and removals about £46 million, the smallest items in the figure. [24](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-24) [23](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-23)

## Observations {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--observations-hotels-share-of-people-and-cost}

### Hotels are a third of people and three-quarters of cost {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--hotels-third-of-people-three-quarters-of-cost}

In the first seven months of 2024-25, people in hotels were around 35% of everyone in asylum accommodation and around 76% of the contracts' cost: £1.3 billion out of an estimated £1.7 billion. Hotels cost from around £127 to £148 per person per night between April 2023 and January 2024, and suppliers' data suggest hotels may be more profitable for them than other accommodation. [33](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-33) [35](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-35) [34](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-34)

### The contracts now cost more than three times their original estimate {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--contracts-cost-overrun}

The Home Office estimated the accommodation contracts at £4.5 billion over ten years when it let them; the estimate is now £15.3 billion over the same period. [32](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-32)

## Subtleties {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--subtleties-hotels-share-of-people-and-cost}

### The shares date from 2024 {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--hotel-shares-date-from-2024}

The 35% and 76% describe late 2024, when far more people were in hotels than in June 2026. The NAO has not published an updated split, and no source here gives a current cost per night for hotels against dispersal housing or large sites. [33](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-33)

## Observations {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--observations-large-sites}

### Large sites were judged dearer than hotels in 2024, and are now being expanded {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--large-sites-cost-and-reversal}

In March 2024 the NAO reported the Home Office's own analysis that, including sunk costs, its four large sites would cost about £46 million more than hotels. By May 2025 the Home Office no longer planned to use large sites other than Wethersfield, having found that "costs increased" and that housing many people near small communities "is difficult to manage". In 2026 it reversed course: Crowborough opened in January, and the three proposed MOD sites could house around 3,750 people. Cherwell District Council gives the total as 3,700. No cost estimate for the new sites appears in the announcement, the factsheets or the council statement cited here. [36](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-36) [37](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-37) [44](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-44) [39](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-39) [43](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-43)

### Other European countries also house asylum seekers in large sites {#asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost--europe-uses-large-sites-too}

In the NAO's comparison countries, Germany houses asylum seekers in initial reception centres for up to 18 months before collective centres, and most people entitled to asylum accommodation in the Netherlands in early 2025 stayed in emergency sites such as halls, ships and tents. The NAO compared how these systems work, not what they cost per person, so there is no like-for-like cost comparison with the UK here. [46](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-46) [47](#source-asylum-backlog-accommodation-and-cost-47)
