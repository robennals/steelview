Removals from the UK fell through the 2010s, collapsed during the pandemic and have risen each year since 2021, but enforced returns in the year to June 2026 (9,669) were less than half their 2004 level. [2](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-2) [1](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-1) Of the 40,605 returns that year, 76% were voluntary. Asylum-related returns reached 12,568, the highest in a series starting in 2010. [5](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-5)

No source measures how many failed removals the ECHR blocks. The Home Office says it does not hold data on people granted status after being told they were liable to removal. [17](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-17) What exists are proxies: issues raised in detention, cancelled removal directions, failed charter-flight seats, and foreign offenders' appeals won on human-rights grounds. Each is shown below with its limits.

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-returns}

### Returns fell for a decade, then recovered after 2021 {#removals-and-what-stops-them--returns-fell-then-recovered}

Enforced returns drifted down from 2004 to 2019 and voluntary returns fell from 2016, before both collapsed during the pandemic. Since 2021 enforced returns have risen each year, to 9,669 in the year to June 2026, and voluntary returns to 30,936. [2](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-2) [3](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-3) The Migration Observatory puts 2025 returns at the highest since 2016 but still slightly below a decade before. [8](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-8)

### Why returns fell in the 2010s is not settled {#removals-and-what-stops-them--why-returns-fell-is-not-settled}

The Migration Observatory attributes the 2010s decline to a combination of resource constraints, more legal challenges, administrative delays and fewer referrals to Immigration Enforcement, and the recovery to bigger budgets and higher immigration. Enforcement spending fell 19% in real terms between 2014/15 and 2020/21. [9](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-9) [10](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-10) It says limited data prevent a definitive assessment of the legal-challenge share. [11](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-11)

### Most returns are voluntary, and much cheaper {#removals-and-what-stops-them--most-returns-are-voluntary}

An enforced return cost an average of £48,800 in 2024/25, against £4,300 for a voluntary one. [14](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-14) Port returns — people refused entry who leave again — are counted separately: 18,646 in the year to June 2026. [1](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-1) All these figures count returns, not people: someone returned twice in a period is counted twice. [7](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-7)

### Return agreements are neither necessary nor sufficient {#removals-and-what-stops-them--return-agreements}

Countries without a formal UK returns agreement accounted for 49% of returns in 2025, and the Migration Observatory cites studies finding agreements had little effect on return rates in the EU. [13](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-13) Failing to obtain travel documents was the most common reason officials decided not to detain someone liable to removal, in a 2014/15 inspection. [12](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-12)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-fno-asylum}

### Foreign-offender returns are back near their 2016 peak {#removals-and-what-stops-them--fno-returns-near-peak}

Foreign offender returns averaged around 5,500 a year in 2010–2019, peaked at 6,437 in 2016, fell below 3,000 a year in 2020–2021, and in the year to June 2026 were 7% below the peak. [4](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-4) The Home Office attributes the fall after 2017 partly to a 2017 Supreme Court judgment that stopped removal before appeals were heard and a 2019 legal challenge that suspended charter flights, as well as the pandemic. [51](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-51)

### Asylum-related returns are at a record {#removals-and-what-stops-them--asylum-related-returns-at-record}

12,568 asylum-related returns in the year to June 2026 were the most since the series began in 2010, and 31% of all enforced and voluntary returns, up from 15% in 2021. [5](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-5) Of everyone who has arrived by small boat since 2018, 9,694 had been returned by June 2026. [6](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-6)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-cohort-counts}

### About half of refused claimants are recorded as leaving {#removals-and-what-stops-them--about-half-of-refused-claimants-recorded-leaving}

Of asylum claims made in 2010–2022 and refused by the end of 2025, around half had led to a recorded return. The share was around 72% for 2010 claims, 33% for 2018 claims and 48% for 2022 claims. [15](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-15) [16](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-16) The chart's own measure, which also counts withdrawn claims, shows the same shape: above half for claims made before 2014, around a fifth to a quarter for 2016–2021.

### Refusals now far outrun returns in any single year {#removals-and-what-stops-them--refusals-outrun-returns}

The government said 58,000 people had their asylum claim refused between June 2024 and June 2025, while fewer than 11,000 people were removed; the two counts are different groups of people, not a removal rate. [18](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-18) The Bill's impact assessment sets 446,000 claims in 2021–2025 against 33,000 asylum-related returns. [19](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-19)

### Not recorded leaving is not the same as still here {#removals-and-what-stops-them--not-recorded-leaving}

193,000 people who claimed asylum in 2010–2024 and were refused or withdrew had not been recorded leaving by January 2026. The Migration Observatory treats this as an upper bound, because some were later granted status or left unrecorded, including via Ireland. [71](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-71) [72](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-72)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-detention-leavers}

### Since 2015 more people leave detention on bail than are returned {#removals-and-what-stops-them--more-bailed-than-returned}

Before 2015 most people leaving detention were returned. In every year since, more have been bailed than returned, though the gap has narrowed. Returns from detention have since recovered: 46% of people leaving detention in the year to June 2026 were returned, against 25% in the year to June 2023. [55](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-55) The Home Office says people who claim asylum or raise other claims or appeals may be bailed while the claim is decided. [56](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-56)

### Detention is used less than a decade ago, but is rising {#removals-and-what-stops-them--detention-use}

Entries fell from 32,447 in 2015 to 24,480 in 2019, partly after the 2016 Shaw review. [57](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-57) 2,152 people were held at the end of June 2026, the most since June 2018. [54](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-54) In 2019 the National Audit Office found the Home Office released 14,900 (62%) of the detainees it intended to remove. [33](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-33)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-detention-issues}

### Late claims and judicial reviews, not human-rights claims, are the most common obstacles {#removals-and-what-stops-them--late-claims-not-human-rights-claims}

In 2019 people facing removal from detention after immigration offences raised at least one issue in 73% of detentions, and 94% of those were released. [20](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-20) The commonest issue was an asylum claim, in 47% of detentions, followed by medical reports and modern slavery referrals. [21](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-21) [23](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-23) Human-rights and other rights-based applications fell from 10% to 5% of detentions between 2017 and 2019, and judicial reviews from 20% to 9%. [22](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-22)

### Most issues raised in detention were eventually refused {#removals-and-what-stops-them--most-issues-refused}

Of issues raised in detentions ending in 2017, 12% led to a positive decision or concession, 83% did not, and 5% were still open. [28](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-28) When no issue was raised, 67% of detentions ended in return. [26](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-26)

### The 2022 figures are the government's only later update {#removals-and-what-stops-them--2022-update}

In January to September 2022, 86% of people who raised rights-based applications in detention were released, and more than 2 in 5 people detained for removal were referred as possible victims of modern slavery. The fuller update has not been published. [30](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-30) [31](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-31)

## Subtleties {#removals-and-what-stops-them--subtleties-detention-issues}

### Raised and then released is not caused by {#removals-and-what-stops-them--raised-is-not-caused}

The Home Office warns that it cannot say anyone was released because of an issue, only that they raised it and were later released. [27](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-27)

### The ECHR is under-counted by the "rights-based" row, but not absent from the others {#removals-and-what-stops-them--echr-hidden-in-other-rows}

Article 3 bars removal to torture or inhuman or degrading treatment in all circumstances, and that risk is usually argued inside an asylum or protection claim rather than as a separate rights-based application. [48](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-48) So the rights-based row is a floor for ECHR-based issues, and the asylum and judicial review rows are not ECHR-free. The data do not say how much of either rests on the ECHR.

### Foreign offenders raise different issues {#removals-and-what-stops-them--fno-issues-differ}

Among foreign offenders leaving detention in 2019, the commonest issue was an appeal (9% of detentions), then Rule 35 reports (8%), judicial reviews (7%) and asylum claims (6%). [29](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-29)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-charter-failures}

### Half of removal directions were cancelled in 2019 {#removals-and-what-stops-them--removal-directions-cancelled}

The Home Office set 25,700 removal directions in 2019 and cancelled 13,500 (52%), against 11% of removals failing in 2013. [32](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-32) [11](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-11) Immigration Enforcement blamed late or spurious asylum, medical and modern slavery claims; the National Audit Office saw no systematic analysis of why claims were increasing. [34](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-34) It listed other barriers: conflict at home, a long-established life in the UK (human-rights grounds), missing identity documents, and no returns agreement — but gave no breakdown by reason. [35](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-35)

### On the two Jamaica charters, fresh asylum claims and judicial reviews led {#removals-and-what-stops-them--charter-reasons}

The two flights planned 144 and 163 removals and removed 42 and 32. Fresh asylum claims after removal directions were set were the commonest reason for failure (55), then judicial reviews (35) and further representations (26). [38](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-38) [39](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-39) Caseworkers believed many late submissions had no merit and aimed to frustrate removal. [37](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-37) About a fifth of the failures in the top five reasons were people the Home Office did not find, or did not visit for lack of resources.

### Two in three planned foreign-offender removals succeeded {#removals-and-what-stops-them--planned-fno-removals}

Of 24,289 planned foreign-offender removals in 2014/15–2016/17, 16,517 (68%) succeeded and 7,772 (32%) failed. [36](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-36) In a review of 1,653 non-detained cases, almost 80% had at least one barrier to removal, including outstanding claims or missing travel documents; only 194 were judged removable. [40](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-40)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-fno-appeals}

### The 2014 Article 8 reforms cut allowed appeals, then the effect faded {#removals-and-what-stops-them--article-8-reforms-cut-then-faded}

Appeals allowed on human-rights grounds only rose to 337 in 2012/13, fell by roughly three-quarters to 92 in 2015/16 after the Immigration Act 2014, then climbed back to 285 in 2017/18. [41](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-41) The 2014 Act raised the bar for offenders' appeals while leaving judges some discretion. [49](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-49) The rebound coincides with the 2017 Supreme Court judgment that paused "deport first, appeal later", so the data cannot separate the two. [50](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-50)

### Human-rights wins equal about 3% of offender deportations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--human-rights-wins-about-3-percent}

From 2015/16 to 2020/21 about 1,000 foreign offenders won appeals on ECHR grounds alone, against 31,400 removed: 3%. [45](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-45) The Bonavero Institute's count for April 2016 to June 2021 is 922 against 26,091, or 3.5%, and around 2.5% for Article 8 alone. [43](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-43) [44](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-44) A sample found 70% of human-rights wins rested solely on Article 8. [42](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-42)

### Across all immigration cases, human-rights appeals usually succeed {#removals-and-what-stops-them--all-human-rights-appeals-53-percent}

Across all human-rights immigration appeals, not only deportations, judges allowed 61,000 of 115,000 human-rights appeals from 2015/16 to 2024/25 (53%). [47](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-47) These are mostly not removal cases, so they do not measure blocked removals, but they show the ECHR's reach in immigration decisions is wider than offender deportations.

## Subtleties {#removals-and-what-stops-them--subtleties-fno-appeals}

### The 3% is appeals won against deportations done, not blocked against attempted {#removals-and-what-stops-them--ratio-is-not-a-block-rate}

Offenders who never appeal, who win on mixed grounds (around 1,500 more), or who are never pursued because return is impossible are outside the ratio, and some First-tier wins were later overturned. [46](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-46) [43](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-43) The government says some offenders stay on Article 3 grounds, including over healthcare, but publishes no count. [58](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-58)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-fnos-community}

### Deportable offenders in the community have grown for a decade {#removals-and-what-stops-them--fnos-in-community-growing}

21,294 foreign offenders liable to deportation were living in the community at the end of June 2026, 12% more than a year earlier, with most of the latest rise after the Sentencing Act 2026 widened the duty to deport. [53](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-53) The Migration Observatory reads the earlier growth, 36% from the end of 2022 to March 2026, as a sign that removals have not kept pace with prison releases. [52](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-52) The government does not publish what share of offenders are deported. [51](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-51)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-removal-scale}

### 150,000 a year is about 15 times current enforced returns {#removals-and-what-stops-them--150000-about-15-times-enforced-returns}

The Conservative "Removals Force" pledge of 150,000 removals a year is about 15 times the 9,669 enforced returns in the year to June 2026, and well above the highest year of enforced returns in the series (2004). [60](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-60) [2](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-2) It is also more than all 27 EU states returned to non-EU countries in 2025. [64](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-64)

### The pledged budget covers a fraction of that at today's enforced-return cost {#removals-and-what-stops-them--pledged-budget}

The pledge comes with £1.6 billion. [61](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-61) At the 2024/25 average of £48,800 per enforced return, that would pay for about a fifth of 150,000 enforced returns; at £4,300 per voluntary return it would cover the target several times over. [14](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-14) For scale, 2,152 people were held at the end of June 2026. [54](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-54)

### The US comparison {#removals-and-what-stops-them--us-comparison}

The US deported about 980,000 people in the first 18 months of the second Trump term, but 300,000 of those followed arrests inside the country; most were border cases. [62](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-62) [63](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-63)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-france-pilot}

### The France pilot moved about 4% of arrivals {#removals-and-what-stops-them--france-pilot-about-4-percent}

By 30 June 2026, 1,087 people had been returned to France and 1,117 brought to the UK under the pilot. [65](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-65) 27,920 people crossed by small boat over the same period, so about 4% were sent back. [66](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-66) Monthly returns rose through spring 2026. The Home Office said on 30 September 2026 that the pilot was no longer processing new cases. [67](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-67) The Home Secretary put the cost at about £56,000 per return. [68](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-68)

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-unlawful-population}

### Hundreds of thousands, on estimates now nine years old {#removals-and-what-stops-them--unlawful-population-estimates}

The Home Office makes no official estimate of the population without lawful status. [70](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-70) The two latest estimates are for 2017, and Pew revised its range down in March 2025. [69](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-69) Both use the residual method: the foreign-born population minus an estimate of those here lawfully.

## Observations {#removals-and-what-stops-them--observations-echr-share}

### What share of failed removals the ECHR blocks is not measured {#removals-and-what-stops-them--echr-share-not-measured}

No published source counts removals stopped by the ECHR. The Home Office does not hold data on people granted status after being told they were liable to removal. [17](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-17) The proxies above each answer a narrower question, and both may understate the ECHR's weight. [21](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-21) [45](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-45)

### Other obstacles are documented alongside the legal ones {#removals-and-what-stops-them--other-obstacles}

The National Audit Office named conflict at home, missing identity documents and countries without return agreements alongside human-rights grounds. [35](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-35) A fifth of failures on the Jamaica charters were people the Home Office did not find or did not visit. [39](#source-removals-and-what-stops-them-39)
