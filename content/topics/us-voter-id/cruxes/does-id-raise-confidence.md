---
question: Would strict ID raise confidence in elections once who won is taken into account?
kind: prediction
divides: [prove-identity, turned-away, id-plus-access]
positions:
  - viewpoint: prove-identity
    holds: "Yes. Voters who see their identity checked have a reason to trust the count, and the one study with a direct confidence measure and party controls finds a large rise."
  - viewpoint: turned-away
    holds: "No. People mostly do not know their state's rule, the national studies find no effect, and confidence moves with who won."
  - viewpoint: id-plus-access
    holds: "Only if both sides accept the rule. A rule one party imposes raises confidence among its supporters and lowers it among its opponents, so the net effect depends on how it is passed."
---
[Confidence follows who holds
power](#fact-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections/confidence-follows-power), so a
rule's own effect is hard to see. [One study finds strict ID raised high
confidence in state elections](#fact-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections/milyo-finds-effect),
from few state changes; [two national studies find no
effect](#fact-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections/studies-find-no-effect); and
[voters trust results less under procedures they
oppose](#fact-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections/procedures-they-oppose).

If strict ID raises confidence on both sides, the confidence argument for photo ID
argument stands and the case for pairing ID with access need not trade anything for it. If it
raises confidence only among supporters, the case for pairing ID with access is right that the way
a rule is passed matters as much as the rule. If it moves nothing,
confidence is no reason for ID at all, and the case against these rules loses nothing by
opposing it.
