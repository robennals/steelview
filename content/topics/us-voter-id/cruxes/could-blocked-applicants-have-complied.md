question: Would a national proof-of-citizenship rule keep eligible citizens off the rolls in large numbers, or would they get the documents?
kind: prediction

positions:
  - viewpoint: prove-identity

  - viewpoint: turned-away

Kansas is the only large test, and its record cuts both ways. [The rule blocked
31,089 applicants against at most 39 non-citizens on the
rolls](#fact-burdens-of-id-and-citizenship-rules/kansas-blocked-vs-noncitizens),
but [the court found no evidence of how many of them lacked a
document](#fact-burdens-of-id-and-citizenship-rules/kansas-dpoc-possession-unknown).
Nationally, [about one adult citizen in ten cannot readily produce
proof](#fact-burdens-of-id-and-citizenship-rules/dpoc-not-readily-available).

If most of the blocked could have complied with a little more effort, a
records-first national rule would cost little, and the case against these rules loses its
strongest number. If many had no document they could reach, the SAVE America
Act's requirement would keep citizens off the rolls at scale, and
the case for proving identity would have to accept that cost openly. A follow-up of a
blocked-applicant list, asking who later registered and why the rest did not,
would settle much of it.
