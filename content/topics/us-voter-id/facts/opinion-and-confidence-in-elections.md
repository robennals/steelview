      Prior research demonstrates voters’ confidence in elections depends on
      whether their preferred candidate wins, but does agreement with the
      electoral rules being used also matter? Leveraging growing procedural
      polarization in election laws, public opinion data from 2008 to 2024,
      and a large-scale survey experiment conducted before the 2024 election,
      I show that agreement with election procedures matters for voter

      election results conducted using procedures they oppose. The effects are

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-pew-photo-id}

### Photo ID has majority support in both parties {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--photo-id-majorities}

Every Pew reading since 2018 shows large majorities of both parties in favour of photo ID (chart). [1](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-1) Gallup's October 2024 poll agrees, at 84% of adults. [5](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-5)

MIT's post-election survey of voters finds Republican support "nearly unanimous" (92.4% in 2024) and Democratic support rebounding after a dip. [10](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-10)

### Democratic support for photo ID has risen since 2021–22 {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--democrats-moved-toward-id}

In Pew's series, Democratic support was flat at 61% from 2021 to 2023 and then rose in 2024 and 2025 (chart). [3](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-3) [1](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-1) Gallup's Democrats dipped in 2022 and then rose 14 points by 2024. [6](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-6) [5](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-5)

Republican support stayed in the 90s throughout. The rise came while photo ID was being argued over in Congress and the states; the surveys do not establish why it happened.

### Support for photo ID crosses racial groups {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--photo-id-across-races}

Pew finds at least seven in ten adults in each racial and ethnic group it reports favour photo ID requirements (2025). [1](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-1) Among Democrats in 2021, White Democrats were the least supportive.

| Group | Favour photo ID |
| --- | --- |
| White adults (Gallup, 2024) | 86% [23](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-23) |
| Non-White adults (Gallup, 2024) | 80% [23](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-23) |
| White Democrats (Pew, 2021) | 54% [4](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-4) |
| Black Democrats (Pew, 2021) | 65% [4](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-4) |
| Hispanic Democrats (Pew, 2021) | 72% [4](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-4) |
| Asian Democrats (Pew, 2021) | 71% [4](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-4) |

### Access measures divide the parties more than ID does {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--access-measures-divide-more}

Automatic registration splits the parties far more than photo ID, and Republican support for it fell after 2018 and has not recovered (chart). [1](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-1) [4](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-4)

No-excuse mail voting splits them further: 83% of Democrats and 32% of Republicans favoured it in 2025. [1](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-1) Of the access measures Pew asks about, only early in-person voting has majority support in both parties.

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-pew-early-voting}

### Republican support for early voting rose in 2025 {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--republicans-moved-toward-early-voting}

In Pew's series, Republican support for two weeks of early in-person voting was nearly flat from 2021 to January 2024, then rose in August 2025 (chart). [1](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-1)

Gallup's differently worded item runs the other way over a longer period: Republican support fell between 2016 and 2024 while Democrats' stayed high (Gallup chart below). [5](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-5) [6](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-6) Read together, the two series show Republican support falling after 2016 and partly recovering by 2025.

### Both majorities exist, but how many back both is not published {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--overlap-not-published}

Photo ID (83%) and two weeks of early voting (80%) were asked of the same half-sample in August 2025. [1](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-1) [2](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-2) Pew does not publish how many respondents favoured both. The two shares alone guarantee that a large majority backed both, but not the exact size of that group; see Data still needed.

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-gallup-photo-id}

### Gallup's series shows the same pattern from 2016 {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--gallup-policy-trend}

In Gallup's three readings, photo ID and early voting each have the support of at least three in four adults, and automatic registration less (chart). [5](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-5) [6](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-6) [7](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-7) The partisan gap on photo ID widened from 32 to 44 points between 2016 and 2022 before narrowing again. [6](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-6)

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-proof-of-citizenship-by-group}

### Proof of citizenship at registration has the same level of support as photo ID {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--proof-of-citizenship-support}

Support for requiring proof of citizenship at first registration matches support for photo ID, and divides by party and race in the same way (chart). [5](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-5) [23](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-23)

Gallup asked the question for the first time in 2024, so there is no trend. The question does not say what documents would count, which is where the dispute over such rules lies (see [the burdens of ID and citizenship rules](#fact-burdens-of-id-and-citizenship-rules)).

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-gallup-ineligible-voting}

### The parties fear different failures, and the gap has grown on one side {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--different-fears}

Republicans have grown more likely to expect ineligible voting to be a major problem, and Democrats less likely (chart). [8](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-8) Concern that eligible voters will be blocked is similar in both parties, apart from a Democratic spike in 2020 (next chart). [8](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-8)

In open-ended answers in October 2026, 19% of Republican voters raised illegitimate or fraudulent votes. [14](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-14)

Democratic voters more often raised legitimate voters being denied access (10%) or interference by the Trump administration (11%). [14](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-14)

### Both goals are nearly universal {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--shared-goals}

90% of adults said in April 2026 that it is important that "no eligible voters are prevented from voting" and 85% that "no ineligible voters are permitted to vote". [13](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-13) The parties disagree about which goal current elections fall short of, not about whether both matter.

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-pew-2026-eligibility-confidence}

### Republicans doubt noncitizens will be stopped; Democrats doubt every eligible voter can vote {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--eligibility-confidence-2026}

Each side is least confident in the safeguard the other side worries about less (chart). [14](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-14) Republican confidence that noncitizens will be stopped is up from 35% among Trump voters in 2024. [14](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-14)

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-gallup-accuracy-party}

### Confidence in the count follows the party in power {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--confidence-follows-power}

Republicans were more confident than Democrats under George W. Bush, the two converged in 2008, and Democrats were more confident under Barack Obama and Joe Biden (changes of president are marked under the chart). [8](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-8) [9](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-9) The gap reached a record 56 points in 2024. [8](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-8)

A study pooling about 30 national surveys from 2000 to 2012 finds "a consistent relationship between voting for the winner and confidence". It attributes the decade's fall in national confidence almost entirely to "a forty-point decline among Republicans". [11](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-11)

### The exceptions point to what leaders say, not only who wins {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--exceptions-2008-2020}

Gallup names two exceptions. In 2008 both parties stood at 57%, and in 2020 Republican confidence fell under a Republican president "amid then-President Donald Trump's public questioning of the validity of mail voting" (marked on the chart). [9](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-9)

A study of 2016 finds the winner effect outweighed pre-election "rigged" messaging from elites. [22](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-22) In 2018 there was essentially no winner effect at all. [12](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-12)

### The national level has not collapsed; the share with no confidence has grown {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--level-versus-not-at-all}

More than half of Americans have been confident in every Gallup reading since 2004 (chart), most of all in 2004 and 2006. [9](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-9) But the share "not at all confident" rose from 6% in 2004 to 19% in 2024. [8](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-8)

Confidence in one's own polling place is higher: 76% in 2024. [8](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-8)

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-spae-nationwide-party}

### After each election the winner's side becomes more confident {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--post-election-winner-swing}

MIT's post-election survey of voters shows the swing directly: each party's confidence in the nationwide count rises after its candidate wins the presidency and falls after a loss (chart). [10](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-10)

Earlier work on 2000–2012 put the average rise among winners at about 20 points for confidence in one's own vote, against a fall of about 2 points among losers. [11](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-11)

In 2016 Democrats went from 15 points more confident than Republicans before the election to 13 points less confident after it. [12](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-12)

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-pew-fair-accurate-party}

### Ahead of the 2026 midterm, there is no partisan gap in Pew's measure {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--no-gap-2026}

In July 2026 Republicans and Democrats were about equally confident that the November elections would be conducted fairly and accurately (chart). [13](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-13)

Republican confidence rose and Democratic confidence fell after the change of administration (marked under the chart). The convergence came from both sides moving, not from either party's confidence reaching earlier highs. [13](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-13)

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-pew-count-by-mail}

### Confidence in mail ballots is lower and has fallen among Democrats in 2026 {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--mail-versus-in-person}

Voters are less confident in the mail count than the in-person count, and Democratic confidence in mail ballots fell sharply in 2026 while Republicans' edged up (chart). [14](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-14)

Confidence follows voters' own method: 76% of mail and absentee voters are confident in the mail count, against 47% of in-person voters. [14](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-14) Mail-ballot rules are covered in a separate topic.

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-gallup-honesty-total}

### US confidence in election honesty has been low by this measure for two decades {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--honesty-long-run}

US confidence in the honesty of elections has averaged 45% since 2006, with its low in 2016 (chart). [15](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-15) This is a different question from Gallup's domestic vote-count item, and gives lower figures.

Party was recorded only from 2024. That year the Democratic–Republican gap was 55 points; in 2025 it closed to 4 points. [15](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-15)

In 2026 it reopened: 63% of Democrats were confident against 37% of Republicans. [15](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-15)

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-leadership-gap-by-administration}

### The approver–disapprover gap held for three presidents, widened under Biden and vanished in 2025 {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--approval-gap-by-administration}

Americans who approved of national leadership were the more confident group under Bush, Obama and Trump's first term; under Biden the gap widened, and since 2025 it has vanished (chart). [15](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-15)

Gallup attributes the collapse partly to approvers changing from mostly Democrats to mostly Republicans, and partly to Democratic confidence falling and Republican confidence rising under Trump. [15](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-15)

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-winner-gap-worldwide}

### A winner–loser gap is the global norm; the US is now the exception {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--global-winner-gap}

Across more than 100 countries, approvers of their national leadership are far more confident in election honesty than disapprovers (chart). [15](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-15) In 2026 the US gap ran the other way, which Gallup says no other country has shown in 15 years. [15](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-15)

### Experts rate US counting well even as public confidence divides {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--expert-ratings}

The Electoral Integrity Project's expert survey found US integrity scores fell in 2024, but rated "the counting, results and procedural aspects of the election" strong; the weak areas were "electoral boundaries, media and finance". [16](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-16) Expert scores measure the process, not public belief, and do not address whether ineligible people voted.

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-georgia-statewide-count}

### Georgia's confidence rebound came on the side that won {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--georgia-rebound}

Republican confidence in Georgia's statewide count rebounded in 2022, the first general election under SB 202 (marked under the chart), while Democratic confidence dipped. [17](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-17)

The authors caution that the rebound may reflect "the noted winner's effect", since the Georgia GOP won every statewide office except a Senate seat in 2022. [17](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-17) Voting itself went smoothly: 1.1% of respondents reported a problem casting a ballot. [17](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-17)

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-sb202-effect-on-confidence}

### SB 202 raised confidence for most Republicans and lowered it for a plurality of Democrats {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--sb202-self-reported}

Most Republicans said SB 202 had increased their confidence; a plurality of Democrats said it had decreased theirs (chart). [17](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-17) A rule passed by one party raised confidence on that party's side and lowered it on the other.

## Observations {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--observations-id-and-confidence-studies}

### One study finds strict ID raises confidence {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--milyo-finds-effect}

Milyo finds that adopting strict ID raised the probability of high confidence by 14 percentage points, "a 25% increase relative to the mean", using a direct question about the honesty and integrity of state elections and controlling for party. [18](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-18)

He notes as "an important limitation" that few states adopted or removed strict ID in the period. [18](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-18)

### Two national studies find no effect on confidence {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--studies-find-no-effect}

Stewart, Ansolabehere and Persily find that people in states with ID laws "do not have greater confidence in elections or perceive lower rates of voter impersonation fraud", and that many people do not know whether their state has such a law. [19](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-19)

Cantoni and Pons find strict ID has "no effect on fraud – actual or perceived", alongside no effect on turnout. [20](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-20) Milyo attributes his different result to the direct confidence measure and longer period, not to his party controls. [18](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-18)

### Agreement with the rules matters as well as who wins {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--procedures-they-oppose}

Clinton finds voters "less likely to express confidence in election results conducted using procedures they oppose", beyond the effects of partisan control and outcomes, with "particularly large" effects for voter ID laws and among Republicans and independents. [21](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-21)

This fits the Georgia pattern. It suggests a rule raises confidence among its supporters and lowers it among its opponents, so its net effect on confidence depends on how widely it is supported.

## About this data {#opinion-and-confidence-in-elections--about}

**What this measures.** These are opinion surveys of adults or registered voters. Each pollster's figures are shown in its own series, because Pew counts independents who lean to a party as partisans and Gallup does not.

**Wording differs.** Gallup asks about "photo identification at their voting place", Pew about "government-issued photo identification".

**What it does not measure.** No series measures whether a given rule changed anyone's confidence; the four studies above take that up. Opinion polls are commonly read as evidence about fraud or access themselves; they measure belief, not the rate of either. For what is actually found, see [the burdens of ID and citizenship rules](#fact-burdens-of-id-and-citizenship-rules) and [non-citizens on voter rolls](#fact-non-citizens-on-voter-rolls).

**How it compares.** The only consistent cross-country measure here is Gallup's World Poll, which asks the same honesty question in more than 100 countries and publishes the global median. No source found gives per-country support for photo ID in the same wording, so support for ID rules has no international comparison.

**Margin of error.** Gallup's telephone polls have a margin of error of about ±4 points, so year-to-year moves of a few points in its policy series are within noise. Gallup itself calls Republicans' fall in early-voting support from 60% to 57% "not significantly different". [5](#source-opinion-and-confidence-in-elections-5)
