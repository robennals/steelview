Democracy works only if the losing side accepts defeat and waits for the next election. It will do that only if it trusts the result. That is why results the losers can accept matter more than any one rule.

### Distrust in the count is growing, and splits by party

The share of Americans with no confidence at all in the vote has roughly tripled in 20 years, to about one in five. Most are still confident, but the doubt is split by side: in 2024 the gap between the parties hit a record 56 points, and after each presidential election, the winning side grows more confident and the losing side less. So at any time, much of one side doubts the count.

### The people who run elections now work under threat, and most say false claims make it worse

About a third of local election officials report threats, harassment or abuse because of their job, and three in four say false information about elections makes their job harder or more dangerous. That is the officials' own account, and no survey measures what caused any threat. But a count that many people doubt still has to be run by someone, and those people now face that doubt directly.

### Each side fears a different failure, but nearly everyone wants both prevented

Republicans doubt non-citizens will be stopped, and Democrats are less sure than Republicans that every eligible voter can vote. Republicans have also grown more likely to expect ineligible people to vote. Yet at least 85% of Americans call each goal important. A rule that answers one fear and ignores the other gives half the country a reason to reject a defeat.

### Beyond who wins, rules passed by one side lower the other side's trust

In Georgia, most Republicans said the 2021 election law raised their trust, and the largest group of Democrats said it lowered theirs. Across states, voters trust results less when they oppose the rules used, even allowing for who won, and especially for voter ID. So the rules are one part of trust that lawmakers can change. Each party's national bill would split the country on a bigger scale: one would require proof of citizenship and photo ID, the other would have required early voting and made ID states accept alternatives, and both stalled in the Senate.

### Most Americans back both photo ID and early voting

83% of adults back photo ID, and 80% back two weeks of early voting. No poll publishes how many back both, but with shares that high, at least six in ten must. Support crosses party lines: large majorities in both parties back photo ID, Democratic support is higher than when Pew first asked in 2018, and Republican support for two weeks of early voting has risen since Pew first asked in 2021.

### So both parties should pass free ID and early voting together

The two already go together: eight of the 10 strict photo-ID states also let everyone vote early, starting two to four weeks before Election Day. Congress, or states one by one, should pass both: free photo ID, at least two weeks of early voting, and a sworn statement for those without ID, as a commission led by Democrat Jimmy Carter and Republican James Baker paired photo ID with free cards. How the rule is written matters: Texas's ID-number rule rejected more mail ballots, and Georgia's fewer. Studies disagree on whether ID itself raises trust, and trust will still follow winning. But a rule both sides passed takes away the losers' complaint that it was rigged against them.
