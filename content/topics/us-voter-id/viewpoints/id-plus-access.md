An election settles a contest only if the losing side accepts the result. Each side now fears a different way of losing unfairly, and rules written by one side alone confirm the other side's fear. The answer is one bargain that guards against both.

### Doubt about the count is growing, and it splits by party

A growing share of Americans do not trust the count. The share with no confidence at all in the vote has roughly tripled in 20 years, to about one in five. Most are still confident, but in 2024 the gap between the parties hit a record 56 points. After each presidential election the winning side grows more confident and the losing side less, so at any time much of one side doubts the result.

### The people who run elections face threats and harassment

The people who run the count work under threat. About a third of local election officials report threats, harassment or abuse because of their job, and almost a quarter worry about being assaulted. In their own view, false claims make this worse: three in four worry that false information about elections makes their job harder or more dangerous.

### Each side fears a different failure, and nearly everyone wants both prevented

The two sides doubt different things. Republicans doubt that non-citizens will be stopped from voting, and Democrats are less sure than Republicans that every eligible voter can vote. Yet at least 85% of Americans call each goal important. A rule that answers one fear and ignores the other gives half the country a reason to reject a defeat.

### Rules passed by one side lower the other side's trust

Rules make this worse when one side writes them alone. In Georgia, most Republicans said the 2021 election law raised their trust, and the largest group of Democrats said it lowered theirs. Across states, voters trust results less when they oppose the rules used, even allowing for who won, and most of all for voter ID. Each party's national bill was written by one side: one would require proof of citizenship and photo ID, the other would have required early voting and made ID states accept alternatives. Neither has passed the Senate.

### Most Americans back both photo ID and early voting

A bargain is there to be had. 83% of adults back photo ID, and 80% back two weeks of early voting. No poll publishes how many back both, but with shares that high, at least six in ten must. Large majorities in both parties back photo ID, and Democratic support is higher than when Pew first asked in 2018. Republican support for two weeks of early voting has risen since Pew first asked in 2021.

### So both parties should pass free ID and early voting together

Most strict-ID states already do both: eight of the 10 strict photo-ID states let everyone vote early, starting two to four weeks before Election Day. A commission led by Democrat Jimmy Carter and Republican James Baker paired photo ID with free cards. Congress, or states one by one, should pass both: free photo ID, at least two weeks of early voting, and a sworn statement for anyone without ID.

How the rule is written matters: Texas's ID-number rule rejected more mail ballots, and Georgia's fewer. Studies disagree on whether ID itself raises trust, and trust has long followed winning. But a rule both sides passed takes away the losers' complaint that it was rigged against them.
