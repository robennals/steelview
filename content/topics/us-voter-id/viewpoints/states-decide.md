The Constitution settles who writes election rules. Each state's voters should choose their own ID and registration rules, and presidents should stay out.

### The Constitution gives election rules to the states and Congress, not the President

[Each state's legislature decides when, where and how congressional elections are run, and Congress may change those rules. The President has no power to set them by order](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/elections-clause-text). [Who may vote is for the states to decide](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/not-who-may-vote).

This keeps [decisions close to the people](#principle-decisions-made-closest-to-the-people) who live with them. The officials who run a polling place answer to the voters who use it. If a state's voters dislike its ID rule, they can elect lawmakers who will change it. That is [democratic consent](#principle-democratic-consent-over-membership) at the level where it counts most.

### Presidents should have no part in setting election rules

[Two federal courts have ruled that the 2025 executive order's proof-of-citizenship rule goes beyond a president's power](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/eo-14248-blocked). That is right, whatever one thinks of proof of citizenship. A rule one president can impose by order, the next can cancel by order. Election rules would then change with every new president.

While [the Supreme Court let the 2026 order's citizenship lists go ahead](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/eo-14399-allowed), it did so because [states do not have to use them](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/eo-14399-allowed). And [it did not rule on whether the order is lawful](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/no-merits-ruling). As long as states can ignore the lists, the choice stays with them.

### States choose differently, and a mistake stays in one state

[States differ on whether ID must have a photo, and on what happens to a voter without one](#fact-voter-id-rules-in-the-us-and-abroad/rules-vary-widely). [Voter ID began in South Carolina in 1950, and states led by both parties had adopted it by 2000](#fact-voter-id-rules-in-the-us-and-abroad/early-adopters). A bad rule in one state stays in that state. And [state courts can review it under the state's own constitution](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/moore-state-courts).

### National bills from either party would take away the states' choice

[The Republican bill would require ID and proof of citizenship. The Democratic one would have required early voting and limited which ID states could ask for](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/freedom-to-vote-act-provisions). [Both stalled because they could not get 60 votes in the Senate](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/national-bills-stalled). Either would force one side's choice on every state, and the next majority would force its own.

### Congress should protect the right to vote, and leave the details to the states

While Congress has real power here, it has used it only for a few national minimum rules. [The Supreme Court calls its power over federal elections "paramount"](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-power-paramount). [Congress has set national minimum rules for registering and voting several times since 1965](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-has-set-national-floors). Some were plainly needed. [Before the Voting Rights Act, Black registration in the Deep South barely grew](#fact-election-fraud-and-voter-exclusion-history/pre-vra-stagnation), and [after it the gaps closed or nearly closed](#fact-election-fraud-and-voter-exclusion-history/registration-gap-closed). Where a state shuts citizens out, national power is the answer.

Which ID a voter shows, and how a state checks citizenship, is a different question. It is the everyday business of running an election. [The national laws Congress has passed leave it to each state](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-has-set-national-floors), apart from [a narrow ID rule for first-time voters who registered by mail](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/hava-id-rule). Congress should keep to protecting the right to vote, presidents should stay out, and each state's voters should decide the rest.
