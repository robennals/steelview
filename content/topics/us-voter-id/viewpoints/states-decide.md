The Constitution settles who writes election rules. Each state's voters should choose their own ID and registration rules, and presidents should stay out.

### The Constitution gives election rules to the states and Congress, not the President

[Each state's legislature decides when, where and how congressional elections are run, and Congress may change those rules](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/elections-clause-text). The clause names no role for the President. [Congress's power covers how elections are held, not who may vote](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/not-who-may-vote).

So who may vote is for the states to decide. This keeps decisions close to the people who live with them. If a state's voters dislike its ID rule, they can elect lawmakers who will change it. That is democratic consent where it counts most.

### A president has no power to set voting rules by order

[Two federal courts have permanently blocked the 2025 order's proof-of-citizenship rule](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/two-courts-blocked-2025-order). [One held that it goes beyond a president's power](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/two-courts-blocked-2025-order). That is right, whatever one thinks of the rule. A rule one president can impose by order, the next can cancel.

While [the Supreme Court let the 2026 order's citizenship lists go ahead](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/eo-14399-allowed), [that order requires nothing of anyone outside the executive branch](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/order-binds-no-state). [It did not rule on whether the order is lawful](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/no-merits-ruling). If states can ignore the lists, the choice stays theirs.

### States choose differently, so a mistake stays in one state

[States differ on whether ID must have a photo, and on what happens to a voter without it](#fact-voter-id-rules-in-the-us-and-abroad/rules-vary-widely). A bad rule in one state stays in that state, and voters elsewhere are not bound by it. [State courts can review a state's election rules](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/moore-state-courts), so voters there have a check close to home.

### National bills from either party would take away the states' choice

[The Republican bill would require proof of citizenship and photo ID](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/save-america-act-provisions). [The Democratic one would have required early voting and made ID states accept alternatives](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/freedom-to-vote-act-provisions). Either would force one side's choice on every state, and the next majority would force its own.

[Both stalled short of the 60 votes needed in the Senate](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/national-bills-stalled). [The Democrats' bill got 49 votes, and a photo-ID version of the Republicans' got 52](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/national-bills-stalled).

### So Congress should protect the right to vote and leave the rest to the states

Some national rules were plainly needed. [Before the Voting Rights Act, Black registration in three Deep South states rose about five points or less](#fact-election-fraud-and-voter-exclusion-history/pre-vra-stagnation). Congress should keep to rules like these, which protect the right to vote, and leave the rest, including ID, to each state's voters. [Congress has set national minimum rules several times since 1965](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-has-set-national-floors), and while [the Supreme Court calls Congress's power "paramount"](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-power-paramount), [none of the three laws in force requires ID or proof of citizenship of every voter](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/laws-require-no-id-for-all).
