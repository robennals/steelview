---
name: Election rules belong to the states, and presidents have no say in them
summary: "[The Constitution gives each state's legislature the first say over how elections are run and Congress the power to alter it, and gives the President no such power](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/elections-clause-text). Each state's voters should choose their own ID and registration rules, and executive orders that try to set them should fail."
order: 5
---
The Constitution settles who writes election rules. [The times, places and manner of congressional elections are set by each state's legislature, and Congress may alter them; the clause gives the President no power to set them by order](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/elections-clause-text). [Who may vote is for the states to decide](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/not-who-may-vote).

That arrangement puts [decisions closest to the people](#principle-decisions-made-closest-to-the-people) who live with them. The officials who run a polling place answer to the voters who use it. A state's voters who dislike its ID rule can elect a legislature that changes it, which is [democratic consent](#principle-democratic-consent-over-membership) at the scale where it is most real.

Presidents should have no part in this. [Two federal courts have held the 2025 executive order's proof-of-citizenship section beyond presidential power](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/eo-14248-blocked). That is right whatever one thinks of proof of citizenship. A rule one president can impose by order, the next can repeal by order, and elections would change with every administration.

The 2026 order has fared differently: [the Supreme Court let its citizenship lists proceed, as an internal directive states need not use](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/eo-14399-allowed). But [the Court made no ruling on whether the order is lawful](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/no-merits-ruling), and as long as states may ignore the lists, the choice stays theirs.

States have used that choice, and choose differently. [Rules differ on whether ID must carry a photo and on what happens to a voter without it](#fact-voter-id-rules-in-the-us-and-abroad/rules-vary-widely). [Voter ID began in South Carolina in 1950, and states under both parties had adopted it by 2000](#fact-voter-id-rules-in-the-us-and-abroad/early-adopters).

A mistaken rule in one state stays in one state, and [state courts can review it under the state's own constitution](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/moore-state-courts).

Both parties now want to take the choice away. [The Republican bill mandates ID and proof of citizenship; the Democratic one would have mandated early voting and limited what ID states could ask for](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/freedom-to-vote-act-provisions). [Both stalled at the Senate's 60-vote threshold](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/national-bills-stalled). Either would impose one coalition's preference on every state, and the next majority would impose its own.

Congress does have real power here. [The Supreme Court calls its power over federal elections "paramount"](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-power-paramount), and [Congress has set national floors for registration and voting several times since 1965](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-has-set-national-floors).

Some of those floors were plainly needed. [Before the Voting Rights Act, Black registration in the Deep South barely moved](#fact-election-fraud-and-voter-exclusion-history/pre-vra-stagnation), and [after it the gaps closed or nearly closed](#fact-election-fraud-and-voter-exclusion-history/registration-gap-closed). Where a state shuts citizens out of the vote, national power is the remedy.

Which document a voter shows, and how a state confirms citizenship, is a different kind of question. It is the ordinary business of running an election, and [the national laws Congress has passed leave it to each state](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-has-set-national-floors), apart from [a narrow rule for first-time voters who registered by mail](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/hava-id-rule).

Congress should keep to protecting the right to vote, presidents should keep out entirely, and each state's voters should settle the rest.
