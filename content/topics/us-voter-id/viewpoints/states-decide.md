The Constitution settles who writes election rules. Each state's voters should choose their own ID and registration rules, and presidents should stay out.

### The Constitution gives election rules to the states and Congress, not the President

[Each state's legislature decides when, where and how congressional elections are run, and Congress may change those rules](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/elections-clause-text). The clause names no role for the President. [Congress's power covers how elections are held, not who may vote](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/not-who-may-vote), so who may vote is for the states to decide.

This keeps [decisions close to the people](#principle-decisions-made-closest-to-the-people) who live with them. If a state's voters dislike its ID rule, they can elect lawmakers who will change it. That is [democratic consent](#principle-democratic-consent-over-membership) at the level where it counts most.

### Courts have blocked a president's proof-of-citizenship rule

[Two federal courts have permanently blocked the 2025 order's proof-of-citizenship rule, and one held that it goes beyond a president's power](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/two-courts-blocked-2025-order). That is right, whatever one thinks of proof of citizenship. A rule one president can impose by order, the next can cancel.

While [the Supreme Court let the 2026 order's citizenship lists go ahead](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/eo-14399-allowed), it did so because [the order requires nothing of anyone outside the executive branch](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/order-binds-no-state), and [it did not rule on the order's lawfulness](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/no-merits-ruling). If states can ignore the lists, the choice stays theirs.

### States choose differently, so a mistake stays in one state

[States differ on whether ID must have a photo, and on how strictly they require it](#fact-voter-id-rules-in-the-us-and-abroad/rules-vary-widely). [Voter ID began in South Carolina in 1950](#fact-voter-id-rules-in-the-us-and-abroad/early-adopters), and [states led by both parties had adopted it by 2000](#fact-voter-id-rules-in-the-us-and-abroad/early-adopters). A bad rule in one state stays in that state. And [state courts can review it](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/moore-state-courts).

### National bills from either party would take away the states' choice

[The Republican bill would require proof of citizenship and photo ID](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/save-america-act-provisions). [The Democratic one would have required early voting and made ID states accept alternatives](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/freedom-to-vote-act-provisions). [Both stalled short of the 60 votes needed in the Senate: 49 for the Democrats' bill, 52 for a photo-ID version of the Republicans'](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/national-bills-stalled). Either would force one side's choice on every state, and the next majority would force its own.

### Congress should leave most election rules to the states

Some [national minimum rules Congress has set since 1965](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-has-set-national-floors) were plainly needed: [Black registration in three Deep South states rose about five points or less in the years before the Voting Rights Act](#fact-election-fraud-and-voter-exclusion-history/pre-vra-stagnation).

While [the Supreme Court calls Congress's power "paramount"](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/congress-power-paramount), Congress has used it only for a few such rules. [None of the three laws in force requires ID or proof of citizenship of every voter](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/laws-require-no-id-for-all), apart from [a narrow ID rule for first-time mail registrants](#fact-federal-and-state-power-over-voting-rules/hava-id-rule). Congress should keep to protecting the right to vote, and each state's voters should decide the rest.
