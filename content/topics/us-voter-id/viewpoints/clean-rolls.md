The weak point in American elections is the voter list, not the line at the polls. Fraud has used the names of the dead and departed left on the list, and non-citizens show up in government files, not at the door. A clean list guards against both, without asking honest voters to prove anything.

### Voter lists hold many out-of-date names, and fraud has used them

Voter lists carry names that should not be there. The only national estimate, from data to March 2011, found about one record in eight wrong or out of date, including dead people and people registered in two states. Federal law keeps old names a long time: removing someone for a move can take through two federal general elections. While most wrong records belong to eligible voters with old details, a stale name is one someone else can use. Chicago's 1982 fraud cast ballots in the names of people who had died or moved.

### Most fraud on record went around the check at the polls

Most fraud on record never came near the check at the door. In 14 of 20 recorded cases, a polling-place ID check would not have reached it: officials changed ballots, ballots went by mail, or real voters sold their votes. Where people did vote in others' names at the polls, the officials checking were often part of it, and they are the people who would check ID. A name taken off the list cannot be used by mail or in person, whoever is checking.

### Non-citizens are found in government records, not at the door

A photo ID shows who a voter is, not whether they are a citizen. Georgia, Iowa, Louisiana, Ohio and Kansas each found non-citizens on their lists, and they found them in government records: transportation files, immigration records and sworn excusals from jury duty. Georgia checked applicants before they reached the list, and held back those its records could not verify. The check that works happens at registration, against records the state already holds.

### Matching must be careful, because it also flags eligible voters

Records checks help only if they are right, and many have not been. In every program with a published follow-up, a large share of those flagged turned out to be citizens. Eleven voters flagged in Travis County, Texas, had already given the state licence agency proof of citizenship, so the state's own records would have cleared them. Challengers say the government has not released an error rate for the federal SAVE database. Removals for moving have the same problem: in Ohio, more of those who answered a notice said they had not moved than said they had.

### Finding voters who moved needs states to share records, and nine have stopped

Moving is one of the main reasons names come off the lists, and a move to another state shows up in the other state's records. ERIC is a group of states that share voter records to find such moves. By October 2023, nine Republican-led states had left it. A match needs both states as members, and ERIC's leads on voters who moved between states fell from about 3.5 million in 2021 to about 0.7 million a year or fewer since 2024. ERIC does not say how much of that fall comes from states leaving. Iowa objected to its rules and Virginia cited data security: reasons to fix the rules or build another match, not to stop matching.

### So states should clean their lists early, carefully, and together

States should check their own records before writing to anyone, and publish each program's error rate. They should give flagged voters time and a simple way to answer. They should finish large clean-ups early: federal law bars them in the 90 days before a federal election, and courts have stopped late ones. A state that wants clean lists should rejoin ERIC, or build an equally wide match. Then neither a stale name nor a non-citizen gets a ballot, and no eligible citizen has to prove again what the state already knows.
