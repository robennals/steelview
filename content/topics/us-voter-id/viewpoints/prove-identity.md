Only citizens may vote, and each only once. A secret ballot cannot be taken back out of the box, so the only check that works is the one made before the vote. Yet the federal registration form takes a person's word that they are a citizen.

### People have stolen votes by voting in other people's names

Voting in someone else's name is not a myth. Tammany Hall paid "repeaters" to vote several times each, and Chicago's 1982 fraud cast ballots in the names of voters who had died or moved. The frauds in Kansas City and Chicago led to hundreds of convictions. Modern counts find few cases, but a vote cast in the name of someone dead or departed leaves no one to complain, and the counts are of detected cases only. While most frauds on record were done by officials or by mail, that is a reason to guard those routes too, not to leave this one open.

### Where impersonation was organised, ID checks all but ended it, without lowering turnout

Northern Ireland is the test case. In the early 1980s, people there voted in other people's names on a planned and organised scale. It brought in ID in 1985 and photo ID in 2003. By 2005, tendered ballots, which are issued when someone has already voted in a person's name, were down 94% on 1983, and there was no evidence of a fall in turnout. That count does not wait on a prosecution: it is the trace a stolen vote leaves. While the scale of the fraud was never measured, the trace of it all but vanished.

### Non-citizens get onto the voter lists, and a sworn statement does not stop them

Georgia, Iowa, Louisiana, Ohio and Kansas each found non-citizens on their voter lists. The reviews that give a cause trace them to mistakes, such as clerks registering people who had ticked "not a citizen". A sworn statement does not catch a mistake. Every vote a non-citizen casts cancels a citizen's, and Louisiana's top election official says some local races are decided by a handful of votes. While the votes found are far fewer than the margins in recent close races, the reviews find only people some government record already shows as non-citizens.

### Few citizens lack photo ID, and the laws have not measurably cut turnout nationwide

A photo ID rule asks little of nearly everyone. Just over 1% of adult citizens have no photo ID of any kind. Many more have a licence with an old address, which a rule accepting any current photo ID does not touch. A national study of voter records found strict ID laws had no clear effect on registration or turnout, for any group. While Texas's ID-number rule for mail ballots rejected one in eight in its first primary, under Georgia's rejections fell. The design decides the cost, which is why the ID should be free and the rule simple.

### Check citizenship against records first, and ask for papers only when they fail

Proving citizenship need not mean asking every new applicant for a citizenship document. Kansas tried that, and blocked 31,089 applicants against 39 non-citizens confirmed on its rolls. Georgia instead checks government records, and held back 1,634 applicants those records could not verify before they reached the list. The federal government says it has now checked more than 65 million voters in 26 states against its records. While records can be wrong about a citizen, a records check asks for papers only from the few it cannot confirm.

### So states should ask for free photo ID, and check citizenship against records

This is not one side's rule. Large majorities in both parties back photo ID, and 83% of adults favour proof of citizenship when people first register. States should ask for photo ID at the polls, free to anyone without one, as Georgia offers. They should check every new registrant's citizenship against government records, ask for documents only when the records fail, and give people time to answer. That protects an honest voter twice: from having their vote cancelled, and from being turned away.
