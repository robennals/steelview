Voters should prove who they are, and that they are citizens.

### Fraud at the polls has happened, and when it works it leaves no trace

People have voted several times, or in other people's names, and frauds in Kansas City and Chicago led to hundreds of convictions. When a vote in someone else's name works, no one is left to complain. So while modern counts find few cases of voting as someone else at the polls, those are only the cases that were caught. A bipartisan commission said good ID could deter this fraud. While 14 of the 20 recorded cases were done by officials or by mail, that is a reason to guard those routes too, not to leave the polls open.

### State reviews keep finding non-citizens on voter lists

Only citizens may vote. Yet all five state reviews charted found or suspected non-citizens on the voter list, and Ohio referred apparent non-citizens for prosecution in 2019, 2021, 2022 and 2024. A photo ID at the polls shows who someone is, not whether they are a citizen. The reviews that give a cause trace non-citizens on the lists to mistakes, and the federal registration form asks people only to swear that they are citizens. So the check at registration is where to fix it.

### Even a few illegal votes matter when races turn on a handful

Louisiana's top election official says some local elections are decided by a handful of votes. In a race that close, a few votes that should not count can change who wins. A rule that is broken rarely still has to be enforced.

The non-citizen votes found are at most about 22 per million ballots. While they are far fewer than the margins in recent close races, they are only the votes found. The checks rest on government records, so others may go unseen.

### Where photo ID came in after organised fraud, suspected fraud fell and turnout held

Northern Ireland brought in ID after organised voting in other people's names. Once it required photo ID, suspected personation fell sharply, and there was no evidence of a fall in turnout.

The American evidence is less clear. A national study found strict ID had no effect on fraud, actual or perceived. But a study can count only the fraud that is found. Northern Ireland's fall showed up in a measure that does not wait on a prosecution: ballots issued when a real voter found someone had already voted in their name.

### Few citizens lack photo ID, and strict ID laws show no clear effect on turnout

Very few citizens have no photo ID at all; many more just have a licence with an old address. A national study found strict ID laws had no clear effect on registration or turnout, and no extra effect on non-white voters compared with white voters. How a rule is written still matters: Texas's ID-number rule for mail ballots rejected one in eight in its first primary. That is why the ID must be free, and the rule easy to meet.

### So states should ask for free photo ID, and check citizenship against records

This is not one side's rule: large majorities in both parties support photo ID, and 83% of adults favour proof of citizenship when people first register. States should ask for photo ID at the polls, free to anyone without one, as in Georgia. They should check citizenship against government records before adding anyone to the list, and ask for documents only when the records fail. The government says it has checked more than 65 million voters in 26 states against federal records. Kansas, which asked for papers first, blocked 31,089 applicants without papers, against 39 confirmed non-citizens on its rolls: that is the model to avoid.
