## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-house-votes}

### A House majority voted to end the war three times {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--house-passed-three-resolutions}

The House rejected the first three resolutions by margins of seven votes or fewer, including a tie, then adopted the next three, from June to September (chart). [1](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-1) [3](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-3) [6](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-6)

Every vote was on a concurrent resolution under section 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution. The House has held no roll call of any kind since mid-September, as of 29 October (see the chart note).

### The resolutions do not bind the President in practice {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--concurrent-resolutions-not-binding}

A concurrent resolution is not sent to the President to sign or veto. CRS calls its effect "uncertain" since the Supreme Court's 1983 *Chadha* ruling, and Lawfare says it was brought into "serious constitutional doubt". [11](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-11) [10](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-10)

A joint resolution would be binding but faces a veto, which two-thirds of each chamber would have to override. [10](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-10)

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-house-crossovers}

### Republican defections grew but stayed small {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--republican-defections-small-but-growing}

Republicans voting to end the war rose from two in March to seven in September, out of more than 200 Republicans voting. Democrats voting against fell to none from June on (chart). [1](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-1) [6](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-6)

In the Senate, Republican yes votes rose from one to four (chart below). With the House this evenly divided, these few members and absences decided each result.

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-senate-votes}

### The Senate voted 14 times and succeeded twice {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--senate-adopted-one-resolution}

Twelve of the Senate's votes failed. A motion to discharge succeeded in May, but the resolution then failed a motion to proceed. In June the Senate adopted H.Con.Res.86, which the House had passed, so both chambers adopted one resolution (chart). [7](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-7) [8](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-8)

The Senate rejected the next House resolution, H.Con.Res.89, by one vote in September. GovTrack's list shows no Iran war-powers vote after that, as of late October. [9](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-9)

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-senate-crossovers}

### Senate defections were steady at a handful {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--senate-defections}

One Republican voted yes on each of the first five votes, and up to four from May. One Democrat voted no on every vote with a member record (chart). [7](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-7) [8](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-8)

The same pattern, one Republican yes and one Democratic no, appeared in the failed June 2025 Senate vote on an Iran war-powers resolution. [26](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-26)

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-pew-handling}

### Most Americans disapprove, and approval has slipped {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--majority-disapproves}

In Pew's surveys about a third of adults approve of the President's handling of the war, down slightly since March; Pew's separate question on whether force was the right decision moved the same way (chart). [28](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-28) [29](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-29)

Gallup in June and Quinnipiac in March, asking about the military action itself, also found majorities against (charts below). [30](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-30) [31](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-31)

### Most Republicans still approve, in falling numbers {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--republicans-support-but-falling}

Pew finds the whole decline in approval came from Republicans; Democrats' views did not change. [28](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-28) Republican support is still a clear majority in every poll here, highest in Quinnipiac's March poll and Gallup's June poll, which exclude leaners (charts below). [30](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-30) [31](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-31)

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-gallup-june-by-party}

### Independents opposed the war by about two to one or more {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--independents-opposed}

Gallup's June poll found independents opposed by more than two to one, and Quinnipiac's March poll by nearly two to one (charts). [30](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-30) [31](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-31) Pew does not report independents separately.

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-quinnipiac-march-by-party}

### Support split almost entirely along party lines {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--party-split}

In Quinnipiac's poll a week after the war began, Republicans supported the action and Democrats opposed it by similar margins; independents sat closer to Democrats (chart). [31](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-31)

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-quinnipiac-congress-approval}

### Most voters said Congress should have been asked first {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--most-wanted-congress-asked}

In March, 59% of registered voters said the President should have received approval from Congress before acting, including most independents and about a fifth of Republicans (chart). [31](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-31) This is one poll; no later reading was found.

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-war-powers-reports-1975-2017}

### Presidents have almost never started the 60-day clock themselves {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--only-one-report-started-the-clock}

Of the War Powers reports filed from 1975 to March 2017, CRS finds only one cited the section that starts the 60-day withdrawal requirement (chart). [11](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-11)

Each 2026 letter that could be read cites the President's Article II authority and is filed "consistent with" the Act. [12](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-12) [18](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-18)

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-statements}

### The 2024 campaign promised to stop wars {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--campaign-said-stop-wars}

On election night 2024 the President said "I'm not going to start a war. I'm going to stop wars." The remarks did not mention Iran. [33](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-33)

### The same campaign called Iran "very close" to a weapon {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--campaign-called-iran-very-close}

In his 2024 convention speech he said Iran was "very close to having a nuclear weapon" and blamed his predecessor. [32](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-32) Both lines were said by the same candidate in the same year.

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-war-powers-letters}

### Hostilities were declared over, then new strikes were reported {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--terminated-letter-then-new-strikes}

The 1 May letter said hostilities "have terminated" because there had been no exchange of fire since the ceasefire. [10](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-10) The blockade that began in April was still running, and from June the administration filed at least five letters reporting new military action (timeline). [14](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-14) [18](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-18)

Goldsmith calls the "no exchange of fire" claim "highly misleading". [13](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-13) The 1 May letter is known only through these quotations.

### Each new letter treats the strikes as a separate action {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--separate-actions}

Each later letter reports military action "commenced" on a new date, in response to a named Iranian or militia attack. [14](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-14) [16](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-16) Lawfare notes the tension between this and treating the blockade as anything but a continuation of the March operation. [10](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-10)

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-earlier-votes}

### Congress has declared war eleven times, in five wars {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--declarations-and-instances}

The Constitution gives Congress the power "To declare War" and makes the President "Commander in Chief". [51](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-51)

CRS counts eleven declarations of war covering five wars, and "hundreds of instances" of US forces used abroad in conflict or potential conflict. [19](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-19) The administration did not seek authorisation for this war before the 60-day mark. [10](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-10)

CRS does not code the instances by authorisation, length or scale (see Data still needed).

### Every binding attempt since 2011 to end an operation failed {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--binding-attempts-failed}

Joint resolutions on Yemen in 2019 and Iran in 2020 passed both chambers and were vetoed; neither override reached two-thirds. In 2011 the House rejected both authorising the Libya operation and limiting its funding; the 2025 Iran resolution failed in the Senate (table). [20](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-20) [21](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-21) [23](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-23) [25](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-25) [26](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-26)

### Research finds Congress shapes wars without binding votes {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--congress-as-constraint}

Kriner's study, as described on its publisher's page, finds that Congress "materially shapes the initiation, scope, and duration of major military actions", and that the party balance in Congress helps explain how long conflicts last. [27](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-27) The publisher's page gives no effect sizes.

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-ceasefire-incidents}

### Both sides used force during the ceasefire {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--both-sides-fired}

The President's own letters record Iranian attacks and US strikes during the ceasefire, before and after the memorandum (timeline). [14](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-14) [15](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-15) In each letter the US strikes answer a named Iranian attack.

Every entry comes from the US government or ISW/CTP. No Iranian or neutral record was found to check the sequence.

### The memorandum broke down within three weeks {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--mou-broke-down}

Within nine days of the signing, the President reported Iranian attacks on shipping and US strikes in reply. [15](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-15) Strikes resumed from early July, and ISW/CTP judged that they and the renewed blockade "mark the end of the ceasefire". [16](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-16) [34](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-34)

## Observations {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--observations-negotiations}

### Talks were under way when the strikes began {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--talks-under-way-when-strikes-came}

The IAEA records indirect US–Iran talks in Geneva on two dates in February 2026, the second two days before the strikes. [38](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-38) In 2025 a sixth round was suspended after Israel's attack, according to Wikipedia's account. [41](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-41)

### Iran signed a no-weapons pledge in June {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--mou-no-weapons-pledge}

The June memorandum states that "Iran reaffirms that it shall not procure or develop nuclear weapons". [39](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-39) Its other terms, including any on inspections, have not been published in any source found, so it cannot be compared clause by clause with the 2015 deal.

### Talks continued alongside strike planning in October {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--talks-continue-in-october}

The President's pledge spoke of "productive discussions", and in late October Qatar, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey were pressing a deal. [42](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-42) [47](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-47) Regional sources told ISW/CTP the Tehran meeting "made no progress on any issue". [48](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-48)

The British, French and German leaders said they "did not participate", while condemning Iran's attacks on its neighbours "in the strongest terms". [36](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-36) This list is the statements found, not a complete record of allied positions.

ISW/CTP assesses that Iran "continuously made efforts to reconstitute its ballistic missile and drone programs" from the April ceasefire. ## Subtleties {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--subtleties}

### Several Senate votes were on procedure, not the resolution {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--procedural-votes}

Eleven of the 14 Senate votes were motions to discharge a committee, and one was a motion to proceed. A yes vote on each favoured ending the war, but only the two votes on House resolutions were votes on a resolution itself. [7](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-7)

### Polls asked different questions {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--poll-wording}

Pew asks about the President's handling and about whether force was the right decision; Gallup asks about approval of "this U.S. military action"; Quinnipiac about support for it. Gallup and Quinnipiac exclude leaners from their party groups; Pew includes them. The pollsters are shown separately for this reason. [28](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-28) [30](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-30) [31](#source-decisions-law-and-diplomacy-31)

### Most claims about the latest events come from one side {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--single-party-claims}

The incident record comes from the President's letters; the reconstitution and October assessments from ISW/CTP, which is affiliated with the American Enterprise Institute; blockade figures from US Central Command. Each is labelled as such above.

## About this data {#decisions-law-and-diplomacy--about}

**What this measures.** Recorded votes in Congress, the President's War Powers letters, public statements, three pollsters' surveys, and the dated record of the ceasefire and talks. It does not measure whether the war is lawful; that is a legal and constitutional question the records inform but do not settle.

**As of.** House and Senate votes as of 29 October 2026 (no votes after 16 and 30 September respectively). Polls run from March to early October 2026. Events run to 28 October; ISW/CTP's 29 October update was not yet published when checked.

**How it compares.** Earlier operations come from CRS's list (1798–2023) and its count of War Powers reports (1975–March 2017); neither has a newer edition on EveryCRSReport. The earlier votes table covers the main roll calls found on Libya (2011), Yemen (2019) and Iran (2020, 2025).

**How confident to be.** The vote counts and letters are primary records. The 1 May letter, the DNI memo, the NYT report and resolution 2817 are known only second-hand. Gallup gives a margin of error of ±4 points; small moves within one pollster's series may be noise.

**Commonly mistaken for.** "Congress voted to end the war" is true of a concurrent resolution that the President need not sign; it is not a law. Pew's 33% is approval of the President's handling, not of the war as such.
