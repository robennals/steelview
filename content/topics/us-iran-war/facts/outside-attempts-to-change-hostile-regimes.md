title: Outside Attempts to Topple, Coerce or Disarm Hostile Regimes, 1816–2023
claimSources: [1, 3, 8, 11, 13, 16]
claim: >-
  Studies of outside attempts to topple, coerce or disarm hostile governments
  find that results depend on how success is defined. Imposed regime change has
  more often been followed by civil war than by democracy, strikes on nuclear
  sites have usually delayed programmes modestly, and sanctions or bombing
  succeed in roughly a third of cases on the commonest definitions (studies
  published 1998–2025, read October 2026).

## Observations {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--observations-nuclear-programmes}

### Ten states built nuclear weapons in about 70 years; most that tried stopped {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--acquisition-base-rate}

Of the 31 states Bleek codes as exploring a weapon, ten acquired one, and fourteen stopped before launching a programme. [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10) Only South Africa built weapons and then gave them up. [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10)

### Iraq's programme ended after a war and years of intensive inspection {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--iraq-ended-after-war-and-inspection}

Bleek dates the end of Iraq's pursuit to 1991, when after the Gulf War it was forced to give up its weapons capabilities "and submit to an intensive verification regime". [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10) Air strikes alone did not end it: Iraq's pursuit is coded from 1981, after Israel bombed the Osirak reactor. [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10)

### Syria stopped pursuing a weapon after Israel's 2007 strike, but is still coded as exploring {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--syria-returned-to-exploring}

After Israel destroyed the Al-Kibar reactor in 2007, Syria "returned to exploring". Bleek finds no public evidence of a decision either to continue or to stop. [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10)

### Libya gave up its programme in 2003 and accepted inspections {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--libya-2003-with-inspections}

Libya "returned to no activity in 2003", announcing it was abandoning its weapons of mass destruction programmes "and allowing international inspections". [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10) Hufbauer and colleagues count Libya as a sanctions success, but "only after 20+ years". [11](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-11)

### Osirak: did the strike delay Iraq or start its bomb programme? {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--osirak-coding-dispute}

Braut-Hegghammer finds the 1981 strike "triggered a covert nuclear weapons program that did not previously exist", and Bleek follows her in dating Iraq's pursuit from 1981. [9](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-9) [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10)

Other datasets disagree: Jo and Gartzke date Iraq's pursuit from 1973, and Bleek grants that the earlier work could "reasonably" be coded as pursuit. [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10) Which coding is right decides whether Osirak counts as a strike that started a programme or one that hit a programme already under way.

### Bleek codes Iran as pursuing a weapon since 1989, with two alternative codings {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--iran-coding}

Bleek codes Iran's pursuit as open-ended from 1989, but adds that one could "reasonably" code Iran as dropping back to exploration after 2003 or in 2015, when it accepted the nuclear deal's limits. [10](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-10)

## Observations {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--observations-sanctions-ties}

### How often sanctions work depends on what counts as success {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--sanctions-success-depends-on-definition}

In the TIES data, the success rate roughly doubles between the strictest count over all cases and the relaxed count over cases with a known outcome. [13](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-13) Counting negotiated settlements as success, as the relaxed definition does, means the sender also gave something up. [13](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-13)

Hufbauer and colleagues count a success when sanctions "contributed significantly" to the goal, and find one in "about one-third" of 204 cases. [11](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-11) Pape argued that sanctions must have caused the result, not merely contributed; his stricter count was not read here. [12](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-12)

### Sanctions do worse against enemies and autocrats {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--sanctions-weak-against-adversaries}

Hufbauer and colleagues find sanctions more likely to work when targets are "friendly or democratic countries, rather than enemies, autocrats". [11](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-11) For Iran and North Korea they judged "destabilization of either existing regime unlikely", and found "carrots plus sticks" more effective than sticks alone. [11](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-11)

### A newer dataset gives a wider range, without a stated definition {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--gsdb-range}

The Global Sanctions Data Base, the largest set here, scores each imposed sanction by its objective, one of which is "regime destabilization". [14](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-14) Its authors' 2021 announcement put the success rate at "30 to 50 percent", without saying which outcomes count. [15](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-15)

## Observations {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--observations-air-power}

### About a third of air-power coercion attempts succeeded {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--air-coercion-about-a-third}

Horowitz and Reiter count 19 successes in 53 attempts from 1917 to 1999. [17](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-17) Success was likelier when the target's military was vulnerable; how exposed its civilians were made no difference. [16](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-16)

### Demanding a change of leader made bombing less likely to work {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--leadership-demands-lower-success}

"Success is less likely when the attacker demands the target change its leadership." The target's regime type made no difference. [16](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-16)

## Observations {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--observations-regime-change}

### Imposed regime change more than doubles the risk of civil war {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--imposed-regime-change-and-civil-war}

In Downes's data a third of imposed regime changes were followed by civil war within a decade. Imposing a new government "more than doubles" the likelihood, and replacing only the leader makes civil war "four and a half times more likely". [1](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-1)

Nearly half of installed leaders were later forcibly removed, and about two-thirds of those installed by leadership change. [1](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-1) One reviewer notes the base rate of civil war is low, so even a doubled risk might look acceptable to an intervener. [1](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-1)

### Restoring an ousted ruler has done best {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--restoration-does-best}

Downes finds that regime changes restoring a previously ousted ruler "tend to have best outcomes". [1](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-1)

### Regime change has not made targets friendlier {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--regime-change-and-relations}

Overall, regime change had no significant effect on later disputes with the intervener, and leadership change more than doubled them. [1](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-1) Targets were "no more likely" to vote with the intervener at the UN. [1](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-1) Downes and O'Rourke find it "damages relations … more often than it improves them". [2](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-2)

### Democracy has followed only where conditions already favoured it {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--democracy-only-in-favourable-conditions}

Downes and Monten find that interveners "will meet with little success" unless the target has "high levels of economic development and societal homogeneity, and previous experience with representative governance". [3](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-3) Their data include Iran in 1953 but exclude the Reagan-era covert operations. [4](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-4)

### Most covert US attempts to change governments failed {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--covert-attempts-failed}

O'Rourke finds Cold War covert operations "unsuccessful more than 60 percent of the time", with targets more likely to have a civil war within ten years. [5](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-5) Most backed authoritarian forces, and the successes came mostly against "democratic governments, weak states, and American allies". [6](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-6) [7](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-7)

## Observations {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--observations-strikes}

### Strikes on nuclear sites have delayed programmes modestly {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--strikes-delay-modestly}

Kreps and Fuhrmann find that peacetime strikes delayed programmes, "although the size of this effect is rather modest", and wartime strikes did less well. [8](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-8) Their successes came when the attacker struck "well before a nuclear threat was imminent". [8](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-8)

### The same authors say past-success conditions are absent for Iran {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--past-success-conditions-absent-for-iran}

Writing in 2011, Kreps and Fuhrmann concluded that "many of the conditions that were conducive to past success are not present in the contemporary Iran case". [8](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-8)

## Observations {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--observations-nonproliferation-pressure}

### Sanctions stopped three of 22 programmes already under way, and deterred others from starting {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--nonproliferation-sanctions-three-of-22}

In Miller's data, once a programme had started, US sanctions succeeded "in only three of 22 cases". [18](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-18) His reviewer Fuhrmann adds that "we cannot infer much" from those cases alone: counting states deterred from starting a programme gives "a much more sanguine picture". [18](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-18)

Threats worked when "credible" and when the state was "highly dependent on the United States". [18](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-18)

### Threats plus face-saving rewards make reversal most likely {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--threats-plus-rewards}

Mehta finds reversal "most likely" when states face sanctions and are offered "face-saving rewards", especially with force "on the table". The summary read gives no figures. [19](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-19)

### Strong ceasefire agreements last longer {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--strong-ceasefire-agreements-hold}

Fortna finds that "strong agreements markedly lessen the risk of further war", naming demilitarised zones, dispute commissions, peacekeeping and outside guarantees. [24](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-24)

## Does backing down cost credibility? Two answers {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--credibility}

| Study | Method | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Press, *Calculating Credibility* (2006 per Dartmouth) | Three 20th-century cases | Leaders who backed down "were still taken seriously in the future" [20](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-20) |
| Weisiger & Yarhi-Milo (2015) | Dispute data | "past action is closely connected to subsequent dispute initiation" [21](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-21) |

## Observations {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--observations-credibility}

### The research on credibility is split {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--credibility-split}

Press expected backing down to hurt credibility and found that leaders who did were "still taken seriously in the future". [20](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-20) Weisiger and Yarhi-Milo find that past action is "closely connected" to later challenges, and that judging credibility by current power and interests alone "is misleading". [21](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-21)

### Saudi Arabia's crown prince said it would follow an Iranian bomb {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--saudi-2018-pledge}

In 2018 Mohammed bin Salman said Saudi Arabia did not want a bomb, "but without a doubt, if Iran developed a nuclear bomb, we will follow suit as soon as possible". [22](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-22)

## Subtleties {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--subtleties}

### None of these studies measures containment {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--no-study-of-containment}

Every study here measures an attempt to topple, coerce or disarm. No systematic study of holding a hostile state in check over years, with a success rate of its own, was found (see Data still needed).

### The datasets were not built on a case like Iran {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--fit-to-iran}

Downes and Monten expect most regime-change targets to be "poor, diverse countries". [3](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-3) Miller's threats worked against states "highly dependent on the United States", which Iran is not. [18](#source-outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes-18) How far the general rates carry over to Iran is a judgement, not something these data settle.

## About this data {#outside-attempts-to-change-hostile-regimes--about}

**What is measured.** Each study counts a different kind of attempt (imposed regime change, covert operations, strikes on nuclear sites, sanctions, air coercion) over a different period, with its own definition of success. The table above keeps each figure next to its definition.

**Why there is no time series.** The studies publish pooled results over long periods, not rates by year.

**How the figures were read.** Several books and articles are paywalled. Their figures are taken from abstracts, publishers' summaries and named reviewers, each labelled in the Sources. Downes's figures are reviewers' summaries with page numbers in his book.

**What this is commonly mistaken for.** A success rate under one definition is not comparable with another's. A third of sanctions cases "succeeding" under a contributed-to test is not a third succeeding because of sanctions.
