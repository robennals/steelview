Iran's nuclear programme was a real danger, but inspectors and a deal had kept it in check before. War should be the last resort. This one was chosen while the slower way could still work, and it has cost thousands of lives.

### The 2015 deal held Iran's uranium in check until the US walked away

Diplomacy had worked. In every inspection report from 2017 until mid-2019, Iran's uranium stock stayed under the 2015 deal's limit. The US left the deal in 2018, and about a year later Iran broke the limit. Only then did its stock climb far above it. Iran's most dangerous uranium, enriched to 60%, came after the deal was gone: it began making it in 2021. The danger the war was meant to end grew after the deal ended, not under it.

### Iran was close to the material for a bomb, but was not building one

Being able to make a bomb is not the same as making one. In 2025 UN inspectors found no credible sign of an active weapons programme, and US intelligence judged that Iran was not building a bomb and that its leader had not ordered one. UN inspectors place Iran's organised weapons work before the end of 2003. While Iran was the only country without nuclear weapons making 60% uranium, that called for close watching and hard bargaining. It left time for both.

### Talks were under way when the bombs fell

The slower path was still open. US and Iranian negotiators held talks in Geneva two days before the strikes. Even in the middle of the war, Iran signed a promise never to make nuclear weapons. While that agreement broke down within three weeks, it shows what talks can still get. Countries give up nuclear programmes most often when threats come with face-saving rewards, and a deal is where those rewards are offered.

### The war has killed thousands of Iranians who had no say in it

The cost of choosing war has fallen on ordinary Iranians. By one group's minimum count, at least 1,701 civilians were killed by the April ceasefire, at least 254 of them children. Every count puts Iran's deaths in the thousands, and the independent counts stop in April, so they miss the fighting since. About 3.2 million people have been driven from their homes inside Iran. Few of them had any say over their government: very few of the people who register are allowed to run for president. War is meant to be the last resort because this is what it costs.

### Strikes since June 2025 have blinded the inspectors, and bombs cannot do their job

Bombing has made the danger harder to see, not smaller. Inspectors had checked almost all of Iran's 60% uranium before the first strikes in June 2025. No inspector has seen it since, and the UN agency cannot say where it is. While Iran then suspended its cooperation by law, by February 2026 inspectors were back at seven of the 13 sites that had not been struck. Since this war began, they have visited one, a power plant. Experts see no route for Iran to make bomb-grade uranium for now, but will not say for how long. Past strikes on nuclear sites delayed programmes only modestly.

### So the US should stop bombing and go back to inspections

The US should end the strikes for good, and offer what worked before: limits on enrichment that inspectors can check, in return for easing sanctions. Iran's economy grew in the deal's first year and shrank after the US left, so the offer plausibly means something to Iran.

A deal never needed Iran's government to become friendly. It needed inspectors who could watch, and a deal is how to get them back in.
