name: Obligation to people in danger

Duties to people in serious danger are not extinguished by the fact that they are
strangers, and they grow stronger as the cost of helping falls and the ability to
help rises. Almost nobody rejects this outright. The disagreements are about how
far it extends beyond the people immediately in front of you, whether it can be
discharged by helping elsewhere rather than here, and what it takes to be
outweighed.

Two viewpoints on this page hold it, and they are not the two anyone would guess.
One is the argument that Britain owes a duty to people fleeing danger, which holds
that the obligation is prior to preference and that cost is a reason to organise
better, not a reason to refuse. The other is the argument for national control,
whose complaint is that a system which cannot distinguish the people it is owed to
from the people it is not ends up honouring the obligation to nobody. The purely
economic case for immigration does not hold this principle at all — deliberately,
because a claim that Britain profits from immigration is a different claim from a
claim that Britain owes it, and its holders think tangling the two weakens both.
