---
name: Continuity of a shared way of life
heldBy: [too-much-too-fast-damages-the-social-fabric]
---
People have a legitimate interest in the continuity of the shared life of the
place they live in — its habits, its calendar, its ordinary unspoken expectations
about how strangers behave towards one another — and an interest in that life
changing at a pace they can absorb and in ways they had some say in. The interest
is not a property right and it does not license excluding anyone; it is the same
interest that makes people mind when a familiar high street is rebuilt over a
weekend rather than a decade, generalised to the thing a high street is part of.

Stated perennially, this principle has nothing to do with immigration. It is what
is appealed to when a village objects to a bypass, when a profession resists
having its norms rewritten by regulation, when a language community wants its
language taught. It carries no claim that the inheritance in question is superior
to any other — the claim is that it is *theirs*, that every people has one, and
that having one is part of what makes a place somewhere rather than anywhere.

This is the one principle on this page held by a single viewpoint, and that is
worth saying rather than hiding. The other four either reject it — holding that a
national way of life has no fixed content to preserve, and that treating it as
though it had is how the argument goes wrong — or they subordinate it so far
beneath consent and contribution that it does no work in what they argue. The
disagreement about whether this interest exists at all, and not just about how
much weight it should get, is a large part of why the sides talk past each other.
