## Observations {#immigration-against-the-long-run--observations-people}

### Late 1990s: non-EU migration rose as labour demand strengthened {#immigration-against-the-long-run--late-1990s-rise}

The sustained rise is clearer after 1997 than at 1990. ONS identifies an increase in non-EU net migration after 1997. The Migration Observatory links the wider rise to a growing economy and low unemployment from the late 1990s, followed by a more open non-EU work-permit system and efforts to attract international students in the early 2000s. These changes developed over several years; the graph cannot attribute the turn to a single election or policy. The dashed line near 1991 instead marks a measurement change. [10](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-10) [11](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-11)

### 2004: EU enlargement added a further increase {#immigration-against-the-long-run--eu-enlargement}

The earlier rise began before eastern European countries joined the EU. In 2004 the UK, unlike most existing members, opened its labour market immediately to workers from the new accession countries. This helped produce the subsequent increase in migration from eastern Europe. It explains a later step, not the beginning of the late-1990s rise. [10](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-10) [11](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-11)

### 2020: the pandemic interrupted migration {#immigration-against-the-long-run--pandemic-dip}

The dip in net migration in 2020 reflects the disruption caused by COVID-19. It is an exceptional baseline for judging the subsequent rise. [6](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-6)

### 2021–2023: more students, care workers and humanitarian arrivals {#immigration-against-the-long-run--why-immigration-spiked}

The surge was driven by non-EU arrivals. The government restored a post-study work route, opened routes for people from Ukraine and Hong Kong, and made care workers eligible for work visas. Universities recruited overseas to ease financial pressures, while care employers recruited to fill vacancies. These changes helped push arrivals far beyond earlier levels. [6](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-6)

### Late 2023–2024: recruitment slowed and visa rules tightened {#immigration-against-the-long-run--why-immigration-fell}

Work visas began falling in late 2023, especially in care. The Home Office says closer scrutiny of sponsoring employers and enforcement against non-compliance likely contributed to the initial decline. The decline began before the 2024 rule changes, so those changes cannot explain its start. [7](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-7)

Restrictions on bringing family members for care workers and most international students then helped reduce arrivals. Further restrictions introduced in the second half of 2025 are not yet fully reflected in this chart. [6](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-6)

### 2025: fewer work arrivals drove the latest fall {#immigration-against-the-long-run--latest-fall}

Non-EU+ work-related arrivals fell by 47% in 2025, the main reason net migration continued falling. Overall emigration actually fell slightly that year: the latest annual fall in net migration came from fewer arrivals, rather than an accelerating exodus. Departures had risen between 2022 and 2024, so the explanation differs across the decline. [1](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-1)

### Gross immigration is far above any historical norm, even after two years of falling {#immigration-against-the-long-run--gross-immigration-is-far-above-any-historical-norm-even-after-two-years-of-falling}

813,000 people moved to the UK for a year or more in 2025, down from the peak of 1,469,000 in the year to March 2023. It is still higher than every year in the series before 2018. Averaged over the chart's own data, the 1990s ran at about 325,000 a year. [1](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-1) [22](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-22)

### Net migration has fallen sharply from its peak {#immigration-against-the-long-run--net-migration-is-not-unusual-at-all}

Net migration fell from its peak of 944,000 in the year to March 2023 to 171,000 in 2025 — "lower than the levels seen during the 2010s", in the Migration Observatory's words, while gross arrivals remained high. The peak is a rolling year ending in March; the annual chart shows calendar years, so its highest point is lower. ONS revises provisional estimates as more complete data arrive. [1](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-1) [6](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-6)

A larger country would naturally receive more people at an unchanged arrival rate; this graph shows that arrivals also grew relative to the population. ## Observations {#immigration-against-the-long-run--observations-international-immigration}

### Separate the non-EU+ comparison from the overall total {#immigration-against-the-long-run--international-differences}

The comparison starts with non-EU+ foreign nationals. Switching to EU+ or all arrivals changes the ranking: these are different questions, so a high total does not by itself establish a high non-EU+ inflow. [12](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-12) This is [gross arrivals](#glossary-gross-arrivals): countries with substantial outward migration can have high arrivals without equally high [net migration](#glossary-net-migration). [8](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-8) [22](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-22) [23](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-23)

### High small-country totals conceal different nationality mixes {#immigration-against-the-long-run--international-reasons}

A modest number of arrivals is a larger share of a small country's population. But the differences are not just arithmetic: Malta has faced acute labour shortages, encouraging recruitment from abroad. The nationality breakdown also differs sharply: EU+ nationals form a much larger share of arrivals in Luxembourg than in Malta. Nationality and previous residence are different measures, so this chart uses citizenship consistently. [12](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-12) These are contributing explanations, not an estimate of how much each policy caused the gap with the UK. [9](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-9) [8](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-8)

### Some low rates reflect who gets counted {#immigration-against-the-long-run--international-coverage}

Poland and Slovakia exclude Ukrainians under temporary protection from these migration statistics. Their low bars therefore cannot be read as evidence that they received very few people fleeing Ukraine. Differing coverage is one reason the chart is an approximate comparison rather than a precise league table. [8](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-8)

## Observations {#immigration-against-the-long-run--observations-nationality-arrivals}

### 2021–2023: Visa Changes, Recruitment and Humanitarian Routes Drove the Surge {#immigration-against-the-long-run--non-eu-arrivals-surge}

Several changes increased non-EU arrivals together: the post-Brexit system removed the cap on skilled workers, the government restored a post-study work route, care workers became eligible for work visas in early 2022, and new routes admitted people from Hong Kong and Ukraine. The post-study route likely made the UK more attractive to international students. [6](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-6)

Demand reinforced those policy changes: universities recruited more overseas students as their finances deteriorated, while social care employers recruited abroad to fill vacancies linked to low pay and poor working conditions. The sharp rise therefore reflects several routes expanding together, rather than a single change affecting all non-EU migrants. [6](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-6)

### Immigration Has Shifted from EU to Non-EU Nationals {#immigration-against-the-long-run--nationality-shift}

627,000 of the 813,000 people who moved to the UK long-term in 2025 were non-EU+ nationals, down from 780,000 the year before but still the great majority. [14](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-14)

EU+ arrivals remain positive, but more EU+ nationals are leaving than arriving: net migration was -42,000 in 2025 and has been negative in every rolling-year estimate since June 2022. Arrivals and net migration measure different things. [13](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-13)

### The EU Turn Began Before Free Movement Ended {#immigration-against-the-long-run--eu-turn}

EU net migration began falling after the 2016 referendum, before free movement ended in January 2021. Recent departures include people who arrived during the earlier period of higher EU immigration. [18](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-18) [15](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-15) [19](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-19)

## Observations {#immigration-against-the-long-run--observations-ukraine-arrivals}

### Ukraine Contributed a Sharp Spike in 2022, but Not the Whole Surge {#immigration-against-the-long-run--ukraine-spike}

ONS estimates 135,000 Ukrainian arrivals in 2022, falling to 39,000 in 2023 and 17,000 in 2025. The 2022 spike coincided with the opening of Ukraine humanitarian routes after Russia’s full-scale invasion. [25](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-25) [6](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-6)

The remaining non-EU+ series rose strongly too, and continued rising in 2023 while Ukrainian arrivals fell. Ukraine therefore explains part of the spike, while the wider surge also involved work and study routes. [25](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-25) [6](#source-immigration-against-the-long-run-6)
