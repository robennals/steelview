Building in England has run below the number of new households the ONS projects. With zero net migration, the ONS projects 1,287,752 fewer households in England by 2032 than in its main projection — 5.0% of the projected total, and just over half of the 2.4 million projected increase. [1](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-1) [3](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-3) Prices, however, have risen far more than migration explains: a review commissioned by the Migration Advisory Committee puts immigration at roughly 4–6% of the rise in real UK house prices over three decades. [8](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-8)

On public services, the national figures cut against a simple population-pressure story. Pupil numbers in England are falling, GP numbers are rising, real school funding per pupil is above its 2010-11 level and real health spending per person roughly doubled between 2000 and 2022. [29](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-29) [32](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-32) [46](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-46) [43](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-43) Investment tells a different story: social rent building collapsed after 2010-11 and UK government investment has been below the OECD median in every year since 1995. [36](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-36) [39](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-39)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-dwellings}

### Building has run below projected household growth {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--building-below-household-growth}

England added 208,600 net dwellings in 2024-25. The ONS projects an average of 242,000 additional households a year over 2022–2032, up from 158,000 a year in its 2018-based projections for the same period. [6](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-6) [2](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-2) A household is not a dwelling — some homes stand empty or are second homes — so the two numbers are not an exact measure of shortfall, and the ONS says its projections are not a forecast of how many homes should be built. [5](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-5)

### Building peaked before migration did {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--building-peaked-before-migration}

Net additions fell to 130,610 in 2012-13 after the financial crisis, rose to 248,590 in 2019-20, and have fallen in each year since 2021-22. [7](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-7) [6](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-6) Net migration peaked at 944,000 in the year to March 2023, so the latest fall in building came while net migration was at or near its peak. [28](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-28)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-rent-growth}

### Rent growth fell from its 2024 peak as migration fell {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--rent-growth-fell-as-migration-fell}

Annual private rent growth peaked in March 2024 and stayed near that level through 2024, then fell through 2025 to 3.8% in the year to September 2026 — less than half its peak. Over roughly the same period net migration fell from 944,000 in the year to March 2023 to 171,000 in 2025. [26](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-26) [28](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-28) Rent growth turned about a year after net migration did.

### Rents are still rising {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--rents-are-still-rising}

Rent levels have not fallen. The average UK private rent was £1,406 in September 2026, 3.8% higher than a year earlier. Growth was fastest in the North West (5.7%) and slowest in London and the South East (3.5%). [26](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-26) [27](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-27)

## Subtleties {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--subtleties-rent-growth}

### The timing does not isolate migration's effect {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--rent-timing-not-causal}

The two series moving together fits migration having pushed rents up, but the data do not separate it from other influences on rents over the same years. The Migration Observatory says research provides "limited insight into the magnitude" of migration's effect on private rents. [21](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-21)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-household-growth-by-variant}

### Migration accounts for just over half of projected household growth {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--migration-share-of-household-growth}

The ONS main projection adds about 2.4 million households in England between 2022 and 2032. Its zero net migration variant has 1,287,752 fewer by 2032, so in the projection migration accounts for just over half the growth; the rest comes from the existing population ageing and forming smaller households. [1](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-1) [3](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-3) [4](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-4)

### The projections follow past trends, not a target {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--projections-not-a-target}

These are projections of past demographic trends, not forecasts of need, and they leave out local economic and housing policy. [5](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-5) Net migration has since fallen well below the levels of 2022–2024, so the migration share will depend on which variant turns out closest. [28](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-28)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-house-price-and-rent-effects}

### Immigration explains about 4–6% of the real house-price rise {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--immigration-explains-4-6-percent-of-house-price-rise}

In housing markets most like the UK's, the median study finds a one-point rise in the immigrant share raises house prices by 0.65%. Applied to the UK's foreign-born share, the review says this "can explain approximately 4-6%" of the three-decade rise in real house prices. It calls immigration "unlikely to be a dominant driver" of UK housing costs, while finding the effect positive. [8](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-8) [13](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-13) [9](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-9)

### Rent effects per person are at least as large as price effects {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--rent-effects}

Across all studies the median rent effect is 0.64%, against 0.36% for house prices; in UK-like countries it is 0.81% for rents and 0.65% for prices. [11](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-11) [12](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-12) [13](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-13) The review produces no rent equivalent of the 4–6% figure.

### Effects are larger where building is constrained, and UK local studies find falls {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--local-versus-regional-effects}

Prices respond more where planning limits construction. Studies of small areas find weaker or negative effects because existing residents move away; in the UK, Sá (2015) finds a 1.7% fall in local house prices per point of immigrant share. [10](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-10) [14](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-14) Migration also supplies the building workforce in London, where migrants are around 46% of construction employment. [15](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-15)

### Recent migrants rent privately; settled migrants own homes at UK-born rates {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--migrants-concentrated-in-private-renting}

Around a third of people in privately rented homes in England and Wales were born abroad in 2021, against around 10% of owner-occupiers. Home ownership was 17% for migrants who had arrived in the past five years and 68% for those here more than 20 years, against 67% for the UK-born. [16](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-16) [17](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-17)

### Non-UK nationals are a rising minority of new social tenants {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--social-lettings}

Lead tenants with a non-UK nationality took 6% of new social lettings in England in 2007/08 and 10% in 2022/23. [18](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-18) People on work, study and family visas are not eligible, because of the no recourse to public funds condition, and at the 2021 Census non-UK passport holders were slightly under-represented in social housing. [19](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-19) [20](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-20) The MAC's 2018 review judged that, with little social housing being built, new lettings to migrants are "inevitably at the expense of other potential tenants". [23](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-23)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-pupils}

### Pupil numbers are falling nationally {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--pupil-numbers-falling}

There were 112,200 fewer pupils in England's schools in January 2026 than a year earlier. DfE attributes the fall to fewer births since a 2012 peak and projects primary and secondary numbers to keep falling to 2030. [29](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-29) [30](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-30) In the state-funded chart, primary numbers peaked in 2019 and the total in 2024.

### GP numbers are rising {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--gp-numbers-rising}

England had 30,183 fully qualified full-time-equivalent GPs in June 2026, 1,381 more than a year earlier and 2,382 more than in December 2019. [32](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-32) [33](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-33) There is no GP chart here: the official monthly series, which starts in September 2015, could not be downloaded. [35](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-35)

### Patients per GP over time was not obtained {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--patients-per-gp-not-obtained}

The number of GPs rising does not show that GPs kept pace with patients. Patients per GP varies by area, from 2,566 in North East London to 1,798 in Bristol, North Somerset and South Gloucestershire after adjusting for need. [34](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-34) The national trend in patients per GP is listed under data still needed.

## Subtleties {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--subtleties-pupils}

### English as an additional language is not a migration measure {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--eal-is-not-a-migration-measure}

21.6% of pupils have a first language other than English, but DfE says this is "not a measure of English language proficiency or a good proxy for recent immigration". [31](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-31)

### National totals can hide local pressure {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--local-pressure}

A falling national roll does not rule out pressure in areas where migrants settle; no local figures were gathered. The MAC's 2018 review found no evidence that migration had reduced parental choice of school or UK-born children's attainment. [25](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-25)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-affordable-completions}

### Social rent building fell after 2010-11 {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--social-rent-building-fell-after-2010}

Social rent completions fell from 39,562 in 2010-11 to 5,827 in 2016-17, and were 12,267 in 2024-25 — less than a third of the 2010-11 level. [36](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-36) Total affordable completions held up because the new affordable rent tenure, at higher rents, replaced much of social rent from 2011-12.

### Council building is at a record, from a low base {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--council-building-record}

Local authorities completed 10,480 affordable homes in 2024-25, the most since the series began in 1991-92. [37](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-37) The release described 12,198 social rent homes as the most since 2012-13; the June 2026 revision puts the figure at 12,267. [38](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-38) [36](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-36)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-school-funding-per-pupil}

### Day-to-day spending per head is above its 2010 level, after a dip in schools {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--day-to-day-spending-per-head-above-2010}

Real school funding per pupil was flat at around £7,700 from 2010-11 to 2015-16, fell 3.6% to 2018-19, then rose, to £8,390 in 2026-27 in 2025-26 prices. [46](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-46) Real health spending per person was almost £3,500 in 2022, almost double its level at the turn of the century, though still below the average of 19 advanced economies. [43](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-43)

### Lower migration raises spending per person {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--lower-migration-raises-spending-per-person}

The OBR projects real day-to-day departmental spending per person to reach its highest level in two decades outside the pandemic, around £7,800 in 2028-29. It attributes the rise from about £7,700 in the June 2025 Spending Review plans to a lower population forecast, "largely due to lower projected net migration". [44](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-44)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-government-investment}

### Public investment has been low by OECD standards {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--public-investment-low}

UK government investment as a share of GDP was below the OECD median in every year from 1995 to 2024. In the decade to 2017, the OBR ranked the UK between 23rd and 27th of 30 OECD countries. [40](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-40) [41](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-41) [39](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-39) UK investment has been higher as a share of GDP since 2020 than in most of the 2010s, but the OECD median rose too.

### Health capital spending is below the OECD average {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--health-capital-below-average}

In 2021–2024 the UK invested 0.4–0.5% of GDP a year in health capital, below the OECD average, after a clear decline through the mid-2010s and a rise since. [42](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-42)

### Investment does not adjust quickly to population {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--investment-does-not-adjust}

The OBR notes that much public investment is fixed in cash terms for each Spending Review period, so it "does not respond immediately to changes in the size of the population"; whether it catches up later is uncertain. [45](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-45)

### The UK ranked 27th of 36 in 2024 {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--uk-rank-2024}

On the OECD's own national accounts, the UK ranked 27th of the 36 members with 2024 data, close to Germany and Denmark and above Spain, Portugal and Ireland. [40](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-40) [41](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-41)

## Observations {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--observations-use-of-services}

### Migrants staff health services as well as use them {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--migrants-staff-services}

The MAC's 2018 review of EEA migration found that EEA migrants contributed more to the health service and social care, in money and work, than they used, and found no evidence that migration had reduced the quality of healthcare. It covered EEA migration before 2018, not the post-2021 arrivals. [24](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-24)

### Non-UK citizens head about a fifth of homeless households {#immigration-housing-and-public-services--homelessness}

Of households in England assessed as homeless or at risk in 2024/25, 22% (71,580) were headed by a non-UK citizen, including 7% by EEA citizens and 6% by people granted refugee status. People subject to the no recourse to public funds condition are not eligible and are not counted. [22](#source-immigration-housing-and-public-services-22)
