## Observations {#how-people-actually-arrive--context}

### Across time: small boats are a recent change in the recorded routes {#how-people-actually-arrive--across-time}

The charts cover every complete year in this Home Office series, starting in 2018. Detected arrivals grew steeply to 2022, fell in 2023, and rose again in 2024 and 2025. They remain small relative to total long-term immigration, but the size and composition of the detected flow have changed substantially. This is not an unchanged historical pattern. [10](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-10) [11](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-11)

### Across places: the UK rose while EU and US detections fell {#how-people-actually-arrive--across-places}

The OECD's comparison for 2024 records three different trends:

- **United Kingdom:** irregular-entry detections rose by 19%. [8](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-8)
- **European Union external borders:** crossing detections fell by 37%. [8](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-8)
- **United States:** Border Patrol encounters fell by 48%. [8](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-8)

These compare each system with its own previous year. They are not a ranking of illegal immigration shares: border encounters can include repeated attempts, detection practices differ, and EU external-border counts do not measure migration into each member country. The OECD also notes that irregular migration in Japan, Korea, New Zealand and Australia mainly involves overstaying after entry. A comparable country-by-country legal-entry share is therefore not available from these sources. [8](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-8)


### The rise involved a shift towards small boats {#how-people-actually-arrive--shift-to-small-boats}

From 2020, small boats became the main detected route. The Home Office says pandemic restrictions likely made alternatives such as air and ferry travel less viable. Boats are also more visible and more likely to be detected, so this shift is not a clean measure of growth in all illegal entry. [3](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-3)

### The 2023 dip and later rebound have different explanations {#how-people-actually-arrive--dip-and-rebound}

The dip coincided with a sharp fall in Albanian crossings. The UK government credits its cooperation and returns agreement with Albania; that is its explanation, not an isolated estimate of the agreement's effect. [9](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-9)

The later rebound coincided with more people per boat and a changing mix of nationalities. Weather also affects crossings. These help explain the observed changes, but the release does not establish one cause for the whole rebound. [4](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-4)

### A rising ratio can reflect fewer arrivals overall {#how-people-actually-arrive--changing-denominator}

After 2023, detections rose while total long-term immigration fell. Both movements raise the ratio in the second chart. The [explanation for the fall in overall immigration](#fact-immigration-against-the-long-run/why-immigration-fell) is different from the explanation for the rise in small boats. [10](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-10) [11](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-11)


### Detections were small relative to total immigration {#how-people-actually-arrive--detected-illegal-arrivals-were-about-one-in-eighteen-of-everyone-who-moved-here}

46,497 detected arrivals via illegal routes against 813,000 people moving to the UK long-term: 46,497 divided by 813,000 is 5.7%. Small boats were 41,472 of those, or 5.1%. [1](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-1) [3](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-3) [5](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-5)

### Study was the largest reason among non-EU+ arrivals {#how-people-actually-arrive--study-is-the-largest-single-reason-anyone-moves-to-britain}

Of non-EU+ arrivals in the same year, 47% came for study, 23% for work, 14% were asylum applicants, 7% for family and 6% on humanitarian routes. [2](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-2)

## Subtleties {#how-people-actually-arrive--subtleties}

### A mismatched denominator gives a much smaller share {#how-people-actually-arrive--a-mismatched-denominator-gives-a-much-smaller-share}

Dividing a recent year's arrivals into the 1,469,000 peak of the year to March 2023 gives about 3% — one year's numerator over a different year's denominator. [1](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-1) [3](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-3)

### Detections are not a complete count of people {#how-people-actually-arrive--detections-are-a-floor-not-a-total}

Undetected entries are missing, while the same person can be detected more than once. Entry route also differs from later immigration status: someone may enter legally and overstay, or enter irregularly and later receive protection. The figures cannot establish an exact legal/illegal split. [5](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-5)

### "Asylum" and "small boats" overlap but are not nested {#how-people-actually-arrive--asylum-and-small-boats-overlap-but-are-not-nested}

Just over half of asylum claimants arrived by illegal routes and 39% arrived on a visa, so the 14% asylum share and the 5.7% irregular share cannot be added together. [6](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-6)

### A third measure again {#how-people-actually-arrive--a-third-measure-again}

ONS counts 88,000 people immigrating for asylum in 2025, however they arrived. That is not the same statistic as either of the other two. [7](#source-how-people-actually-arrive-7)
