title: Integration Outcomes in the UK
claimSources: [2, 1, 6, 11, 13, 36, 41, 20]
claim: >-
  The share of residents born abroad has roughly quadrupled since 1951, and outcomes vary widely by group, mostly improving with time and across generations.
status: well-supported
dataStillNeeded:
  - measure: 'Net fiscal contribution by country of birth and by generation, UK'
    why: >-
      No UK body publishes the annual or lifetime net fiscal contribution of
      immigrants and their children by country or region of origin, so the
      Danish, Dutch and Norwegian figures are the only ones available and come
      from countries whose immigrant intakes differ from the UK's.
  - measure: 'Employment, earnings and school results by migrant generation, UK'
    why: >-
      The Census does not record parents' country of birth, and the official
      employment and GCSE series are by ethnicity, which mixes immigrants with
      UK-born people whose families arrived generations ago. Whether gaps close
      between the first and second generation cannot be read from them.
  - measure: 'Published Index of Dissimilarity values and an isolation (exposure) index by ethnic group, 1991–2021'
    why: >-
      Catney and colleagues publish their segregation values only as a chart
      image, and the dissimilarity index measures evenness, not how many of a
      group's neighbours belong to the same group. An isolation index for the
      Pakistani and Bangladeshi groups would show whether neighbourhood
      exposure moved differently from evenness as those groups grew.
  - measure: 'A current measure of ethnic segregation between schools'
    why: >-
      The only school-level measure found covers 2011 to 2016.
  - measure: 'A repeated survey of social attitudes by religion and migrant generation, UK'
    why: >-
      The attitude evidence by religion is two one-off polls and one small 2009
      academic survey. No British Social Attitudes or European Social Survey
      series by religion and generation was found, so whether attitudes change
      between generations cannot be tracked.
  - measure: 'Local trust and cohesion measured against the local pace of immigration'
    why: >-
      The research reviewed relates trust to the level of ethnic diversity,
      not to how fast an area's population changed through immigration.
  - measure: "Denmark's net-contribution analysis for 2020 onwards"
    why: >-
      The latest edition found covers 2019; a combined 2020–2021 update was
      announced but not found published.
  - measure: 'Foreign-born share of England and Wales for 1961–1991 in a published table'
    why: >-
      The ONS publishes the 1961, 1971, 1981 and 1991 Census shares only as a
      chart image, so the series here shows only the years the ONS states in
      text.

Who arrives and how they fare afterwards are measured by different statistics, and most of the UK's are by ethnicity or religion rather than by country of birth or generation. Read together, they show wide differences between groups, with most gaps narrowing over time: language improves with years in the country, employment gaps have shrunk, the children of most minority groups outperform White British pupils at school, and residential segregation has fallen. No UK fiscal estimate by origin was found; the three countries that do find large differences by origin and by reason for migrating. [1](#source-integration-outcomes-1) [6](#source-integration-outcomes-6) [11](#source-integration-outcomes-11) [36](#source-integration-outcomes-36) [41](#source-integration-outcomes-41) [20](#source-integration-outcomes-20)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-foreign-born-share}

### The foreign-born share has roughly quadrupled since 1951 {#integration-outcomes--foreign-born-share-quadrupled}

In England and Wales, 4.3% of residents were born abroad in 1951, 8.9% in 2001, 13.4% in 2011 and 16.8% in 2021, about one person in six. The largest single increase between the last two censuses was in people born in Romania, after work restrictions on Romanian citizens were lifted in 2014. [2](#source-integration-outcomes-2) [1](#source-integration-outcomes-1) [3](#source-integration-outcomes-3)

### This is a count of residents, not of recent arrivals {#integration-outcomes--a-stock-not-a-flow}

The share includes people who arrived decades ago, many now British citizens, and it falls when people born abroad leave or die. It says nothing about how long people have been here, which is what the language and identity figures below are about. [1](#source-integration-outcomes-1)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-fastest-changing-areas}

### The fastest-changing places are mostly outer London and towns near it {#integration-outcomes--fastest-changing-places}

Between 2011 and 2021 the foreign-born share rose by about 10 percentage points in Barking and Dagenham and about 9 in Watford, Havering and Thurrock, against 3.4 points for England and Wales as a whole. Inner London boroughs that were already the most foreign-born changed little. [4](#source-integration-outcomes-4) [5](#source-integration-outcomes-5)

### EU-born residents drove the change in some towns, not in most {#integration-outcomes--eu-share-of-the-change}

EU-born residents accounted for more than half of the rise in 6 of the 20 places: Boston, where they were almost all of it, Peterborough, Thurrock, Luton, Barking and Dagenham, and Watford. In the other 14, people born outside the EU accounted for half or more. Across England and Wales the two contributed about equally. [4](#source-integration-outcomes-4) [5](#source-integration-outcomes-5)

### The detailed origins of the non-EU rise vary by place {#integration-outcomes--non-eu-origins-vary}

In Hillingdon, Swindon, Sandwell and Leicester most of the non-EU rise was in people born in the Middle East and Asia; in Dartford and Salford a large part was in people born in Africa. The Census groups do not separate Muslim-majority countries, so these figures cannot say how much of the change was from any particular culture or religion. [4](#source-integration-outcomes-4) [5](#source-integration-outcomes-5)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-fastest-changing-areas}

### Places where the non-EU-born led {#integration-outcomes--non-eu-led-places}

The 14 places where people born outside the EU accounted for half or more of the rise include Hillingdon, Dartford, Swindon, Sandwell and Sutton. [4](#source-integration-outcomes-4) [5](#source-integration-outcomes-5)

### Origins are by continent {#integration-outcomes--census-origins-by-continent}

The same Census tables split the non-EU-born by continent. [4](#source-integration-outcomes-4) [5](#source-integration-outcomes-5)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-english-proficiency}

### Recent arrivals are the most likely to struggle with English {#integration-outcomes--recent-arrivals-struggle-with-english}

11% of people who arrived between 2011 and 2021 said they could not speak English well, against 4% of those who arrived before 1971. Across the whole population, 1.5% (880,000) could not speak English well and 0.3% (161,000) could not speak it at all. [6](#source-integration-outcomes-6) [9](#source-integration-outcomes-9)

### English as a main language rises with time in the UK {#integration-outcomes--english-rises-with-time}

84% of residents who arrived before 1971 had English as their main language, against 37% of 2011–21 arrivals; most recent arrivals who do not have English as a main language say they speak it well or very well. The share who cannot speak English at all is around 1 to 2% in every arrival period. [7](#source-integration-outcomes-7)

### British identity follows the same pattern {#integration-outcomes--identity-rises-with-time}

94% of non-EU-born residents who arrived before 1951 described their national identity as UK-only, against 81% of 1981–90 arrivals and 20% of 2011–21 arrivals. [8](#source-integration-outcomes-8)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-english-proficiency}

### One snapshot, not people followed over time {#integration-outcomes--snapshot-not-cohort}

These are different groups of people measured once, in 2021. Earlier arrivals came from different countries, and those who stayed may differ from those who left, so the gap between periods is not only the effect of time spent in the UK. The Census does not record parents' birthplace, so the children of immigrants cannot be separated out. [7](#source-integration-outcomes-7)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-employment-by-ethnicity}

### Employment gaps by ethnicity are large but narrowing {#integration-outcomes--employment-gaps-narrowing}

From 2004 to 2022 the employment rate of the combined Pakistani and Bangladeshi group rose from 44% to 61%, the largest increase of any group, but it remained the lowest. The Indian rate overtook the White British rate over the same period. [11](#source-integration-outcomes-11) [12](#source-integration-outcomes-12)

### Rising employment across all groups partly reflects the wider labour market {#integration-outcomes--wider-labour-market}

The overall rate also rose over the period, so part of each group's rise is the general recovery after 2010 rather than anything specific to that group. [11](#source-integration-outcomes-11)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-women-employment}

### The remaining gap is mainly among women {#integration-outcomes--gap-is-among-women}

In 2022, 75% of Pakistani and Bangladeshi men were in work and 46% of women. The publisher notes that the Commission on Race and Ethnic Disparities cited cultural and religious reasons for this difference. [13](#source-integration-outcomes-13)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-employment-by-religion}

### Muslims have the lowest employment rate of any religious group {#integration-outcomes--muslim-employment}

At the 2021 Census, 51.4% of Muslims aged 16 to 64 were in work against 70.9% of the population. The ONS attributes the gap to high shares of students and of people looking after home or family. 28.7% of Muslim women were looking after home or family, against 3.9% of Muslim men. [14](#source-integration-outcomes-14) [15](#source-integration-outcomes-15)

### Qualifications are polarised rather than uniformly low {#integration-outcomes--muslim-qualifications}

25.3% of Muslims had no qualifications, against 18.2% of the population, but 32.3% had a degree-level qualification, close to the 33.8% overall. [16](#source-integration-outcomes-16)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-employment-by-religion}

### Not adjusted for age {#integration-outcomes--religion-not-age-adjusted}

The ONS figures are not age-standardised, and the Muslim group is the youngest in the Census, so more of its working-age members are students. That explains part of the employment gap; it does not explain the gap among women. [14](#source-integration-outcomes-14) [15](#source-integration-outcomes-15)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-denmark-net-contribution}

### Denmark: immigrants from MENAPT countries are net costs; Western immigrants are net contributors {#integration-outcomes--denmark-menapt-vs-western}

In 2019, immigrants from the Middle East, North Africa, Pakistan, Afghanistan and Turkey (the Danish finance ministry's "MENAPT" group) cost Danish public finances DKK 79 thousand each on average, while immigrants from Western countries contributed DKK 52 thousand each, and people of Danish origin DKK 22 thousand. [19](#source-integration-outcomes-19) [20](#source-integration-outcomes-20) [25](#source-integration-outcomes-25)

### The cost fell sharply as employment rose {#integration-outcomes--denmark-cost-fell-with-employment}

The average net cost of MENAPT immigrants fell by about DKK 37 thousand a person between 2014 and 2019. The Danish finance ministry attributes about half the fall for non-Western immigrants to stronger attachment to the labour market, during years when employment was rising for everyone. [24](#source-integration-outcomes-24) [26](#source-integration-outcomes-26)

### Reason for migrating explains part of the gap, not all of it {#integration-outcomes--denmark-route-matters}

Refugees cost about DKK 95 thousand each in 2019, while those who came to work contributed about DKK 120 thousand. MENAPT immigrants came mainly as refugees or family members, Western ones mainly to work or study. With the Western mix of reasons, the MENAPT average would have been about DKK 55 thousand higher, still negative. [21](#source-integration-outcomes-21) [22](#source-integration-outcomes-22)

### Descendants' figures mostly reflect their age {#integration-outcomes--denmark-descendants-are-children}

Descendants of every origin, including Western, show net costs because most are children in school. At the Danish-origin age profile, descendants' total contribution would be about DKK 13 billion higher and immigrants' about DKK 28 billion lower. [23](#source-integration-outcomes-23)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-denmark-net-contribution}

### One year, not a lifetime {#integration-outcomes--denmark-annual-not-lifetime}

These are annual accounts for each year, not lifetime ones, and are not adjusted for the business cycle, which is why the Danish-origin line swings from year to year. They allocate shared spending such as defence across everyone. Denmark's MENAPT intake was mainly refugees and their families, so the figures say more about humanitarian routes than about work routes such as the UK's. [25](#source-integration-outcomes-25) [21](#source-integration-outcomes-21)

### All immigrants and descendants together {#integration-outcomes--denmark-all-immigrants-and-descendants}

In 2019 immigrants and descendants together cost Danish public finances DKK 16 billion, about DKK 21 thousand per person. [19](#source-integration-outcomes-19) [20](#source-integration-outcomes-20) [25](#source-integration-outcomes-25)

### What matching Danish employment would add {#integration-outcomes--denmark-employment-gap-value}

Had MENAPT immigrants aged 25 to 64 worked at the Danish-origin rate, their 2019 contribution would have been about DKK 12 billion higher. [24](#source-integration-outcomes-24) [26](#source-integration-outcomes-26)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-netherlands-by-motive}

### Netherlands: labour migrants are lifetime net contributors; asylum and family migrants are large net costs {#integration-outcomes--netherlands-by-motive}

An independent study estimates that, over a lifetime and including the second generation, labour migrants contribute €125,000 each on average, while study migrants cost €75,000, family migrants €275,000 and asylum migrants €475,000. [27](#source-integration-outcomes-27)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-netherlands-by-region}

### Region of origin produces gaps as large as reason for migrating {#integration-outcomes--netherlands-by-region}

Western immigrants contribute €25,000 on average and non-Western immigrants cost nearly €275,000; Morocco, the Horn of Africa and Sudan sit at a net cost of "€550,000 to €600.000" per immigrant. The authors find that where the first generation is a large net cost, the second generation is usually also negative or at best budget-neutral. [28](#source-integration-outcomes-28) [29](#source-integration-outcomes-29)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-netherlands-by-region}

### An independent study, not the CPB {#integration-outcomes--netherlands-not-cpb}

The study is by Jan van de Beek, Hans Roodenburg, Joop Hartog and Gerrit Kreffer, published by van de Beek's own imprint in 2021 and part-funded by the Renaissance Instituut. It describes itself as an update of a 2003 report by the Netherlands Bureau for Economic Policy Analysis (CPB), which is why it is sometimes attributed to the CPB; the CPB did not produce it. Its lifetime figures are projections of future earnings and spending, not observed totals. [30](#source-integration-outcomes-30) [31](#source-integration-outcomes-31)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-norway-by-region}

### Norway's model finds a net cost only for immigrants from outside Europe and the West {#integration-outcomes--norway-model}

Statistics Norway modelled a one-off increase in immigration in 2015. Over 2015–2100 each extra immigrant from the rest of the world (R3) adds NOK 4.1 million of net public spending, while Western immigrants (R1) reduce it by NOK 0.8 million and Eastern EU immigrants (R2) add 0.8 million. [32](#source-integration-outcomes-32)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-norway-by-region}

### A broad group and generous assumptions {#integration-outcomes--norway-assumptions}

R3 is everything outside Western Europe, North America, Oceania and the Eastern EU, and includes most refugees. The model assumes immigrants' children behave like non-immigrants and gives everyone of a given age and sex the same use of public services, which SSB says probably underestimates spending on refugees. It rests on 2006 behaviour. [33](#source-integration-outcomes-33) [34](#source-integration-outcomes-34)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-gcse-by-ethnicity}

### Most minority groups outscore White British pupils at GCSE {#integration-outcomes--minority-pupils-outperform}

In 2022/23 Chinese (65.5) and Indian (59.4) pupils had the highest average Attainment 8 scores, and Bangladeshi, Black African and Pakistani pupils also scored above White British pupils (44.9). Gypsy and Roma, Irish traveller and Mixed White and Black Caribbean pupils had the lowest, with Black Caribbean pupils also below White British. [35](#source-integration-outcomes-35) [36](#source-integration-outcomes-36)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-gcse-fsm}

### The gap is widest among poorer pupils {#integration-outcomes--gcse-fsm}

Among pupils eligible for free school meals, White British pupils scored lower than every group except Gypsy, Roma and Irish traveller pupils. Every group scores lower when eligible for free school meals. [37](#source-integration-outcomes-37)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-higher-education-entry}

### Minority pupils enter university at higher rates, and White pupils at the lowest {#integration-outcomes--university-entry}

In 2024, 66.1% of Chinese state-school pupils went to university at 18, and 29.8% of White pupils, the lowest rate. Research cited by the publisher finds almost all minority groups more likely to go to university by 19 than White British pupils, even after allowing for prior attainment and family background. [38](#source-integration-outcomes-38) [39](#source-integration-outcomes-39)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-higher-education-entry}

### Every group rose {#integration-outcomes--every-group-entry-rate-rose}

Every group's entry rate rose between 2006 and 2024. [38](#source-integration-outcomes-38) [39](#source-integration-outcomes-39)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-gcse-by-ethnicity}

### Ethnicity is not generation {#integration-outcomes--ethnicity-is-not-generation}

These are ethnic groups, most of whose pupils were born in the UK. The school figures show how the children and grandchildren of earlier migrants are doing, not how recent arrivals or the children of recent arrivals are doing, and they cover state-school pupils in England only. [38](#source-integration-outcomes-38) [40](#source-integration-outcomes-40)

### The lowest scores, and a second source {#integration-outcomes--lowest-attainment-scores}

Average Attainment 8 scores in 2022/23 were 20.3 for Gypsy and Roma pupils, 26.8 for Irish traveller pupils and 39.1 for Mixed White and Black Caribbean pupils. The Education Policy Institute found most ethnic groups achieved higher GCSE grades than White British pupils in 2023. [35](#source-integration-outcomes-35) [36](#source-integration-outcomes-36)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-segregation}

### Residential segregation fell for every ethnic group, 1991–2021 {#integration-outcomes--segregation-fell}

On the standard measure, the index of dissimilarity across neighbourhoods, segregation declined steadily for every ethnic group, White and minority, across the four censuses from 1991 to 2021. The largest fall was for the Black African group; Bangladeshi, Black Caribbean and Indian segregation also fell sharply. White British segregation was 0.55 in 2001 and 2011 and 0.53 in 2021. [41](#source-integration-outcomes-41) [43](#source-integration-outcomes-43)

### The standard measure is of evenness, not of who lives next to whom {#integration-outcomes--evenness-not-exposure}

The index compares how evenly each group is spread across neighbourhoods relative to everyone else. A growing group can become more evenly spread while more of its members' neighbours belong to the same group; the study does not report that second measure, called isolation or exposure, and none was found for 2021. [42](#source-integration-outcomes-42)

### Between schools, segregation was highest in non-Christian faith schools {#integration-outcomes--school-segregation}

A 2017 analysis of 2011–16 school census data counted a school as segregated when its share of White British pupils differed from its ten nearest schools' by more than 15 percentage points. By that test 84% of primary and 93% of secondary non-Christian faith schools were segregated. No later measure was found. [44](#source-integration-outcomes-44) [45](#source-integration-outcomes-45)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-cohesion-and-trust}

### Cohesion has stayed high {#integration-outcomes--cohesion-stable}

81% of adults in England agree their local area is a place where people from different backgrounds get on well together, in a narrow range since 2018/19; the 2024/25 figure is a small but statistically significant fall from 81.4% to 80.6%. [47](#source-integration-outcomes-47)

### Neighbourhood trust is lower than in 2013/14 {#integration-outcomes--trust-lower-than-2013}

40% say many people in their neighbourhood can be trusted, within a 40% to 42% band since 2016/17 but down from 48% in 2013/14, the first year of the current survey method. [46](#source-integration-outcomes-46)

### Trust tracks deprivation closely {#integration-outcomes--trust-tracks-deprivation}

63% of adults in the least deprived tenth of areas say many neighbours can be trusted, against 18% in the most deprived tenth. Trust is also lower among most minority religious groups, including 25% of Muslims. [48](#source-integration-outcomes-48) [49](#source-integration-outcomes-49)

### Research finds a small negative association between diversity and trust, weaker or absent once deprivation is allowed for {#integration-outcomes--diversity-and-trust-research}

Most studies find more diverse communities report less trust, and a 2005 British survey linked rising diversity to lower neighbourhood trust. Other British studies find no strong erosion once deprivation is taken into account, and a London study found diversity linked to higher perceived cohesion. [50](#source-integration-outcomes-50) [51](#source-integration-outcomes-51) [52](#source-integration-outcomes-52)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-cohesion-and-trust}

### The research measures diversity, not immigration or its pace {#integration-outcomes--diversity-not-immigration}

Almost all of the trust research relates trust to an area's ethnic mix, not to immigration or to how fast an area changed, and the national survey does not link trust to local migration. A national trend cannot show whether places that changed fastest lost the most trust. [53](#source-integration-outcomes-53)

### Segregation within neighbourhoods {#integration-outcomes--london-segregation-and-cohesion}

The London study found diversity linked to higher perceived cohesion, with segregation within neighbourhoods linked to lower cohesion. [50](#source-integration-outcomes-50) [51](#source-integration-outcomes-51) [52](#source-integration-outcomes-52)

## Observations {#integration-outcomes--observations-attitudes-homosexuality}

### British Muslims are markedly more conservative than the public on homosexuality {#integration-outcomes--muslim-attitudes-homosexuality}

In a 2024 poll, 28% of British Muslims said it would be undesirable to outlaw homosexuality, against 62% of the public. In a 2015 poll in areas where at least a fifth of residents were Muslim, 52% of Muslims disagreed that homosexuality should be legal and 18% agreed; 39% agreed that wives should always obey their husbands. [59](#source-integration-outcomes-59) [55](#source-integration-outcomes-55) [56](#source-integration-outcomes-56)

### The same polls find belonging to Britain as high as the public's {#integration-outcomes--muslim-belonging}

In the 2015 poll, 86% of Muslims felt a strong sense of belonging to Britain, against 83% of the national control sample. [57](#source-integration-outcomes-57)

### The evidence on generations is thin and mixed {#integration-outcomes--attitudes-generations}

The one academic study comparing generations, from 2009, found young Muslims more liberal than their elders on gay marriage, with 74 percent supporting same-sex marriage or civil unions, but the most conservative of any group on the morality of homosexuality. No repeated survey tracks these attitudes across generations. [61](#source-integration-outcomes-61) [62](#source-integration-outcomes-62)

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties-attitudes-homosexuality}

### Each survey has a limitation that matters {#integration-outcomes--attitude-survey-methods}

- **ICM for Channel 4, 2015:** 1,081 Muslims interviewed face to face, only in areas at least 20% Muslim, which over-represents concentrated communities; the control sample of 1,008 was by telephone, a different method. [54](#source-integration-outcomes-54)
- **J.L. Partners for the Henry Jackson Society, 2024:** 1,000 Muslims and 2,013 members of the public from an online panel, weighted by country of birth among other things; the commissioner is a think tank with a position on the subject. [58](#source-integration-outcomes-58)
- **Faith Matters survey, 2009 (Kashyap and Lewis):** 480 interviews in areas at least 10% Muslim, with 79 percent non-response; old and small. [60](#source-integration-outcomes-60)

No British Social Attitudes or European Social Survey series of these attitudes by religion and generation was found.

## Subtleties {#integration-outcomes--subtleties}

### What this report does not measure {#integration-outcomes--what-is-not-measured}

Almost no UK statistic follows immigrants and their children by country of origin. Employment and school results are by ethnicity; the Census measures language and identity by year of arrival, not generation; and fiscal contribution by origin was found only for other countries. Comparisons with Denmark, the Netherlands and Norway carry over the differences in who those countries admitted, which were more often refugees and family members than the UK's recent work and study arrivals. [21](#source-integration-outcomes-21) [34](#source-integration-outcomes-34) [53](#source-integration-outcomes-53)
