Britain has taken in more people than it can house, and its people are no richer for it. A government's first duty is to its own people. Parliament should cut immigration hard and make sure it stays down.

### Britain has just had the biggest wave of immigration on record

More people moved to Britain in each year from 2021 to 2024 than in any year since the record began in 1964, and in those four years net migration added about 2.5 million people. Migration now makes up almost all of the country's population growth.

### More people make the economy bigger, but barely make anyone richer

Since 2019, output per person has barely moved, even as the economy as a whole grew. The budget watchdog expects no more: in its scenarios, migration moves output per person by under 1%, up or down, and extra migrants leave output per person lower if buildings and equipment do not keep up, which public investment does not do straight away. While Canada and Germany stalled too, so migration is not the only cause, a bigger economy is not a richer people.

### Migration is expected to add half of England's new households, and building falls short

Migration accounts for just over half of the extra households expected in England, yet England is adding fewer homes each year than the households projected. About a third of private renters were born abroad, and rent growth dropped to less than half its peak as net migration fell. While immigration explains only a small part of the long rise in house prices, in countries like Britain it raises rents more than prices, and rent is what young people without a home of their own pay.

### Low-paid routes bring people who cost more than they pay in

Skilled workers outside health and care are a large gain to the public purse. But care workers, and the adult family members of health and care workers, cost more than they pay over their lives. The average resident is a lifetime cost too, but that is no reason to add more people at that cost. While lower migration raises borrowing within five years, that window ends before the costs that come as migrants grow old, and lower migration raises public spending per person, which is what residents receive.

### The fall in numbers rests on rules a future government can undo

Net migration has fallen by about four-fifths from its peak, and the net flow for work and study fell as visa rules tightened. A future minister can loosen those rules again, and promises have failed before: since the 2010 pledge, net migration has reached the promised "tens of thousands" only in 2020, during Covid, and later Conservative and Labour pledges named no number.

### So Parliament should set a binding cap and lengthen the wait to settle

Most people want immigration reduced, and a country should have a say over who joins it. So Parliament should set a binding yearly cap on net migration, and close the low-paid routes that cost more than they bring in. It should also lengthen the wait for settlement, the right to stay for good: about 1.6 million people are expected to settle by 2030, and once settled, they can claim most of the benefits that people on temporary visas cannot.
