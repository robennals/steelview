A country is more than an economy. It is a shared way of life: a language, laws applied equally to everyone, and values people expect to hand on. Immigration on today's scale, from very different cultures, is wearing that away.

### Newcomers take years to join in, and Britain has far more of them

One resident of England and Wales in six was born abroad in 2021, roughly four times the share in 1951. Newcomers do join in, but slowly. Recent arrivals are the most likely to struggle with English. Among residents born outside the EU, those who came long ago are far more likely than recent arrivals to give only a UK national identity. The faster people arrive, the more people are still at the start.

### Some communities hold values far from the majority's

In a 2024 poll, 28% of British Muslims said it would be undesirable to outlaw homosexuality, against 62% of the public. A shared way of life rests on shared values, and on this one the gap is wide. A 2015 poll found Muslims' sense of belonging to Britain as high as the public's, but belonging is not agreement. While each poll has a flaw, and the evidence on younger generations is thin and mixed, no repeated survey shows the gap closing.

### Fear of seeming racist held back the response to gangs abusing children

Inquiries in four towns described the gangs who abused children as Asian men or men of Pakistani or southern Asian heritage. Official reports found that fear of seeming racist held officials back. In Rotherham, about 1,400 children were abused, and some staff were told not to mention the offenders' ethnicity. Police still do not record ethnicity for about two-thirds of abuse suspects, so no one can say how big the problem is. While most recorded child sexual abuse happens outside gangs, rules must mean the same for every community.

### Where it is measured, problems can grow in the second generation

In Denmark, men born to non-Western immigrants are convicted more often than the immigrants themselves, after allowing for age. So problems need not fade with each generation. While that group's conviction rate has fallen over time, Britain publishes no such figures, so it cannot tell whether the same is happening here. In Britain, Islamist extremists are the largest group of terrorist prisoners, and most terrorist prisoners are British. Extremism among citizens is still a failure of integration.

### Outcomes differ widely by origin, so whom Britain chooses matters

After adjusting for age and sex, Afghan nationals in Britain were convicted of sex offences at seven times the British rate, and people from the Middle East and north Africa at about three times. EU and East Asian nationals were below the British rate. In Denmark, immigrants from the Middle East, north Africa, Pakistan, Afghanistan and Turkey cost the public purse on average, while Western immigrants pay in. While across all offences foreign nationals are convicted at about the British rate, that average hides the groups that differ most.

### So admit far fewer, choose for integration, and measure the results

A people is entitled to continuity in its way of life and to a say over who joins it. So Britain should admit far fewer people, and favour those likely to share its language and laws. It should record the ethnicity of every suspect, and publish how each group fares by origin and generation. The government resists even that: the Ministry of Justice is appealing an order to publish convictions by nationality and offence.

While most people say their area is one where people from different backgrounds get on, segregation has fallen and most minority groups outscore White British pupils at school, national averages did not protect the children of Rotherham.
