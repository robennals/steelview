People who came to Britain lawfully, on a path the state set out, should be allowed to finish it. Parliament can change the rules for people who have not yet come.

### About 2.2 million people built their lives around a path the state set out

About 2.2 million people were on a five-year path to settlement at the end of 2024. Settlement is the right to stay in Britain for good. Most came to work, or came with someone who did, and 430,000 to 520,000 of them are children. They moved to Britain, took jobs and put children in school, on a path whose terms the state set out.

### Changing the terms part-way through has been ruled an abuse of power before

When ministers toughened one scheme for migrants already on it, the High Court ruled in 2008 that this was "conspicuous unfairness and an abuse of power". The scheme had promised the terms in force when people joined. The government's texts set out terms today too: it called the Hong Kong route a "historic and moral commitment". While the skilled-worker guidance says only that people "may be able to apply", nobody moves their family for "may" unless they trust the state to mean it.

### The government now proposes to make them wait longer, part-way through

The government proposes applying a longer wait to everyone here who has not yet settled. Most adult dependants of skilled workers earn too little for the five-year route, so they would face ten years. MPs found it "common" for the Home Office to protect people already on a route, though there is precedent for not doing so. Ten months on, the Home Secretary had still not said whether anyone already on the path would be protected.

### Many on the path are well-paid workers, worth more to Britain if they stay

Skilled workers on the main visa earn a median of £56,600 a year. Skilled workers who stay are worth far more to the public purse than those who leave. While the evidence on whether longer waits drive people away is limited, Britain gains nothing by giving its best-paid newcomers a reason to go. That about half of adult skilled-worker dependants were not working is a case for different terms for future arrivals. It is not a case for breaking the terms given to people already here.

### A longer wait would cost these families more, for years longer

Britain already charges more for settlement than most of Europe, and its fee is ten times what it costs the Home Office to process. A single skilled worker pays about £9,900 in fees before settling after five years, and would pay £16,900 over ten. Until they settle, they are barred from most benefits, however long they have worked and paid tax. A ten-year wait would make Britain an outlier among rich countries, where five years is usual.

### So change the terms only for people who have not yet come

Parliament may set any terms it likes for people who have not yet come. But everyone who arrived on a five-year path should be able to finish it on the terms they were given, because rules must mean what they say, in the sense people reasonably took them. Most people oppose taking settlement from those who hold it, and majorities want to keep it for full-time workers and NHS staff.
