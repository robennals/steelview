Every other argument on this page is about a ledger. This one is not, and the
first thing to say is that the ledger was never the point. Suppose the accounts
had come out perfectly: every arrival a net contributor, wages untouched, houses
built to match. The objection would remain, because the objection is that the
town you grew up in is not the town you live in, that this happened faster than
anyone can absorb, and that nobody asked. Answering it with a fiscal number is
not a rebuttal. It is a change of subject, and people can tell.

Start with pace, because pace is the whole argument. A country that admits a
million people over thirty years and a country that admits a million over three
end up with the same population and have done two entirely different things. The
difference is integration, and integration is not a status that gets conferred at
a ceremony — it is a slow social process that people do to each other. Children
in the same class. Neighbours who borrow things. Workmates, marriages, a shared
stock of jokes and grievances, an agreed way of queuing. Processes like that have
a rate, and the rate is set by the receiving side as much as the arriving one:
there are only so many school places, so many hours in a nurse's day, so many
people in a street with the energy to get to know anybody. Push arrivals past
that rate and you do not get conflict. You get something quieter and worse — two
sets of people occupying the same postcode and not meeting, which is a loss to
both and which nobody chose.

Britain went past that rate, on its own figures, and the figures only mean
something with a baseline attached. Long-term immigration ran at about 325,000 a
year through the 1990s and about 722,000 through the 2010s. It reached 1,469,000 in
the year to March 2023 and, after two years of falling, was still 813,000 in 2025 —
higher than every year before 2018 and about two and a half times the 1990s norm.
The stock moved with the flow: the share of England and Wales born outside the UK
went from 4.3% in 1951 to 8.9% in 2001 to 16.8% in 2021, one person in six, and
the ONS estimate for the UK at mid-2024 is 19%. And the next cohort is already
here — more than a third of babies born in England and Wales in 2024 had a mother
born abroad, against about a quarter in 2008. Net migration reached 944,000 in
a single year, roughly triple anything in the country's history before 2020, and
the composition of arrivals shifted at the same time: free movement ended, EU net
migration went negative, and the great majority of new arrivals came from further
away, in cultural distance as well as miles. That combination — the largest
inflow ever recorded, and the largest change in where it came from, arriving
together in three years — is not a thing any society has a settled method for
absorbing. And it is not a phase. The Office for National Statistics now projects
that migration is the only source of population growth Britain will have for the
next quarter century, because deaths will exceed births. Whatever number is
chosen from here is a decision about what kind of country exists in 2060.

Which brings the second half, and the harder one. There is a difference between a
country changing and a country being changed. Every generation remakes the place
it inherited, and nobody has a right to a Britain held still. What people are
entitled to is to be the ones doing the remaking — to argue about it, vote on it,
lose the vote and know they lost it. The change of the last five years was not
lost at an election. It was produced by visa rules written in a department, at a
scale no manifesto contained and no minister defended in advance. To be told
afterwards that the accounts balance is to be told that a decision you thought was
yours belonged to somebody else all along. That is the grievance, and it does not
go away when the numbers fall, because the falling was decided the same way the
rising was.

Say plainly what this argument is not, because its enemies and too many of its
friends agree on the caricature. It is not a claim about the worth of anybody. It
does not need one, and it is weaker with one — an argument that starts from what
people are is answered by the crime figures and the fiscal figures, and answered
badly for it, which is exactly what happens every time somebody makes it. This
argument starts from what people do together and how long that takes. That is why
its holders are untroubled by the family three doors down whose grandparents came
from Lahore, and troubled by a rate of arrival that leaves nobody the time to
become the family three doors down. A shared way of life is joinable — that is the
entire premise. The complaint is about the speed of joining, not the identity of
the joiner.

The public makes precisely this distinction, and the polling shows it doing so. It
does not object to foreigners as such: asked route by route in 2026, 67% called
asylum numbers too high and 39% said the same of students, and majorities want it
made *easier* for care workers to come. That is not xenophobia; it is a public
sorting arrivals by whether the terms were consented to and reciprocal. It is a
more discriminating instrument than either the government or its critics have
credited.

**What this view has to give up.** The fear that migration brings crime is the
one part of the cultural case that can actually be measured, and when it is
measured it does not hold: non-citizens are about 13% of convictions and 12% of
the prison population against roughly 13.5% of adults, and lower still once you
account for how young they are. Anyone making this argument should stop making
that one. Nor is migration why British wages stalled — the Migration Advisory
Committee looked and it is not there. Skilled migrants are a large fiscal gain and
it would be squalid to pretend otherwise. And the pace being complained about was
substantially the country's own doing: Britain decided not to pay for social care,
staffed it from abroad instead, and the people who came did the work. A quarter of
all jobs held by recent non-EU migrants are in health and care. They are owed
better than being made the emblem of a decision they did not take.

**Four things this view refuses to lean on.** First and most awkwardly, the
evidence it would most want does not exist. There is no good British evidence that
rising local immigration erodes trust or cohesion: the literature measures ethnic
diversity rather than immigration, its association weakens or vanishes once
deprivation is controlled for, and the survey behind it stopped in 2011. This
argument therefore rests on what integration *is* and how long it takes, not on a
finding about trust, and it should be honest that Britain stopped collecting the
data that would have tested it. Second, it will not claim the public is simply on
its side: aggregate opposition swings twenty points on question wording, and a
case that needs a poll is not a case. Third, it will not blame small boats.
In the twelve months of 2025, detected illegal arrivals were 46,497 against
813,000 people moving here long-term — 5.7%, about one in eighteen, and the figure
is 5.7% rather than the 3% often quoted because the 3% divides one year's arrivals
into a different year's peak. Either way small boats are vivid, they are the reason
many people think they are angry, and they are not what changed any neighbourhood
in Britain.

Fourth — and this is the one its opponents will least expect — it will not argue
from the census figures on ethnicity or religion. They are real: 74.4% of England
and Wales identified as White British in 2021, down from 87.5% in 2001, and 6.5%
described themselves as Muslim, up from 4.9%. This view declines them because they
answer a different question. The census asks which group people feel they belong
to, not where they were born or when they came: the largest single increase across
the nineteen ethnic groups was white Europeans, nearly half the foreign-born
identified as White and one in eight identified as White British, and the largest
religious movements in Britain by far are the collapse of Christian affiliation and
the rise of "no religion", neither of which has anything to do with migration. An
argument about the rate at which people can become neighbours cannot be run on a
variable that measures who someone's grandparents were. The honest position is also
that nobody currently knows what share of Britain’s minority or Muslim populations
was born here: the last published breakdown, in 2011, put it at about half, and the
2021 equivalent has not been published. This view will not guess in either
direction.

So the ask is modest and unfamiliar: not a target for how few, but a limit on how
fast — a rate Parliament votes on and owns, set against something real like school
places and housing completions rather than picked as a slogan, with the case for
exceeding it made in public before it is exceeded. And an honest test attached. If
a decade of much lower net migration passes and the same people are just as
unsettled, this view will have been wrong about what was bothering them, and
should say so.
