Asylum is not immigration policy and the confusion between them has wrecked the
debate. Work and study visas are instruments: a country sets them where it likes,
adjusts them when circumstances change, and owes nobody an explanation. A claim
for protection is not an instrument. It is a promise Britain made — in 1951, in
its own courts, and repeatedly since — that a person who reaches its jurisdiction
with a well-founded fear of persecution will have that fear examined and will not
be sent back to face it. Promises of that kind are worth exactly as much as they
cost to keep. A protection guarantee that is honoured only when the numbers are
convenient is not a weaker version of the promise; it is the absence of one.

The distinction matters practically, not just morally. Roughly 40,000 people a
year arrive by small boat against long-term immigration that peaked near 1.5
million. Asylum is a rounding error in the numbers and the overwhelming majority
of the political argument. That mismatch is the tell: what people object to is
not the scale but the visible disorder — the boats, the hotels, the sense that
nobody is in charge. Those are real objections and they are answerable, because
the disorder was administrative.

Look at what happened when the state simply did its job. The backlog of people
awaiting an initial decision fell from 175,000 at its 2023 peak to 49,000, down
72%, and hotel use fell by more than a third in a single year — while arrivals
did not fall. Nothing was deterred and nobody was offshored. Claims were decided.
That is the whole of it. For years the country was told that the asylum system
was overwhelmed by the people arriving; it was overwhelmed by a decision-making
capacity that had been allowed to collapse, and the £15.3 billion accommodation
bill is what happens when you warehouse people instead of assessing them. The
hotels were never a consequence of generosity. They were a consequence of delay.

From that follows the rest of the programme, and it is not a soft one. Decide
claims fast, in weeks not years. Let people work while they wait, which costs the
taxpayer nothing and is the single best predictor of later integration. Remove,
promptly and with due process, those found not to need protection — a system that
never removes anyone teaches people that the outcome of a claim does not matter,
which is corrosive to the genuine refugee above all. And open safe routes, because
the reason people are in dinghies is that there is no queue to join: refugee
family reunion and resettlement are the only mechanisms that have ever moved
people out of the smugglers' hands, and Britain paused family reunion in 2025.

What this view has to concede, and does. The grant rate at initial decision has
fallen to 39%, well below its 2022 peak of 77%. That is uncomfortable, and the
comfortable answers are not good enough. It is true that appeals overturn a
substantial minority of refusals and that a grant rate is partly a fact about the
decision-maker — but it is not honest to pretend that a majority of claims are
being wrongly refused. Some people making claims are not refugees. A system built
on the promise above has to be able to say so and act on it, or it forfeits the
authority to say the opposite. Equally, £15.3 billion against an original £4.5
billion estimate is not a smear invented by opponents; it is the National Audit
Office, and the money came from people with no say in how it was spent.

Two further concessions, both of which this view would rather not make. The public
is not simply reacting to disorder in general: asked route by route, it objects to
asylum far more than to anything else — 67% say asylum numbers are too high,
against 39% for students — so the claim that people are only upset about
competence has to survive the fact that they name this route in particular.
And the removal half of the programme above is not being delivered. The number of
foreign national offenders liable for deportation and living in the community grew
36% between the end of 2022 and March 2026. A view that argues removal is what
keeps the promise credible cannot treat that as somebody else's problem.

Two arguments this view refuses, though allies make both. It will not assert that
deterrence has been disproved. The measured effects are small and asylum seekers
demonstrably know little about destination policy before travelling, but the
Rwanda scheme removed nobody and therefore tested nothing, and Australia's
arrivals really did collapse — under interception at sea rather than offshore
processing, which is a different policy but not an imaginary one. The correct
statement is that the proposed deterrence has not been tested, not that it has
failed. And it will not claim asylum seekers cost nothing because they cannot
claim benefits. They receive accommodation and subsistence while barred from
working, and recognised refugees are eligible for support immediately and have
low employment rates. The obligation is worth honouring at a real price, and the
price should be stated.
