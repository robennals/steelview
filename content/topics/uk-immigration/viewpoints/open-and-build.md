Britain needs the people who come here to work, and skilled workers among them put far more in than they take out. It should keep welcoming them, and build the homes and services a growing country needs.

### Skilled migrants pay far more in than they take out

Skilled workers outside health and care add hundreds of thousands of pounds each over their lives, and the government's migration advisers expect one year's skilled workers to add tens of billions of pounds to the public purse. So cutting numbers would cost money: in the budget watchdog's scenarios, lower migration raises borrowing within five years. Care workers, and adult dependants on the health and care route, cost more than they pay over a lifetime. That is a reason to choose carefully, not a case against the workers who pay their way.

### Britain's hospitals and universities depend on people from abroad

About a fifth of NHS staff in England are not British, and over a third of doctors. Students from abroad pay close to half of university course fees, and over a third of English universities are already in deficit. Britain is also ageing: with no net migration, there would be 641 people aged 65 or over for every 1,000 of working age by 2074, against 525 on the main projection. While migrants grow old too, which reverses much of their early gain, the alternative is fewer workers carrying more pensioners, sooner.

### Immigration adds a little to housing costs; cheap borrowing drove most of the rise

In countries like Britain, raising the immigrant share by one point raises house prices by under 1%, and rents by a little more. But Bank of England economists find that falling interest rates more than account for the rise in house prices against incomes since 1985, and the government's advisers put immigration's share of the long rise at about 4–6%. The extra demand is real: migration is just over half of expected household growth. More people need more homes. That is a reason to build them, not to shut the door.

### Building more homes needs migrant workers

Immigration is more likely to raise prices where planning rules hold back building. So fixing the planning rules would let Britain build. But migrants are almost half of London's construction workers, so cutting migration would take away many of the people who build there.

### Immigration has moved wages, and output per person, by only a few percent

The government's advisers estimate that 24 years of EU immigration cut the lowest wages by about 5%, and raised wages from the middle up. The budget watchdog puts migration's effect on output per person at under 1%, up or down, and output per person has barely grown since 2019, though Canada and Germany did worse. So immigration has barely changed how well off the average person is, while in one count of a hundred fast-growing firms, over half had a founder born abroad.

### So keep the door open, and spend the gains on homes and services

Freedom to work and trade together is how countries grow rich. Britain should keep its doors open to people who come to work and study. It should spend the gains on homes, transport and services, so that everyone shares in the growth.
