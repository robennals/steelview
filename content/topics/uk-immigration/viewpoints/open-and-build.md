Britain needs the people who come here to work, and the skilled among them put far more in than they take out. It should keep welcoming them, and build the homes and services a growing country needs.

### Skilled migrants pay far more in than they take out

On average, skilled workers outside health and care each add hundreds of thousands of pounds to the public purse over their lives. Cutting migration costs money too: in the budget watchdog's scenarios, lower migration raises borrowing within five years. While care workers, and the adult family members of health and care workers, cost more than they pay, that is a reason to choose carefully. It is not a reason to turn away the workers who pay their way.

### An ageing Britain needs workers, and its hospitals already rely on them

Britain will age whatever happens, but fastest with no migration. By 2074, with no net migration, there would be 641 people aged 65 or over for every 1,000 of working age, against 525 on the main projection. Already about a fifth of NHS staff in England are not British, and over a third of doctors. While migrants grow old too, so migration delays the strain of ageing rather than ending it, the alternative is fewer workers carrying more pensioners, sooner.

### Immigration has moved wages, and output per person, by only a few percent

Twenty-four years of EU immigration cut the lowest wages by about 5%, and raised wages from the middle up. In the budget watchdog's scenarios, migration changes output per person by under 1%, up or down. So cutting immigration would not make the average person much richer. Meanwhile, 54 of Britain's 100 fastest-growing firms had a founder born abroad.

### Immigration adds a little to housing costs, and building would meet it

In countries like Britain, a one-point rise in the immigrant share raises house prices by under 1%, and rents by a little more. Immigration explains only about 4–6% of the long rise in house prices. Falling interest rates likely more than account for the rise in prices against incomes. While migration is just over half of England's expected household growth, more people need more homes, and that is a reason to build them. Migrants are almost half of London's construction workers.

### The children of earlier migrants are doing well

Most minority groups outscore White British pupils at GCSE, and minority pupils go to university at higher rates. Segregation between neighbourhoods fell for every ethnic group from 1991 to 2021. About four in five adults say people from different backgrounds get on well where they live. While recent arrivals are the most likely to struggle with English, people who came long ago are far more likely to speak it as their main language.

### So keep the door open, and spend the gains on homes and services

Freedom to work and trade together is how countries grow rich. Britain should keep its doors open to people who come to work and study, and choose with care on the routes that cost more than they pay. It should spend the gains on homes, transport and services, so that everyone shares in the growth.
