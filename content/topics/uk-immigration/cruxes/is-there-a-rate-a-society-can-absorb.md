---
question: Is there a rate at which a society can absorb new arrivals, and did Britain exceed it?
kind: assumption
divides: [keep-the-place-recognisable, open-and-welcoming, select-for-contribution]
positions:
  - viewpoint: keep-the-place-recognisable
    holds: Yes. Integration is a social process with a rate, set by school places, housing, and how many people in a street have the time to get to know anybody. Britain went past it, and the cost appears as parallel lives rather than as conflict, which is why the usual indicators show nothing.
  - viewpoint: open-and-welcoming
    holds: There is no fixed rate. What people call absorption capacity is the capacity of public services, which is a funding decision, and Britain has absorbed comparable inflows before and converged within a generation each time.
  - viewpoint: select-for-contribution
    holds: The question is real but the variable is wrong. Whatever absorption capacity exists tracks language, earnings and whether people settle, not headcount — which is why the same number of arrivals produces completely different outcomes on different routes.
---
This is the cultural axis of the argument, and it is the one place on this page
where the disagreement is about something nobody is measuring. It is not a
disagreement about whether integration happens: all three positions accept that it
does. It is about whether it has a speed limit, and about what falling foul of one
would look like.

That last part is why the crux resists resolution. The first position predicts an
absence — of contact, of overlap, of the ordinary friction that makes strangers
into neighbours — and absences do not show up in crime statistics, employment
rates or fiscal accounts, which is where Britain looks. The evidence that would
bear on it directly does not exist: the trust and cohesion literature measures
ethnic diversity rather than immigration, weakens under deprivation controls, and
rests on a survey discontinued in 2011.

What would move people is measurement of the process rather than its supposed
symptoms — how mixed friendship groups and school cohorts actually are, how fast
English proficiency and residential mixing change with cohort and with local
arrival rates, and whether those move with the *rate* of arrival or only with its
level. Net migration has just fallen from 944,000 to 171,000, which is the sharpest
change in arrival rate in modern British history and the best natural experiment
anyone is going to get. Nobody is currently set up to read it.
