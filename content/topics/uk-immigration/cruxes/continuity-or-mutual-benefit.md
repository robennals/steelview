---
question: Should the pace of immigration be limited even if it makes people materially better off?
kind: tradeoff
divides: [too-much-too-fast-damages-the-social-fabric, immigration-makes-britain-better-off]
positions:
  - viewpoint: too-much-too-fast-damages-the-social-fabric
    holds: Yes. Shared life takes time to build, and residents should have a say in its pace of change. Higher incomes cannot compensate for losing that control and continuity.
  - viewpoint: immigration-makes-britain-better-off
    holds: Restriction sacrifices opportunities and resources that can improve life for residents and newcomers alike. Invest in housing and services so people can build a shared life together.

[Continuity of a shared way of life](#principle-continuity-of-a-shared-way-of-life)
and [freedom to cooperate and exchange](#principle-gains-from-open-exchange) can
pull in different directions. Consider arrivals that improve incomes and public
finances but change a neighbourhood faster than its residents want. The question
is how much material benefit should be forgone to preserve a chosen pace of change.

If continuity deserves that sacrifice, the economic argument must accept some
limits even when arrivals bring a net material gain. If opportunities for mutual
benefit deserve greater weight, the continuity argument must accept some unwanted
change and seek ways to help people adapt.

Whether integration actually has a speed limit is a separate
[empirical disagreement](#crux-is-there-a-rate-a-society-can-absorb). Resolving it
would clarify the costs. This crux asks how to weigh those costs if they exist.
